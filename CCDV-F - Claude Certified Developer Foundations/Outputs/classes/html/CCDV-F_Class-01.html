    You Can't Reach Through a Telephone

    One physical fact about where this thing runs. Everything else in the class is derived from it — where instructions live, how they get loaded, what each surface can touch, and why a plugin that works perfectly on your laptop dies on everybody else's.

    What you leave holding
    1. The model is never in the room. It can only ask; something else has hands.
    2. The board is finite and gets rewritten every turn, so every instruction is rent.
    3. Loading is a prediction, not a lookup — which is why the description does all the work.
    4. Four surfaces, one variable: whose hands.
    5. Directions are not the thing. A path gets resolved later, somewhere else.

    Ten screens. Use the arrow keys, or the buttons at the foot of each screen.

    01 · The wire

    Where is the thing you're talking to?

    By the end of this screen you'll be able to say exactly what happened when Claude Code edited a file on your disk — and it isn't what it looks like.

    Not in the room with you.

    Claude runs on machines in Anthropic's data centre. Not on your laptop, not on your server, not anywhere you can point at. When your program uses Claude, here is the whole of what physically happens: some text goes out over the internet, and some text comes back. Out, and back. There is no other part to it.

    Now you're going to object, and it's a good objection, because everybody makes it. You've sat and watched Claude Code work in your terminal. It opened your files. It changed them. It ran your tests and read the output. You watched it happen with your own eyes. So it must be on your machine.

    It isn't. And what really happened is more interesting than what you thought happened.

    There's a small program running on your laptop — the thing you started when you typed claude. Call it the harness, which is what everyone calls it. That little program is the one on the phone, and it's the one with hands.

    Claude sends back a piece of text that says, more or less, "I'd like to open the file at src/main.py." Now look carefully at what that is. It isn't an action. It's a sentence. It's a request, written down, and it arrives the same way every other piece of text arrives.

    The harness reads that request. It decides whether to allow it. If it allows it, the harness opens the file — your computer, your disk, your permissions — and then types the contents back down the phone.

    So Claude never touched your file. Your own computer touched your file, because Claude asked it to.

    You can't reach through a telephone. Not even a very good telephone.

    Carry this forward. Everything left in the class is one question: who's on the other end of the phone, and what have they got within arm's reach?

    02 · Memory

    The board that gets rewritten before every sentence

    By the end of this screen you'll know why a long conversation costs more than a short one — and why the limit isn't about storage.

    This one is stranger than the first.

    Claude doesn't remember your last message.

    I don't mean it forgets. Forgetting means you had something and lost it. It never had it. Every request that goes over the wire is complete in itself, and between requests nothing is kept. Nothing at all.

    So how do you have a conversation with it? Your program cheats. Every time you send a new message, it sends the entire conversation again, from the very first line. Turn forty is one enormous request containing all forty turns.

    Picture a blackboard. Before anybody says a single sentence, somebody wipes the board clean and rewrites everything that's been said so far, from the beginning. Then the next sentence gets said. Then they wipe it and write the whole thing out again, including the new bit. Every sentence. All the way through.

    That isn't an analogy for how it works. That's how it works.

    Two consequences worth more than any rule

    Anything written permanently in the corner of that board is paid for on every sentence. Not once, at setup. Every time. It's rent, not a purchase.

    The board is only so big — and the reason isn't that they ran out of chalk. When the model reads the board it doesn't go left to right the way you do; it weighs every word against every other word. Double the words and you've roughly quadrupled the work. There's a second cost too, and it's the one you feel: the more junk on the board, the harder it is to find the one line that mattered. A crowded board makes the thing dumber, not just slower.

    Next. You've now got a hard physical limit and a pile of instructions you'd like followed. Those two facts are about to collide, and the design of the whole instruction system falls out of the collision.

    03 · The library

    So where do you put your instructions?

    By the end of this screen you'll be able to derive the three homes for instructions rather than memorise them — and say what each one costs.

    Now you have a real problem, and I want you to feel the problem before I hand you the answer.

    You've got forty different sets of rules you'd like followed. Coding standards. A review checklist. How this team writes migrations. The house format for a report. Written out in full they'd fill the board and leave no room for the actual work.

    So you can't have them all up there. But you don't know in advance which one you'll need.

    What do you do in a library? A library has a hundred thousand books and nobody can carry a hundred thousand books. So the library keeps a card catalogue. One card per book — the title, one line about what's inside. The cards are tiny. The whole catalogue fits in one room. And the books sit on the shelves doing nothing at all until somebody actually wants one.

    That's the trick, and it's exactly the trick. They call it a Skill. It's a file, SKILL.md, with a name, one sentence saying what it's for, and then the whole body of instructions underneath. The name and the sentence go on the board; the body stays on disk costing you nothing, and gets fetched only when Claude decides it wants it.

    Now, some things really are relevant to every job in a repository — the language version, the conventions, the directories nobody's allowed to touch. Those go in CLAUDE.md, which loads into every session whether you need it or not. That's the stuff written permanently in the corner. Right trade when it genuinely applies to everything, bad trade the moment it doesn't — because a bloated CLAUDE.md doesn't just cost money, it's more junk competing with the actual question.

    And the third place is just typing it into the conversation. Worth understanding mechanically rather than as a category. Why does something you typed stay around for the whole conversation? Not because it's stored anywhere — because your program retypes it into every request. Why is it gone when the session ends? Because nothing was ever kept. There's no state to lose.

    Checkpoint

    Your team has a 40-line checklist that applies only when someone touches the payments module. Where does it go, and what would putting it in the wrong place cost you?

    A Skill — it applies to some tasks, not all of them. Put it in CLAUDE.md and you pay for all 40 lines on every turn of every session in the repository, including the hundreds that never go near payments. And you pay twice: once in tokens, and once in the dilution of every other instruction on the board.

    Next. The catalogue only works if the right card gets picked. So how does Claude choose? The answer is not what almost everyone assumes, and getting it wrong sends people hunting for a bug that doesn't exist.

    04 · There is no matcher

    The part almost everybody gets wrong

    By the end of this screen you'll know why a skill that never loads is not a broken skill — and where the fix actually lives.

    How does Claude pick which card?

    You're imagining machinery. Something that takes your request, looks at the forty descriptions, works out which one matches best, and picks a winner. An index. A search. A lookup.

    There isn't one. There's no such machinery anywhere in the system.

    The card catalogue is written on the blackboard, in plain words, sitting right next to your question. And Claude does the only thing it ever does: it looks at everything on the board and works out what the likeliest next move is. Given your question and forty little cards, the likeliest next move is asking for whichever card sounds like it was written for a question like yours.

    That's a judgment. Not a lookup.

    And now the rule about descriptions stops being advice and becomes something you can't avoid: the description does all the work, by itself. The instructions in the body can't help it. At the moment the decision gets made, nobody has read the body. It isn't on the board. As far as that decision is concerned, it doesn't exist.

    Written as…Reads likeResult
    "When reviewing a pull request that touches database migrations" A question somebody asks out loud Matches the moment somebody asks it
    "Database migration review guidelines" A label on a filing cabinet Nobody has a reason to walk to that shelf

    A librarian who files the migrations book under "Assorted Engineering Topics" hasn't lost it. It's right there on the shelf, perfectly intact. It's just that nobody who needs it will ever have a reason to walk to that shelf.

    This saves you an afternoon

    It's a guess, so sometimes it guesses wrong. When your skill doesn't fire and you go hunting for the broken lookup, you'll hunt for a very long time. Nothing's broken. A judgment went the other way, and the fix lives in that one sentence, not in the plumbing.

    05 · Whose hands

    Four phones, one question

    By the end of this screen you'll be able to work out what a surface can reach without having seen it before, instead of recalling a table.

    Back to the telephone. Who's on the other end, and what have they got within reach?

    Claude Code, in your terminal

    The harness is on your laptop. Your hands, your disk, your tools. So where would you keep a skill? On your disk, obviously — a folder called .claude/skills, because the harness is sitting right there and can read it.

    The Agent SDK

    Same engine, but now it's a library living inside a program you wrote, and your program might be a web service on a machine with no repository anywhere near it. So should it go rummaging around the filesystem and stuff whatever it finds into your prompts? Of course not — that would be a defect. So it doesn't, unless you tell it to, with a setting called settingSources. Not a gotcha to memorise. A library minding its own business.

    The Messages API, called from a service

    Stop and think about where a skill's body would have to be for anybody to read it. The decision about which skill to load happens over there, on Anthropic's side of the wire. So the body has to be over there too. And whose machine is over there? Theirs.

    The harder reason, which is the better one

    Even if Anthropic wanted to hand that container your disk, there's no wire to do it on. Your program dialled out to them; they never dialled in to you. No address, no route back through your firewall, no credentials. And there had better not be — because the moment there is, some container in their data centre can reach some customer's files, and the whole arrangement that lets a bank and a two-person startup share the same machines falls apart.

    A hosted agent that Anthropic runs for you

    Same container story, with one addition: they keep it. You call it by an ID and it's still there tomorrow.

    Take a skill that runs ./scripts/lint-diff.sh and move it to the Messages API. It will load perfectly. Then it will die — because you wrote instructions for your own toolbox and handed them to somebody in another country who's never seen it.

    06 · Delegation

    The helper who walks in

    By the end of this screen you'll know which half of a subagent's behaviour is a feature you're paying for, and which half is a security invariant — and why only one of them can hurt you.

    When you hand a job off to a subagent, it starts with an empty head. No conversation history. It doesn't inherit your skills either — it gets only what its own configuration lists.

    But it runs with your permissions.

    People file those two together under "surprising." They aren't the same kind of fact at all.

    The empty head is the product

    That isn't a limitation, it's the entire thing you're buying. Why did you delegate in the first place? Because the board is only so big and you didn't want to fill it. If the helper walked in carrying a copy of your whole conversation, you'd have paid for all that text a second time and got nothing for it. The point is that the helper starts clean — it can go and read sixty thousand tokens' worth of files in its own room, think it over, and hand you back three sentences. Your board only ever absorbs the three sentences.

    Inherited authority is close to forced

    Ask why permissions must carry over. Suppose they didn't — suppose a helper could hold powers you don't. Then "send a helper" becomes a way of getting powers you don't have, and anybody who could talk the system into calling for one would get a free upgrade. So it can't work that way. Someone you send on an errand can't do things you aren't allowed to do. It's a power of attorney: you can't hand over more authority than you've got. The helper isn't a new person. It's you, in another room, still wearing your badge.

    Go deeper — it varies by which subagent, and that catches people

    The built-in Explore and Plan subagents deliberately skip CLAUDE.md and git status, to stay fast and cheap — so your project rules genuinely aren't in their context. The general-purpose subagent loads both. If you delegate to Explore and a CLAUDE.md rule gets ignored, nothing malfunctioned; that context was never loaded.

    Custom subagents don't see your skills automatically and must list them in their own frontmatter. Built-in agents have no preloaded skills at all — if you need skill-backed behaviour, the route is a custom subagent that names them.

    07 · Late binding

    Alex Morgan's laptop

    By the end of this screen you'll know why an install succeeding tells you almost nothing, and why the author was structurally incapable of catching this by testing.

    A developer wrote a deployment routine as a skill, wrapped it in a plugin, tested it on their own machine, and shipped it to the team through their internal marketplace. Everybody's install worked. Nobody's run worked.

    Inside the skill was a line pointing at /Users/alexmorgan/projects/deploy-utils/validate.sh.

    Think about what that line actually is. It isn't a file. It's directions to a file. It's somebody saying "go down the hall, second door on the left." And directions only work if you're standing where the person who wrote them was standing.

    They call this late binding, if you want the term. All it means is that the words get written down now and looked up later, and "later" happens somewhere else.

    There was a second failure in the same package and it's the nastier kind. One of the skills used an environment variable, DEPLOY_TOKEN. Alex had set it in a shell profile years earlier and completely forgotten about it. It wasn't in the README. Nothing in the package mentioned it at all. Three people spent two hours chasing that one down.

    Alex could not have caught either of these by testing. That machine satisfied every assumption Alex had made, so every test passed and every test was meaningless — you cannot test for the absence of something that's always there.

    The habits that follow
    • Reference paths relative to the project, never absolutely.
    • $CLAUDE_PROJECT_DIR for scripts kept in the project; ${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT} for scripts bundled inside the plugin. Two variables, because there are two different meanings of "here" — the repository someone is working in, and the package your skill shipped inside.
    • Document and validate every environment variable at install time, so a missing one announces itself immediately rather than two hours into somebody's afternoon.
    • Install once on a clean machine before handing it to anybody — the only test where your own assumptions aren't quietly holding the thing up.
    Checkpoint

    A teammate reports that your plugin "installed fine but doesn't work." Before you look at any code, what are the two most likely causes, and which one will take longer to find?

    An absolute path that exists only on your machine, and an undeclared environment variable. The path takes longer to type but almost no time to find — it's sitting in the file as plain text and any reviewer reading it can spot it.

    The environment variable is the expensive one, because nothing in the package announces it. The skill runs perfectly right up to the step that needs it. That's why it cost three people two hours.

    08 · Where to spend your memory

    What's real, and what somebody just decided one Tuesday

    By the end of this screen you'll have a habit worth keeping for all twenty-nine classes: sorting what you can reason from against what you must simply recognise.

    Some of what I've told you is close to physics. It'll be true in five years, and you can work things out from it. Some of it is just how they've set it up this year.

    Treating both with the same reverence teaches you to spend memory in the wrong places.

    Close to physics — reason from itThis release's convention — recognise it
    The model runs on their machines and never on yours The file is called CLAUDE.md
    Nothing is remembered between calls Skills live in .claude/skills
    The board is finite and rewritten every turn The SDK's filesystem setting starts off
    A helper can't hold more authority than whoever sent it The variables are $CLAUDE_PROJECT_DIR and ${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}
    There's no wire from their container back to your laptop Beta header strings and their dates

    Learn the right-hand column, because the exam names mechanisms in its options and you'll have to recognise them. But don't mistake it for the left. From the left you can derive an answer to a question nobody taught you. The right is vocabulary, and somebody could change it next quarter.

    Recap

    What you're leaving with

    Everything in this class came out of one fact stated on the second screen. If you can rebuild the chain, you don't need the details.

    1. The model is never in the room. It can only ask. Something else has hands, and which something changes per surface.
    2. The board is finite and rewritten every turn, so every instruction you pin to it is rent, charged again on every exchange.
    3. Loading is a prediction, not a lookup. There is no matcher, so the description does all the work, alone, and it's probabilistic.
    4. Four surfaces, one variable. Whose hands — which decides what the instructions may assume is present.
    5. Directions are not the thing. A path resolves later, on a machine the author never saw, which is why install success proves nothing.

    And the one that ties the subagent to the security classes ahead: context doesn't travel, authority does. Missing knowledge fails closed. Inherited authority fails open.

    Before Class 2

    A team keeps its coding standards in CLAUDE.md. In the terminal it works fine — good, consistent results, day after day. Then they run the same workflow as a scheduled overnight job on the Agent SDK, pointed at the same repository. Now the output ignores several of those standards. Not every night. But often enough that somebody complained.

    What's the first thing you'd check — and what does the intermittency tell you? If the file simply never loaded, you'd expect the standards missing every night, not most.

    First check whether the job has filesystem sources configured to include CLAUDE.md. In the terminal it loads unconditionally; under the SDK it loads only if settingSources says so. If it isn't loaded, the standards were never on the board.

    But the intermittency says that can't be the whole story, because a file that never loads fails every night. Something probabilistic is also involved, and the only probabilistic mechanism in the picture is description-matching. Some of those standards are almost certainly in a skill — and a skill loads on a judgment, so it fires on some phrasings of the nightly task and not others.
