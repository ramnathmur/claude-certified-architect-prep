    There Is No Best Model

    Only the right model for a task at a stated quality bar. Which sounds like a platitude until you notice what it rules out — and that reaching for the most capable model by default is the most common and most expensive model-selection mistake in production.

    What you leave holding
    1. The same prompt runs on any tier, so model choice is a lever, not an architecture.
    2. A more capable model is sometimes cheaper and faster. Work out why.
    3. The cost of a wrong answer belongs in the arithmetic.
    4. Start at Sonnet; move in either direction only on eval evidence.
    5. You don't have to pick one model for everything.

    02 · The trade

    The trade — and the direction of it people miss

    By the end of this screen you'll know why "bigger model" doesn't reliably mean "slower and dearer."

    The straightforward version first. Upgrading a tier buys quality at the price of higher per-token cost and usually higher latency. Downgrading buys speed and lower cost at the risk of a quality drop. Fine, and mostly true.

    Now the part that catches people: a higher-tier model can process a request faster and cheaper if it reaches a conclusion in fewer tokens than a lower-tier model would.

    Work through why that has to be possible. You are billed per token, not per request. A cheaper model that flails — restating the problem, exploring a wrong branch, producing a longer and hedgier answer — can burn more tokens than a capable model that goes straight to the point. At a lower price per token, but more of them. And the tokens are also the latency, so it's slower on the wall clock too.

    So "cheap model" is a claim about price per token, and your bill is price × tokens. Those come apart, and when they do the intuition inverts.

    And one term nobody puts in the spreadsheet

    The cost of a mistake belongs in the calculation. Saving a few dollars a day on a lower tier is not a sound trade if the quality drop introduces errors carrying significant downstream cost — a wrong extraction that reaches a ledger, a misroute that costs an hour of somebody's afternoon, a bad summary a human then has to redo.

    The most expensive mistake in production

    Reaching for the most capable model by default. It feels safe and it is the single most common and most expensive model-selection error. The discipline is to make the trade-off measurable rather than settling it with an instinct about risk.

    03 · Procedure

    The default path, and when to stop having one model

    By the end of this screen you'll have a starting position and two moves, each with a condition attached.

    The default

    Start with Sonnet.

    Move up to Opus only when an eval shows Sonnet missing the quality bar on the hardest cases your traffic actually contains — and the cost of a wrong answer is high.

    Move down to Haiku only when an eval shows a cheaper model holding the bar on the bulk of traffic, freeing budget and latency.

    Notice both moves have the same instrument. In either direction, the eval is what promotes the change — a model change gets shipped on a measured score against your own cases, not on a benchmark, a release note, or somebody's afternoon of trying it.

    The eval you built for quality is also the gate for a model decision. It's the same instrument doing two jobs.

    You don't have to pick one model for everything

    A common production pattern is a default model with an override. Route the bulk of traffic to a balanced default, and send specific request types to a larger or smaller model based on a cheap signal read from the request — task type, input length, a difficulty classification.

    It's the same routing idea used for retrieval, applied to model choice: you pay for the more capable model only on the requests that need it.

    RouteWhen it earns its keepWhat it costs
    One pinned modelEvery request is the same shape at one quality barNothing. Skip the router
    Default plus overrideTraffic is genuinely mixed — a long tail of hard requests among easy onesA classification step, and a second model path to maintain

    The router is not free, and it is a real answer to a real shape of traffic. Where the traffic doesn't have that shape, it's complexity bought for nothing.

    Recap

    What you're leaving with

    1. The same prompt runs on any tier, so this is a setting you revisit, not an architecture you commit to.
    2. Price per token is not your bill. A capable model that answers in fewer tokens can be cheaper and faster than a cheap one that flails.
    3. The cost of a mistake is part of the arithmetic, and it is usually the term nobody writes down.
    4. Start at Sonnet; move on eval evidence, in either direction. Defaulting to the most capable model is the expensive mistake.
    5. Route only if the traffic is mixed. Uniform traffic, one pinned model, no router.
    Before Class 9

    A team runs document extraction on Haiku to keep costs down. It handles about 90% of documents well. The other 10% are dense multi-column contracts, where it produces wrong values roughly a third of the time. Those errors flow into a finance ledger and each one takes a person about twenty minutes to find and correct.

    Somebody proposes moving everything to Opus. Somebody else says the current setup is "90% accurate, which is fine."

    Both are wrong. What would you do, and what would you measure first?

    Why "move everything to Opus" is wrong: it pays the higher per-token price on the 90% that Haiku already handles. That's the default-to-the-most-capable mistake, bought wholesale.

    Why "90% is fine" is wrong: it prices the errors at zero. A third of 10% is about 3% of documents wrong, each costing twenty minutes of a person's time — and those are landing in a ledger, so the downstream cost is real and recurring. That term belongs in the comparison and was left out of it.

    What to do: this traffic is exactly the mixed shape that justifies a router. Keep the cheap model as the default and override to a more capable tier on a cheap signal read from the request — page count, column detection, document type. You pay the higher price on roughly a tenth of the volume.

    What to measure first: the eval, on the hard cases specifically. You need to know that the upgrade actually fixes those contracts before you build any routing — otherwise you've added a classification step and a second model path to maintain in exchange for an assumption. And second, the real cost of the errors, so the comparison has all its terms.
