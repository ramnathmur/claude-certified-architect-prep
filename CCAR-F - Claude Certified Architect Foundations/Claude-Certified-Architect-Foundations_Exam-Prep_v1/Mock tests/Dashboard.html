What D1–D5 mean

Every chart below reports by exam domain. The percentage is that domain’s share of the scored paper, so a weak D1 costs far more than a weak D5.

  • D1 · Agentic Architecture & Orchestration — 27% · loops, coordinators, subagents, hooks
  • D2 · Tool Design & MCP Integration — 18% · tool descriptions, error handling, MCP servers, built-ins
  • D3 · Claude Code Configuration & Workflows — 20% · CLAUDE.md, skills, rules, permissions, CI
  • D4 · Prompt Engineering & Structured Output — 20% · success criteria, schemas, batches, review
  • D5 · Context Management & Reliability — 15% · memory, escalation, provenance

What your score actually means

Read this before you draw a conclusion from a single number.

The scaled figure on each test is a linear conversion of your raw score: correct out of 60, mapped onto 100–1000. The real exam scales psychometrically across equated forms, so an item that everyone gets wrong counts differently there than it does here. Treat the number as a gauge of preparation, not a forecast of the result.

One test tells you very little. Each sitting draws 4 of 6 scenarios, so a single score partly reflects which scenarios you drew. Two tests taken under the same conditions tell you more than one test taken twice as carefully.

Only Hint-off scores count. A Hint-on run is a teaching session; its score measures nothing, because you saw the reasoning as you went. Keep those out of your readiness judgement.

Not readyBelow 720 on any Hint-off test, or a domain under 60%. Read that domain before sitting another test.
Getting there720–800 on one Hint-off test. Real, but one form. Sit a second under the clock before you trust it.
ReadyTwo consecutive Hint-off tests above 800, no domain below 70%, and both finished inside 120 minutes.

Watch the clock separately from the score. Finishing at 118 minutes with a good score is a worse position than it looks — the real paper gives you no margin to re-read a hard stem. The pacing table below is where that shows up.
