The exam itself

Before the content, the container. This chapter covers the shape of the paper: how it is scored, how it is framed, what it will never ask, and how a correct option reads differently from a confident-sounding wrong one.

The shape of the paper

The paper is 60 items in 120 minutes. Every item is multiple choice with one correct response and three incorrect ones, and the official guide says plainly that the incorrect options are designed to be plausible to a candidate with incomplete knowledge or experience. That sentence is the most useful line in the whole document. The distractors are not filler; they are the misconceptions a half-prepared architect actually holds.

Two minutes per item is the arithmetic, but the budget is not flat. Each scenario block opens with a production narrative that then frames roughly fifteen questions. Reading that narrative slowly is cheap, because the cost spreads across fifteen items. Re-reading it at item twelve is expensive, and it usually means you skimmed it at item one.

There is no penalty for a wrong answer. The corpus records that the platform requires an answer before you can advance, so no item is ever left blank. Whether or not the platform still forces an answer, your strategy is identical: never leave an item unanswered, and never spend four minutes on one you cannot crack.

No penalty, no blanks

A wrong answer costs exactly what a blank costs. Guess, note your uncertainty, move on. The only expensive mistake is spending a second question's worth of time on a first question.

Every published sample question is still four options with one correct answer, so default to single-select and read the instruction line on each item. Do not be thrown if the interface offers a flag-for-review control your corpus says does not exist.

What "scaled" actually means

Your score arrives on a 100 to 1,000 scale and the pass line is 720. The number is not a percentage and it is not a curve. The exam is criterion-referenced: you are measured against a fixed performance standard set by subject-matter experts in a formal standard-setting study, not against the other people who sat that day. Scaled scoring exists to equate forms, so that a slightly harder set of items and a slightly easier one both put the pass line in the same place.

The practical consequence is that any raw-to-scaled conversion you compute from a mock is an approximation, and the corpus says so explicitly. Your Mock #1 result of 45 out of 60 converted to roughly 775, clearing 720 by 55 points. Underneath that total, Tool Design and MCP came in at 5 of 11.

You missed this: the total hid the floor

On Mock #1 you read 45/60 and 775 as a readiness signal. It was not one. Sixty items absorb a six-item hole in an eleven-item domain without the total ever dipping under 720, and the CI scenario block sat at 67% inside the same clean-looking result. An aggregate score can carry a domain floor breach invisibly. The rule that generalises: score a mock per domain first, per scenario block second, and total last. The total is the least informative number on the page.

One change since your corpus was written works in your favour. Version 0.2 said results are reported pass or fail. Version 1.0 reports pass or fail, plus the scaled score, plus percent-correct within each content domain. Those per-domain percentages are informational and do not determine the outcome, which rests on the total scaled score alone. You will walk out with the same breakdown your mocks give you, which means a real attempt feeds your log with genuine domain data rather than estimates.

How the correct option reads

Across the official sample rationales and the practice-test analysis, the keyed answer keeps the same shape. It attacks the cause the stem named, at the smallest level of machinery that addresses it, using a mechanism whose reliability matches the stakes. Those are three separate tests, and the keyed option passes all three.

Fix the cause the stem named

Stems hand you the diagnosis if you read them for it. When a stem reports that two retrieval tools carry one-line descriptions and the model routes to the wrong one on about one call in six, it has already named the cause. Descriptions are the mechanism the model selects with, so the repair belongs in the description. An option that puts a classifier in front of the model is compensating for a bad signal rather than repairing it. The same logic runs in the other direction: when a stem states that every subagent completed successfully and the combined output still missed half the subject, it has cleared the subagents for you, and any option that tunes one of them is working on a component the stem already ruled out.

Proportion the response

The guide's own rationales reject options for being over-built more often than for being factually wrong. A classifier trained on historical tickets, a routing layer that parses input before every turn, a consensus vote across three review runs: each is a real technique, and each is disqualified while a prompt or description change remains untried. Watch the qualifier in the stem. "Most effective first step" is asking for the cheapest change that addresses the cause, not the most complete architecture.

Match the mechanism to the stakes

Prompt instructions are probabilistic. Hooks, programmatic prerequisites and tool whitelists are deterministic. When a stem involves money, identity, an irreversible action, or a sequence that must not be skipped, the keyed answer is structural. When the stem is about consistency of judgement or output format, the keyed answer is usually prompt-level: explicit criteria when the rule can be stated, few-shot examples when it can only be demonstrated.

Sounds right

The four distractor archetypes

Every wrong option in the official sample set falls into one of four families. Naming the family fast is worth minutes across a full sitting.

  1. Symptom-level fix. It treats the visible failure and leaves the cause running. Adding examples to work around a description that does not distinguish two similar tools is the canonical instance.
  2. Over-engineering. A classifier, a routing layer, a bigger model, a three-run consensus vote. Real engineering at the wrong altitude for the question asked.
  3. Wrong problem. It solves something adjacent and plausible. Sentiment detection when the stem is about case complexity. Tool availability when the stem is about tool ordering.
  4. Non-existent feature. A flag, environment variable or config file that does not exist: CLAUDE_HEADLESS, --batch, a .claude/config.json holding a commands array. If you have never seen it in the docs, that is because it is not there.

Read the stem twice: broken state or fixed state

This is the reading discipline that returns the most points for the least effort, and it is the one your log flags by name.

A scenario stem nearly always describes something going wrong. The question then asks one of two very different things: what this design produces (a diagnosis of the broken state) or what change repairs it (a description of the fixed state). Both question types offer options drawn from both states. Answer a diagnosis question with the remedy and you will pick a statement that is completely true, and score zero for it.

You missed this: answering the fixed state

In Session 17 a stem described a schema whose fields were all marked required and asked what that design causes. You picked the option describing nullable fields returning null for absent information. That statement is true, and it is the fix. The question asked for the failure: the model fabricates values to satisfy required fields rather than reporting absence. The rule that generalises: before you look at the options, say to yourself whether the stem is asking about the broken state or the fixed one. Diagnosis stems use root cause, most likely explanation, what does this produce. Remedy stems use what change, most effective first step, how should you restructure.

The other half of the discipline is the qualifier. "Most effective first step" excludes the complete architecture. "Most likely root cause" excludes anything the stem has already told you is working correctly. "How should you evaluate this proposal" invites a split verdict, where part of the proposal is sound and part is not: the sample set does exactly this with two workflows, one batch-appropriate and one blocking, and the keyed answer separates them instead of accepting or rejecting both.

The spine: one architecture, five domains

Five domains is how the exam is scored, not how the system works. This chapter rebuilds the single architecture running underneath all of them, then names the four principles that decide most cross-domain questions.

The path a single request takes

Follow one request end to end. A scheduled job hands your system a month of supplier invoices and asks for every line that has no matching delivery record. Nothing in that sentence names a domain, yet the request crosses all five before an answer comes back, and each domain owns one stretch of the path. The exam dresses this path in six scenario costumes: support agent, research pipeline, code generation, developer productivity, CI review, document extraction. The costume changes. The path does not.

Station 1: the loop that decides whether to keep going

Your orchestrator sends a message with tools defined, Claude answers, and the orchestrator reads stop_reason to decide what happens next. tool_use means execute the requested tool, append a tool_result block to messages, call again. end_turn means stop and return. That one field is the authoritative control signal for the whole loop. Text content is derived from it, an iteration cap is a safety net behind it, and parsing the assistant's prose for something like "I'm done" is a named anti-pattern.

The field carries that weight because of the second fact at this station: the Messages API is stateless. Everything the model knows on iteration nine is in the messages array you resent on iteration nine. Two consequences get tested further down the path. Cost and latency grow with turn count, because you resend the history. And every form of memory is something your application constructs.

The loop's only stop signal

Continue while stop_reason is tool_use. Terminate when it is end_turn. Everything else at this station follows from the API being stateless.

Four values or seven

The corpus names four stop_reason values: end_turn, tool_use, max_tokens, stop_sequence. Current API docs list seven, adding pause_turn, refusal and model_context_window_exceeded. Answer loop questions on tool_use versus end_turn, which has not changed, and never pick an option whose whole claim is that exactly four values exist.

Station 2: the coordinator does two jobs before anything else

Job one is partitioning: deciding what the pieces of work are. If the coordinator splits the reconciliation into "invoices above ten thousand" and "invoices from the five largest suppliers", every subagent can execute flawlessly and the report still misses most of the file. When subagents all succeed and coverage is still wrong, the defect is in the coordinator's task list, not in subagent execution. That inversion is one of the most reliably tested root-cause questions on the exam.

Job two is the handoff. Subagents run with isolated context and do not inherit the coordinator's conversation history, so whatever the delivery-lookup subagent found reaches the synthesis subagent only because the coordinator wrote it into the synthesis prompt. Spawning runs through the Task tool, which must be present in the coordinator's allowedTools or the coordinator cannot delegate at all, whatever its system prompt says. Multiple Task calls emitted in one coordinator response run in parallel; the same calls spread across turns run sequentially. And the prompt itself should state goals and quality criteria rather than a numbered procedure, because a procedural coordinator breaks the moment a step returns something unexpected.

You missed this

Mock #1 Q39 gave you three subagents all reporting success and a combined map missing two of four subtopics. You chose a tooling or interference explanation. The keyed answer was that the coordinator's own decomposition never assigned those subtopics. Generalize it: subagent success reports tell you about execution, never about coverage. Coverage is a property of the task list, and the task list is written upstream of every subagent. Ask who decided the pieces before you ask whether the pieces ran.

Station 3: what each subagent may touch

Two levers control which tool gets called, and neither is a prompt. The first is which tools exist for that subagent at all, set by allowed_tools in its AgentDefinition. The second is how well each tool's description separates it from its neighbors.

Tool count is itself a reliability variable. The official numbers are 18 versus 4 to 5: an agent holding eighteen tools misroutes far more than a peer holding the five relevant to its role, because every extra tool is another candidate to discriminate against on every turn. The fix is domain-scoped subagents with small tool sets, not longer descriptions on all eighteen.

Descriptions are the primary mechanism the model uses to select a tool, and every description of every available tool sits in context on every turn. They are not fetched lazily when a tool starts to look relevant. Always present means always influencing. A description earns its place by stating purpose, accepted input, when to use it instead of the similar tool beside it, and when not to use it. That last clause is the one candidates skip, and it is exactly what disambiguates two tools whose names sound alike.

Station 4: what comes back, in both shapes

A tool call returns a failure or a result, and both have to arrive structured, for different reasons. Failures use the MCP isError: true flag with the category in the payload. The exam's four categories are transient (timeout, service unavailable), validation (malformed input), business (a policy limit was hit) and permission (auth or access denied). Each demands a different response: retry, fix the input and re-call, explain the policy outcome and stop, escalate. A generic "operation failed" collapses four decisions into one guess. An empty result is not a failure at all: a query that ran and matched nothing succeeded, while a query that could not run did not. Conflating those corrupts the agent in both directions.

Results use a tool whose input_schema is your desired output shape; you read the data out of the tool_use block. The tool need not do anything, it exists to force schema-conformant JSON. That mechanism eliminates the entire class of syntax failures and does nothing whatsoever about semantic failures. A response can validate perfectly and still put a value in the wrong field or claim a total the line items do not sum to.

Station 5: the join, where everything accumulates

Findings from several subagents land in one aggregation prompt, and the join is where Domain 5 lives. Long inputs are read reliably at the beginning and the end and unreliably in the middle, so key findings go at the top with detail below under explicit section headings. Verbose tool results get trimmed before they enter context, typically with a PostToolUse hook, because a forty-field order record then sits in the window for every later turn. Precise transactional facts move into a persistent case-facts block outside the summarized history, so compression cannot blur them. Claims travel as claim-and-source pairs with publication dates, because attribution not carried as structured data is lost at the first summarization, and two sources from different years read as contradicting each other when the dates are gone.

Two more behaviors at the join. When some upstream inputs failed, synthesis completes on what is available and annotates coverage, marking which conclusions are well supported and where a real gap sits: neither erroring out the whole report nor quietly omitting the gap is acceptable. And for work spanning crashes or context limits, each agent exports structured state to a known location and the coordinator loads a manifest on resume, because conversation history cannot survive a crash on a stateless API.

Station 6: the surfaces where all of this is written down

Domain 3 is the same architecture expressed as files a team can share. CLAUDE.md holds universal standards and loads every session; discovered files across the hierarchy are concatenated into context in load order rather than overriding one another, so a subdirectory file never silently replaces the project file. .claude/rules/ files carry a paths: glob in YAML frontmatter and load conditionally when Claude works on matching files, which is how a convention follows a file type across directories. Skills in .claude/skills/ carry frontmatter: context: fork runs the skill in an isolated subagent context so its output never pollutes the main window, allowed-tools scopes what it may do, argument-hint prompts for required parameters. Plan mode is for architectural scope and multiple viable approaches, direct execution for well-specified work. In CI, -p makes the run non-interactive, and --output-format json with --json-schema makes the findings machine-parseable.

Two different things are called allowed-tools

AgentDefinition.allowed_tools in the Agent SDK is the whitelist deciding which tools a subagent has at all: the least-privilege lever from Station 3. allowed-tools in SKILL.md frontmatter is the Domain 3 key for scoping a skill during execution. Answer skill questions with the official framing (it restricts tool access during skill execution); current docs describe it as a permission pre-grant, worth knowing for real work and never the scored distinction.

Two currency notes so neither surprises you. The corpus puts the @path import nesting maximum at 5 and current docs say 4, so answer the mechanism (relative paths resolve against the importing file) and let the digit go. And the subagent-spawning tool has been renamed from Task to Agent, with Task(...) still working as an alias; a stem using either name is answerable.

Four principles the whole exam is testing

Almost every scored item is one of four judgements wearing a scenario. Hear which one a stem is asking and the option set collapses from four plausible answers to one correct answer and three named misconceptions.

Principle 1: determinism beats persuasion

Anything the model is asked to do can be skipped. Anything the code will not permit cannot be. The deterministic side of that line: a PreToolUse hook blocking a call above a threshold and routing it to escalation; a programmatic precondition that will not let one tool run until another returns a verified value; allowed_tools as a whitelist; tool_choice set to {"type": "any"} to guarantee a tool call rather than prose, or to {"type": "tool", "name": "..."} to guarantee a specific first step; a schema that makes malformed output impossible; a preview tool returning a single-use token the execute tool requires.

The probabilistic side loses every time the stem involves safety, ordering or compliance: a stronger system-prompt instruction, few-shot examples demonstrating the correct sequence, a rule in CLAUDE.md, or a boolean like dry_run that the model sets itself and can therefore set to false on the first call.

The tell is a compliance rate in the stem. When it says an instruction is followed about ninety percent of the time, or that a step gets skipped in twelve percent of cases, the stem has told you prompting was tried and is working exactly as well as prompting works. The answer is a mechanism, not a better sentence.

The loop and the coordinator

Domain 1 carries more scored content than any other domain, and almost all of it reduces to two mechanisms: a loop steered by one field in the API response, and a coordinator that owns everything its subagents cannot see. This chapter restores the exact mechanism behind both, in the exam's own vocabulary.

One request, all of it, every time

An agent run is a sequence of ordinary API calls. Nothing about the run lives on Anthropic's servers between calls. Each request to the Messages API carries model, max_tokens, the system prompt, the full messages array, the tools definitions, and tool_choice. All of it is re-sent on every iteration of the loop.

{
  "model": "claude-sonnet-4-6",
  "max_tokens": 1024,
  "system": "You are a reconciliation agent...",
  "messages": [
    {"role": "user", "content": "Reconcile yesterday's ledger."},
    {"role": "assistant", "content": "[text block + tool_use block]"},
    {"role": "user", "content": "[tool_result block]"}
  ],
  "tools": [ ... ],
  "tool_choice": {"type": "auto"}
}

Two details in that shape are exam-scoreable. First, the system prompt is not a message role: it travels in the top-level system parameter, outside the array. Second, tool output is not a role you set. The study guide labels it a tool role, but on the wire your code appends the output as a tool_result content block inside the next user turn, so the model reads the result in the same conversational stream that produced the call.

The Messages API is stateless. It keeps nothing between calls. The only reason Claude appears to remember turn three at turn nine is that your application re-sent turns one through eight. That is also why latency and cost climb as a conversation lengthens: each request is physically bigger than the last. If you stop appending history, the model does not become forgetful in a graceful way, it simply has no knowledge that the earlier turns happened.

The trap

Distractors here offer plausible-sounding infrastructure. A session_id parameter that makes Claude remember does not exist. A vector database is not how a standard multi-turn conversation is remembered either: embeddings and retrieval are for pulling specific exchanges out of months of accumulated history, not for ordinary conversational memory. The tell is that both options invent a server-side memory that the stateless design does not have. Conversation memory is the messages array.

The agentic loop, and what makes it agentic

  1. Send a request to Claude with the tool definitions attached.
  2. Receive the response and read stop_reason.
  3. If it is tool_use: extract every tool_use content block, execute each tool, append the results as tool_result blocks to messages, and go back to step 1.
  4. If it is end_turn: the task is done. Return the final text.

Step 3 is the part people skip when they describe this from memory. Results have to go back into the conversation history, because that is the only way the model can reason about what it just learned. An agent that executes a tool and does not append the result is asking the same question again with the same information.

The reason this pattern is called agentic is the vocabulary the exam uses for it: model-driven decision-making. Claude reasons about which tool to call next from the context and the results so far. The alternative it is contrasted against is a pre-configured decision tree, where the sequence of tool calls is fixed in your code before the run starts. Both are legitimate engineering, but only one is an agentic loop, and questions that describe a system adapting its next step to what the last step returned are describing the model-driven kind.

Pre-configured decision tree

The order of tool calls is decided in your code. Step two always follows step one. When a step returns something unexpected, the script has no branch for it and either fails or carries on with a wrong assumption. Reproducible, and blind. The corpus calls this shape a fixed sequential pipeline and blesses it when the steps genuinely never vary, so read it as the wrong answer for adaptive work, not as bad engineering everywhere.

Model-driven agentic loop

The order of tool calls emerges from the results. Claude sees the tool_result from step one and chooses step two accordingly. The loop's control flow lives in stop_reason, not in your branching logic.

The three completion anti-patterns, named precisely

The exam guide lists three anti-patterns for loop termination by name, and questions in this area are usually built by offering one of them as a confident-sounding option next to the real answer.

  • Parsing natural-language signals. Watching the assistant text for "task complete" or "I'm done". Phrasing is generative output, not a protocol field, so it varies run to run and can appear mid-task.
  • An arbitrary iteration cap as the primary stopping mechanism. Note the word primary. A cap such as max_iterations=25 is perfectly reasonable as a runaway backstop. It is wrong as the thing that decides the task is finished, because the number is unrelated to whether the work is actually done.

The third one deserves the mechanism spelled out, because it is the one that reads as reasonable. The same model-driven principle that governs the loop governs the orchestration layer. A coordinator prompt should specify research goals and quality criteria. It should not specify a numbered procedure.

Prompt styleShapeEffect

Hub-and-spoke: what the coordinator owns

The multi-agent topology the exam tests is hub-and-spoke. One coordinator, several specialised subagents, and no direct subagent-to-subagent communication. Every message between two subagents goes through the hub.

  • Task decomposition: splitting the work into subtasks, and partitioning the space so two agents do not cover the same ground.
  • Dynamic selection: deciding which subagents this particular query actually needs.
  • Delegation: writing each subagent's prompt, including the context it will need.
  • Aggregation and validation: assembling results and judging whether coverage is sufficient.
  • Error handling: deciding retry versus skip versus escalate when a subagent fails.
  • Reporting: returning the result to the user.

The four reasons this topology wins are worth naming, because the exam asks for the advantage directly: visibility (the coordinator observes every interaction), uniform error handling (one place decides recovery), information control (the coordinator decides what each subagent is allowed to see), and separation of responsibilities (each subagent has a narrow, defined scope).

The trap

Asked why the coordinator should stay the central hub, the wrong options attach real-sounding benefits that are not the reason. Batching subagent requests to cut API calls is an efficiency claim, not the topology's purpose. "Only the coordinator can implement retry" is false, since a subagent can retry locally for transient failures. "Direct communication would need complex serialisation" invents a technical obstacle. The correct option names observability, uniform error handling and controlled information flow together.

Passing them as merged free text is the next failure. When you flatten a finding and its source into one paragraph, the claim-to-source mapping is lost the moment anything downstream summarises. The exam's answer is structured context passing: a format that separates content from metadata so attribution survives aggregation.

{
  "content": "Regional churn rose 4.1 percent in Q2",
  "metadata": {
    "source_url": "https://example.com/report",
    "document_name": "Quarterly Retention Review",
    "page_number": 12,
    "retrieved_date": "2026-06-15"
  }
}

Two consequences follow. Instructing a synthesis agent to "remember to cite your sources" cannot work, because it cannot cite metadata it never received. And having the report agent re-search the web to find a source for each claim duplicates work and risks attaching the wrong source to the right claim. When two sources disagree, the rule is to carry both values with attribution and annotate the conflict, letting the coordinator decide, rather than silently picking one.

Spawning, parallelism, and where the time goes

Subagents are spawned with the Task tool, which is why "Task" must appear in the coordinator's allowedTools. The parallelism rule is mechanical and precise: multiple Task calls emitted in a single coordinator response run in parallel. The same calls issued across separate turns run sequentially, because each turn waits for the previous result before the next request is even sent.

That distinction is what the timing arithmetic rests on. With four independent investigations running in parallel, elapsed time is the maximum of the four, not the sum. Sequential delegation of the same four gives you the sum. Nothing about the subagents changes; only where the Task calls were emitted changes.

Parallel spawning is the right answer for genuinely independent work: distinct research subtopics, the separate concerns inside a multi-issue customer request, different dimensions of one analysis. It is the wrong answer when step two needs step one's output, since a parallel spawn simply denies step two the information it required.

Docs have moved, the exam has not

In current Claude Code the subagent-spawning tool was renamed from Task to Agent, with Task(...) still working as an alias. The exam guide and the corpus both say Task. Answer with Task, recognise Agent if you see it, and never let the name be the deciding difference between two options.

When the coordinator should not delegate

Delegation is not free. Every Task call spins up a fresh context that has to be re-fed with everything it needs, and every result has to be aggregated back. The exam guide asks for coordinators that analyse query requirements and dynamically select which subagents to invoke, rather than always routing through the full pipeline. A simple request one agent can answer directly should not be pushed through search, analysis, synthesis and reporting in turn.

There is a second category: work that belongs to the coordinator by definition. Evaluating synthesis output for coverage gaps, deciding retry versus skip versus escalate, partitioning the research space, and judging when coverage is sufficient are all hub responsibilities. The classic wrong answer is letting the synthesis agent search the web to fill its own gaps, which breaks separation of responsibilities and least-privilege tooling in one move. The correct design has the coordinator re-delegate targeted queries instead.

Third: when the round trip costs more than the capability. A synthesis agent that needs a simple fact check in 85 percent of cases can be given one narrowly scoped verification tool, removing most round trips while complex cases still route through the coordinator. That is still least privilege, because the tool is scoped to the need. Full web search for the same agent is not.

Control, escalation and decomposition

The first half of Domain 1 was about what the architecture is. This half is about who guarantees it. Hooks, escalation, handoff, sessions and decomposition are five judgement calls, and each one has a single precise answer the exam is looking for.

Hooks: code that runs whether or not the model cooperates

Everything the model decides inside the agentic loop is a prediction. Which tool to call is a prediction. Whether to verify identity before acting is a prediction. Whether to obey the policy line you wrote into the system prompt is also a prediction. A hook is different in kind. It is ordinary code registered at a fixed point in the loop, and it runs on every matching tool call no matter what the model intended. The exam has exact words for that difference: hooks give you a deterministic guarantee, prompt instructions give you probabilistic compliance.

The corpus names two interception points, and the official task statement covers both: PostToolUse by name, and outgoing-call interception as the other.

HookFiresWhat you do there
PreToolUseBefore the tool executesValidate arguments, block the call, modify parameters
PostToolUseAfter the tool returns, before the model sees the resultTransform output, normalize formats, log

PreToolUse is the enforcement point

Say an operations agent can deactivate user seats on a tenant, and policy caps unattended deactivation at a fixed number of seats. A PreToolUse hook inspects the arguments before the call ever reaches the tool. Above the ceiling it blocks the call and redirects to human escalation. The official wording for this is interception that blocks policy-violating actions and redirects to an alternative workflow. The load-bearing property is that it fires regardless of LLM behaviour. The model does not have to remember the rule, agree with the rule, or even know the rule exists.

PostToolUse is the shaping point

Now the other direction. Three tools you do not own return the same logical field in three shapes: a Unix timestamp, an ISO 8601 date string, and a numeric status code. You cannot modify the sources. The documented answer is a single PostToolUse hook that intercepts every result and normalizes centrally, before the model reasons over it. The corpus marks three tempting alternatives wrong: patching the tools you control and writing wrappers for the third-party ones (maintenance fragments across two places), adding a normalize_data tool the agent is supposed to call after every retrieval (an extra model call that depends on the model choosing to make it), and documenting the formats in the system prompt (back to interpretation).

The same hook does a second job worth carrying across domains: trimming a forty-field tool result down to the handful of fields that matter, before it accumulates in context. That is a context-management goal implemented with a Domain 1 mechanism. Same hook, different purpose.

The rule that decides these questions

When failure has financial, legal or safety consequences, enforce with a hook or a precondition, never with a prompt instruction. Prompts are for guidance and preference. Hooks are for guarantees.

Sounds right

Add an explicit policy section to the system prompt, plus four few-shot examples showing the agent refusing the oversized request. Reinforce it in CLAUDE.md so every session picks it up.

Actually right

Register a PreToolUse hook that blocks the call above the threshold and routes it to escalation. The prompt work is fine as guidance, but it is not the answer to a question about guaranteed compliance.

The diligent-sounding prompt fix

Any option that opens with "strengthen the system prompt", "add few-shot examples showing the correct sequence", or "document the rule in the project configuration" is wrong the moment the stem involves money, policy or safety. These read as conscientious engineering, which is exactly why they are the most-picked distractor. The tell is in the stem: it usually hands you a failure rate. A rule that fails in 12% of cases is a rule that is not enforced.

One currency note, not exam material. The live product documents a far larger set of lifecycle events than these two, and a hook signals a hard block through its exit code rather than by returning a value. Do not carry that into the exam. Answer with PreToolUse and PostToolUse, which is the pair the corpus names.

Compliance belongs in the code path

There are three places a rule can live in an agent system, and they are not equivalent. In the prompt, a rule biases what the model chooses. In the tool or hook layer, a rule enforces, because the violating call cannot complete. In the log, a rule detects, after the fact, so you can find out you were breached. All three are worth having. Only the middle one is a guarantee, and the exam almost always scores the middle one.

The corpus gives you two named shapes of enforcement, and they answer different question types. The programmatic precondition blocks a downstream tool until an upstream one has returned a verified result: a deployment assistant cannot call the migration-apply tool until the schema-diff tool has returned a reviewed plan. The two-tool token-binding pattern splits one dangerous tool into two, where the preview tool returns a single-use token and the execute tool refuses to run without it. Both make the unsafe path structurally unreachable rather than merely discouraged.

Why a boolean is never enough

A single tool with a dry_run flag the model sets itself is not a guarantee, because the model sets it. If skipping a step must never happen, the interface has to make skipping impossible. A prompt can only make it unlikely.

Broken state or fixed state

When a stem describes a design that is going wrong, at least one option will describe what the corrected design does well. It reads like a true statement, because it is one. It is just not an answer to the question that was asked. Before you commit, say the question type out loud: is this asking what is failing, or what to change? You have lost points to that exact inversion, so make it a deliberate step rather than a hope.

Escalation: on category and impact, never on a counter

Escalation sits under Domain 1 in the corpus and under official task 5.2 in the guide, so it can reach you in either framing. Either way it is decided by one idea: escalate on structural signals. A structural signal is a fact about the situation, not an inference about the person or about the model's own state.

SituationCorrect response
The user explicitly asks for a human or a managerEscalate immediately. Do not attempt a resolution first
Policy is silent or ambiguous about the requestEscalate. The agent must not invent policy
The agent cannot make meaningful progressEscalate after a reasonable number of attempts, with what was attempted
A financial operation is above a thresholdEscalate, preferably enforced by a hook rather than a prompt
High-stakes ambiguity where a wrong call causes real harmEscalate on the category and the impact

Immediate versus attempt-then-escalate

An explicit request for a human is an immediate escalation. The wrong answer is the helpful-sounding one: "I can certainly help with that, let me first check your account." Trying to resolve after the person has asked for a human is a documented failure, not diligence. Now the mirror case, which is where the exam separates candidates. A first expression of frustration is not a request for a human. The correct sequence there is acknowledge the frustration, offer a concrete resolution, and escalate only if the person reiterates that they want a person. Anger is an emotion. Asking for a manager is a request. Only one of them is a trigger.

The policy-gap case

A subscriber asks for a service credit after an outage. Your documented policy covers refunds for billing errors and says nothing at all about outage credits. Escalate. Two wrong moves live in every version of this question: applying the nearest documented rule by analogy (that is inventing policy), and refusing on a rule that does not exist ("we do not issue outage credits" is not in the policy either). Silence in the policy is exactly the condition that requires a human interpretation.

The multiple-match case

Direction reverses here, so watch it. When a lookup returns several matching records, the agent asks for an additional identifier: an email, an order number, a phone number. Selecting the most recently active match is a heuristic that misidentifies people and refunds the wrong account. Selecting the first result is the same error with less thought behind it. And escalating to a human is the over-reaction, because the ambiguity is resolvable by asking one question. Ambiguity that a clarifying question can close is not an escalation trigger.

The handoff payload, field by field

Escalating mid-process is not simply calling the escalation tool. The agent must compile a structured handoff summary, and the reason is one sentence long: the human receiving the case does not have access to the conversation transcript. Whatever you put in that payload is the entire universe of what they know. Anything you leave out, they will ask the person again.

{
  "account_id": "ACC-77310",
  "issue_summary": "Repeat device failure after two remote resets",
  "asset_id": "PRN-4482",
  "root_cause": "Fuser unit reports over-temperature on every job above 20 pages",
  "actions_taken": [
    "Verified the account via get_account",
    "Confirmed asset and warranty state via lookup_asset",
    "Ran two remote resets; the fault returned within an hour each time"
  ],
  "replacement_cost": "$310.00",
  "recommended_action": "Dispatch an on-site engineer with a replacement fuser",
  "escalation_reason": "Remote remediation exhausted; on-site dispatch is outside the agent's tool scope"
}

Read the fields for what each one buys the human. The record identifier lets them pull the case in their own systems without asking for it. The root cause saves them re-diagnosing what the agent already established. The amount or impact tells them what authority level this needs. Actions already taken is the field that stops the duplicate work, because without it the human repeats the two resets. The recommended action turns the handoff from a question into a decision they can approve. The escalation reason tells them why it reached them at all, which is how you audit whether your criteria are calibrated.

The exam names four fields most often: customer or record ID, root cause analysis, the refund or amount, and the recommended action. Actions taken and escalation reason ride alongside.

Three wrong fixes for the same symptom

The symptom is always the same: human agents keep re-asking for information the agent already collected. Giving the humans the raw transcript is wrong twice over, because they lack transcript access by design and because a transcript forces them to re-read an entire conversation to extract five facts. Having the human re-run the agent's tools duplicates completed work and slows resolution. Escalating earlier, so less context accumulates, is the sneakiest of the three: it sounds like context hygiene, it does not fix the information loss, and it lowers your autonomous resolution rate as a side effect.

Degrade gracefully, report partially

When upstream inputs are incomplete, the synthesis stage still runs. It completes using the data that arrived, then adds a coverage annotation marking which conclusions are well-supported and where a genuine gap exists, and it propagates that uncertainty upward. This is graceful degradation with transparency: value is preserved from the work that succeeded, and the reader is told exactly how much to trust it.

Two wrong moves bracket it. Returning an error because the input was incomplete throws away everything that worked. Proceeding without noting the gaps produces a report that looks whole and is not, which is worse than an honest partial, because nobody downstream knows to compensate. A third option shows up in fresh scenarios: pausing the whole pipeline to retry the failed sources with a longer timeout before synthesis starts. That blocks completed work on a recovery that may never succeed.

Underneath the annotation sits the subagent's own reporting duty. A failing subagent returns structured error context to the coordinator, never a generic failure string and never silence. Four elements: the failure type, what was attempted (the actual query or parameters), any partial results completed before the failure, and possible alternatives. The coordinator needs all four to choose between retry, reroute, continue-with-gaps and escalate.

Error typeRecoveryNote
Transient, such as a timeout or a network dropRetry, with the same or modified parametersResolve inside the subagent where it can; escalate only what it cannot
Permanent, such as a malformed query or denied accessDo not retry. Log it and continueRetrying changes nothing; the call will fail identically every time
A valid empty result, such as zero matchesAccept it as a findingNot a failure at all. This is the pair the exam most likely puts against a timeout
Partial completion beats fake success

Never catch an error and return success. Never terminate the whole workflow because one subagent failed. Report what completed, label what did not, and let the coordinator decide.

Resume, fork, or start fresh

Three session operations, three different jobs. Confusing them is easy because all three sound like continuity.

SituationChoiceWhy
Prior context is mostly still valid--resume <session-name>Continues that specific conversation with its saved context, including its tool results
You want to compare two approaches from one expensive shared analysisfork_sessionBoth branches inherit context up to the branch point, then diverge without contaminating each other
Prior tool results are stale: files changed a lot, a long time passed, context degradedNew session with an injected structured summaryCarries the conclusions forward without carrying the stale evidence

The staleness rule is the whole game. A resumed session reasons over yesterday's tool results as though they describe today's files. It does not know they are old. It has no mechanism to notice. That is why a fresh session plus a short structured summary beats resuming with stale data: the summary carries what you concluded, and deliberately drops the evidence that has since expired.

There is a middle move too, and it is the correct answer more often than people expect. If you do resume after code changes, tell the session exactly which files changed so it re-analyses only those. That is targeted re-analysis. It beats resuming blind (stale reasoning) and it beats re-exploring the whole codebase (wasted work when most of the analysis still holds).

This is not a mock miss. It is worse in a way: your evidence base is a single clean recall from one session, and single-facet evidence is exactly what collapsed on you elsewhere. Say those three sentences back to yourself once and the sub-domain is closed.

When fork is wrong

Fork answers one question only: two approaches, one shared expensive baseline, no cross-contamination. Running both approaches sequentially in a single session is wrong because the second evaluation is biased by the first one's reasoning. Two brand-new sessions is wrong because each has to redo the shared analysis. If the stem is not asking you to compare alternatives, fork is not the answer.

Multi-pass review and attention dilution

A single review pass over a large changeset fails in three characteristic ways: it goes deep on some files and shallow on others, it flags a pattern in one file and approves the same pattern in another, and it misses defects because everything is competing for the same limited attention. The exam term for this is attention dilution, and producing that exact phrase is worth more than describing it correctly in your own words.

The fix is a pass structure, not a bigger model. The iterative refinement loop

Synthesis is not necessarily one shot. The coordinator runs a loop:

  1. Delegate the work; subagents return findings
  2. Invoke the synthesis subagent
  3. The coordinator evaluates the synthesis output for gaps
  4. If gaps exist, re-delegate to search or analysis subagents with queries targeted at those specific gaps
  5. Re-invoke synthesis with the enriched findings, and repeat until coverage is sufficient

Gap evaluation and loop termination belong to the coordinator, not to the synthesis agent. Three documented wrong turns: letting the synthesis agent go and search for itself (that breaks separation of responsibilities and least-privilege tooling), shipping the report with a "further research needed" note when re-delegation was available (coverage annotation is for gaps you cannot recover, not a substitute for the loop), and looping without a sufficiency criterion (the coordinator needs stated quality criteria to know when to stop).

Where coverage failures come from

Complete-looking subagent output plus missing topics points upstream, every time. Check the coordinator's decomposition before you question the subagents' execution or their tools.

Errors: two channels, four categories

Split this in two before anything else, because the exam's error questions are really two different questions wearing one coat.

Channel one: the protocol error. The request itself was malformed or unanswerable as a request. An unknown tool name, a structurally invalid call, a server fault. Nothing executed. This comes back as a standard JSON-RPC error, and a model rarely recovers from it by adjusting parameters, because the problem was not the parameters.

Channel two: the tool-execution error. The call was well-formed, the server accepted it, the tool ran, and the operation failed. This comes back as a successful result carrying isError: true. This is the channel that produces feedback a model can act on, and it is the channel the exam cares about.

Worked pair. Your agent calls publish_metric and gets back "unknown tool: publish_metrics": that is channel one, a name mismatch, nothing ran. Your agent calls publish_metric correctly and gets back a result with isError: true saying the metrics endpoint timed out after 30 seconds: that is channel two, the tool ran and failed. Same tool, same turn, two completely different remedies.

Exam vocabulary check: the flag is isError. The errorCategory and isRetryable fields are the exam guide's own payload convention for what belongs inside a channel-two result. Useful, testable, and not spec-defined fields. Do not treat them as protocol.

The four categories and the response each demands

The business row deserves a sentence of its own, because it reads like a failure and is not one. A policy limit refusing an action is a valid answer that the action is disallowed. }

Compare that with "Operation failed". The generic string forces the agent to guess the category from prose, and guessing wrong is expensive in both directions: it retries what can never succeed, or it abandons something a single retry would have fixed.

There is a ranking the exam applies here, and it has three rungs, not two. Returning a retryable flag beats making the agent guess from error text. But handling transient failures inside the tool or subagent, and surfacing only what needs a coordinator decision, beats both. Local recovery first, then propagate what you could not resolve, along with partial results and what was attempted.

Follow that logic to its consequence for writes. The retryable flag exists precisely so the agent never has to guess whether repeating a call is safe. On a write whose outcome is unknown, guessing is the entire risk, and the categorisation has to come from the tool, which is the only component that knows whether anything landed. If the correct precondition is "never run this twice", that is not an error-handling problem at all. That is the token-binding pattern two sections down.

Distributing tools, and tool_choice from the design side

Give an agent too many tools and selection reliability drops. The official number is 18 instead of 4 to 5. The mechanism is decision complexity: every extra tool is another candidate the model must discriminate against on every turn, another chance for two descriptions to sit close enough together to be confusable.

The fix is to reduce the decision space, which in practice means domain-scoped subagents each holding the 4 to 5 tools their role needs. Two documented wrong answers cluster around it. Keeping all 18 and writing better descriptions for every one treats a symptom: descriptions do help, but tool count is the root cause here. Merging overlapping tools into fewer general-purpose ones trades a selection problem for a precision problem.

Least privilege is the same principle applied to capability rather than count. An agent with a tool outside its specialisation will eventually misuse it. The sanctioned fix is not a prompt instruction and not a denylist of bad targets. It is replacing the generic tool with a constrained alternative that enforces the boundary at the interface: a document-loading tool that validates the URL points at a document, in place of a generic URL fetcher. Prompt instructions are probabilistic. An interface constraint is structural.

When an agent has a high-frequency need that would otherwise force constant round-trips through the coordinator, give it a narrowly scoped cross-role tool while complex cases still route through the coordinator. Built-in tools: choose by what you are searching

Grep and Glob are separated by one question: are you searching inside files or matching file paths? Grep is content search: function names, error strings, import statements, call sites. Glob is path pattern matching: files by name or extension. "Find every module that imports the deprecated queue client" is a Grep job, because the target lives inside the files. "Enumerate every component story file" is a Glob job, because the target is a naming pattern.

Getting this backwards fails in a specific way worth naming. Globbing for a symbol name only finds files named after it. Grepping for a word like "test" to find test files matches unrelated files that mention it and misses test files that never contain the literal word.

Edit and the unique-anchor requirement

Edit performs exact string replacement using a unique text anchor. When the anchor matches more than once, Edit cannot decide which occurrence you meant, so it fails. That is the whole mechanism, and everything else follows from it.

Which means the instinct to "be more specific" by shortening the anchor is inverted. A shorter, more generic string appears in more places, not fewer. Specificity here comes from length and surrounding context, not from brevity. The exam's sanctioned fallback when no unique anchor exists is Read the file in full, modify the content, Write it back. Forcing the replacement through a shell utility is a documented wrong answer: it bypasses the tool designed for the job.

Prompt engineering and structured output

Domain 4 splits into two halves: shaping what the model produces, and guaranteeing the shape of what comes back. You cleared the domain floor on Mock #1 and still lost the two questions that test the boundary between a guarantee and a hope. This chapter restores the mechanism behind each technique and names it the way the exam names it.

The question behind every Domain 4 question

Almost every Domain 4 stem hands you an output that is wrong in a specific way and asks which lever fixes it. There are only a few levers, and they fail in different places. Instructions shape judgment probabilistically. Examples shape judgment by demonstration. Schemas constrain shape deterministically. Validators check meaning in your code. Retries fix what the model can re-derive. Nothing on that list fixes information that was never in the source.

Distractors here are rarely absurd. They are the right lever aimed at the wrong failure: more instructions when instructions have already failed, a tighter schema when the error is semantic, another retry when the data is absent. Few-shot: demonstrate what an instruction cannot describe

An instruction is a description the model has to interpret before it can act. An example is a demonstration it can pattern-match directly. That difference is the whole technique. When a prose instruction has already produced inconsistent output, the failure is interpretive, and adding more prose gives the model more to interpret. The exam's own phrasing: few-shot examples are the most effective technique for consistently formatted output when detailed instructions alone produce inconsistent results.

The exam term is few-shot examples, or few-shot prompting. Not training, not teaching, not fine-tuning. That matters twice over: fine-tuning is on the official out-of-scope list, so an option phrased as retraining is wrong by construction, and your logged habit is to say "training" when you mean few-shot.

The corpus documents five kinds of example. A stem usually points at exactly one.

  • Ambiguous-scenario examples: the request, the action taken, and the rationale for choosing it over the plausible alternative. For routing and tool selection that fails on borderline phrasing.
  • Output-format examples: a filled-in specimen of the required structure (location, issue, severity, suggested fix). For when the content is fine but the shape varies between runs.
  • Acceptable-versus-problematic examples: paired samples, one marked do-not-flag and one marked flag. For cutting false positives while still letting the model generalise.
  • Varied-document-structure examples: the same fact extracted from an inline citation and from a bibliography reference. For inconsistently formatted sources.
  • Informal-measurement examples: "about two handfuls" mapped to a normalised amount plus a precision marker. For input spaces too diverse for rules.

Count and placement are the part candidates get wrong. The official guide asks for 2 to 4 targeted examples; the corpus worked scenario uses 4 to 6 for an ambiguous-routing fix. Either way the set is small, and it is aimed at the cases the model is currently getting wrong. Examples of the clear cases add tokens and change nothing, because the model was never wrong on those.

Explicit criteria: give the boundary, not a disposition

"Be conservative." "Only report high-confidence findings." "Check that comments are accurate." Each of these asks the model to adopt a disposition and then invent the boundary itself, freshly, on every item. That is why precision does not improve: nothing in the instruction says where the line is. Explicit criteria supply the line. The exam states it directly: general instructions like "be conservative" fail to improve precision compared with specific categorical criteria.

A criteria block has two halves, and candidates usually remember only the first. The positive criteria name the conditions under which to act: flag a comment only when its claimed behaviour contradicts the actual code behaviour, when it references something that does not exist, or when a TODO refers to an already-fixed bug. The stated non-flags name what to leave alone: stylistically dated comments, minor wording inaccuracies, missing comments. Both halves are doing work. Without the second, the model keeps inventing its own edge cases on the exclusion side.

Severity works the same way. "Rate severity appropriately" drifts between runs; defining each level with a concrete example holds steady. The exam calls this explicit severity criteria with concrete examples per level.

There is a second-order effect the exam tests on its own. False positives are not merely noise: high false-positive categories undermine trust in the accurate categories. Once a reviewer has dismissed twenty style findings, they dismiss the security finding too. The documented response is surgical: temporarily disable the noisy categories while their prompts are rewritten, keeping the high-precision ones running. Confidence scores do not help, because every finding is still on screen, and reducing strictness uniformly damages the categories that were working.

Sounds right

Actually right

Prompt chaining and attention dilution

When one prompt covers many items at once, quality does not degrade uniformly. It degrades in a signature pattern: detailed treatment of some items and one-line treatment of others, an obvious problem missed somewhere in the middle of the set, and contradictory verdicts where the same pattern is flagged in one item and approved in another. The exam term for the cause is attention dilution. Not "too much context", not "context overflow". Attention dilution.

The fix is prompt chaining: break the task into a sequence of focused passes where each pass covers one unit and each step's output feeds the next. For a multi-item review that means a per-unit pass for local issues plus a separate integration pass for cross-unit interactions. The integration pass is not optional garnish; splitting into per-unit passes alone loses exactly the cross-boundary findings a single pass was nominally covering.

The trap

"Move to a model with a larger context window." It is the most attractive wrong answer in the domain because the symptom looks like a capacity problem. Larger context does not fix attention quality. More tokens available is not the same as consistent attention per token, and the dilution pattern reproduces at any window size. Related wrong answers: raising max_tokens (that caps output length, not attention) and instructing the model to be equally thorough on every item (that is the vague instruction the criteria section just told you fails).

One boundary worth holding: chaining is for predictable, repeatable work where the steps are known in advance, such as a review or a migration. When subtasks only become clear during execution, that is dynamic decomposition, and it belongs to Domain 1.

The Message Batches API: what you buy and what you give up

Batch is a latency-for-cost trade with a hard architectural limit. You get a 50 percent cost saving. You give up latency certainty: the window is up to 24 hours with no latency SLA, and most batches finish far sooner, which is exactly what makes people plan badly. Match the API to what is blocking. A pre-merge check has a developer waiting, so it stays synchronous. An overnight technical-debt report, a weekly audit, a nightly test-generation run and a ten-thousand-document extraction are all latency-tolerant and belong in batch.

The limit the exam words precisely: the batch API does not support multi-turn tool calling within a single request. Read that carefully, because the sloppy version is a trap in both directions. A batch request can carry tool definitions and a full multi-turn history. What it cannot do is pause mid-request while your client executes a tool and returns the result. Each request is one shot: submit, get one model response, which may itself end in a tool_use you handle in a follow-up request.

custom_id is the join key. Results can come back in any order, so matching by position silently mis-joins your data. A meaningful unique id also tells you which items failed, so you re-submit only those, with the modification that addresses why (chunking the documents that blew the context limit, for instance). Re-submitting all hundred pays again for the ninety-five that worked; re-submitting the five unchanged reproduces the same failure.

And the arithmetic: plan against the worst case, never the typical one. Results needed within 30 hours means a submission deadline at 30 minus 24, so a 6-hour submission window. Stated generally, submission deadline equals downstream deadline minus 24 hours. Before committing a large batch, refine the prompt on a small synchronous sample: finding a prompt defect after it has been replicated across ten thousand requests costs a full batch and up to a full day.

The trap

Two opposite wrong answers live here. "Batches do not support tool use" is false and the corpus flags it as outdated. "Batch is just cheaper synchronous with a delay, so poll faster" is the other half: polling frequency changes nothing, there is no SLA, and anything blocking must stay synchronous. Calibrated confidence and stratified sampling

Two design choices carry the answers for a high-volume extraction system. Confidence is reported per field, not per document, because a document-level number averages away the one unreliable field. And scores are calibrated against a labeled validation set before any threshold is set on them, because a raw self-reported score is not a probability until something has checked it against ground truth. Low confidence, or ambiguous and contradictory sources, routes to human review; only segments with validated accuracy get automated.

The measurement half is stratified random sampling: audit a sample stratified across document types and field categories, and include the high-confidence stream, because that is where novel error patterns hide and compound unseen. The trap built on this is the aggregate-accuracy mask: an overall 97% can conceal a 40% failure rate on one rare document type. Validate accuracy by document type and by field before reducing human review, and require every segment to clear the bar independently.

The trap

Confidence appears twice in this domain with opposite verdicts. As an escalation trigger, model self-rated confidence is unreliable: a model can be confidently wrong, so escalate on structural signals (explicit human requests, policy gaps, repeated failures). As a review-routing signal in an extraction pipeline, field-level confidence calibrated on a labeled validation set is the correct design.
