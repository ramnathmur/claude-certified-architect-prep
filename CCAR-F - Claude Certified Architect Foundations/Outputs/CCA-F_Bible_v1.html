The tell"Use a model with a bigger context window" is the named trap. More room to put tokens is not more attention per token.

D4 §4.12 · KD#17

Prompt chaining — one job per link

"Find the issues" then "write fixes for these issues" beats one prompt doing both. Each link gets the previous link's output and one job to do with it.

The tellPredictable multi-stage work with known steps → chain it. Same family as the fixed pipeline in D1 §1.7; the opposite is dynamic adaptive decomposition, for when the scope is unknown.

D4 §4.14

Draw the escalation line with examples

"Escalate complex cases" is a line nobody can see, so the agent draws it somewhere private — usually backwards. Put worked cases on both sides of it.

The tellExplicit criteria plus few-shot examples of resolve-this / escalate-that. Not a self-rated confidence threshold, not a separate classifier — both add machinery around a boundary that was never drawn.

D4 §4.15

Testable criteria beat adjectives

"Check that comments are accurate" is resolved differently on every run. "Flag a comment only when the behaviour it claims contradicts the code" either holds or it does not.

The tellMove from abstract intent to an explicit condition. Adding "complete" and "helpful" alongside "accurate" makes it worse, not better.

D4 §4.16

Switch off the noisy categories

Two categories at ~50% false positives train the team to dismiss everything — including the 6% category that catches real safety defects. Stop the bleed first, fix the prompts after.

The tellTemporarily disable the high-false-positive categories. Confidence scores still show every finding; uniform strictness reduction damages the accurate ones; few-shot across all of them is too slow.

D4 §4.17

It can only avoid duplicates it can see

Six of ten suggested tests already exist. The generator is not careless — it has never been shown the suite.

The tellPut the existing test files in context. Cutting the request from 10 to 4 assumes an ordering it never promised; keyword matching misses semantic duplicates.

D4 §4.18

State your assumptions and go

Four clarifying questions and a third of people just leave. Name what you assumed, do the work, and invite the correction.

The tellApplies inside multi-agent systems too — a synthesis agent should not block waiting on the coordinator for every gap. Hidden defaults are the other failure: the user never learns what you chose.

D4 §4.19 · KD#19

Drift is your own voice drowning the brief

By turn nine the model is pattern-matching its own last eight replies rather than the standing orders at the top. Nothing overflowed — the orders are simply outnumbered.

The tellDegrading at 2,500–3,000 tokens is never window exhaustion, and the system prompt is resent every request. Fix: reminders at breakpoints, or replace verbose rules with few-shot examples.

D4 §4.20 · KD#23
Domain 5 · 15% of the paper

Context management & reliability

13 things to hold

The API remembers nothing

Every request is a stranger at the counter. It knows what you handed it and nothing else — so you hand over the whole file, every time.

The tell"It forgot what I said two turns ago" → the app is not resending messages[]. There is no session_id, and a vector DB is for months of history, not turn three. Also why cost and latency climb as a conversation grows.

D5 §5.1 · KD#25

Lost in the middle

The top and bottom of a long input get read properly. The middle sags — and the decisive finding sitting at the 40,000-token mark quietly does not exist.

The tellKey-findings summary at the start plus explicit section headings. Rotating whose output goes first does not change the attention pattern; compressing to 20K risks losing the thing itself.

D5 §5.2 · KD#20

The hybrid window — extract, summarise, keep

Three jobs at once. Extract the numbers, dates and IDs verbatim into a block. Summarise the low-density chat. Keep the last few exchanges intact so the current turn still makes sense.

The tellUniform summarisation loses precision; a pure recency window drops whatever was said early. Semantic retrieval is for months across sessions, not one long conversation.

D5 §5.3 · §5.13

The case-facts block survives compression

"Four instalments of £310, first due 12 October" becomes "a payment plan was discussed" by turn forty. Put the facts in a box outside the summariser's reach and they cannot be blurred.

The tellPersistent case-facts block, updated as facts appear, included in every prompt. Raising the summarisation threshold only delays it; a better summariser prompt still relies on perfect execution.

D5 §5.4 · KD#21

Trim tool output before it lands

One lookup returns forty fields when five matter — and all forty sit in context for every remaining turn of the conversation.

The tellPostToolUse hook filters to the relevant fields. Choose which fields by what downstream actually uses — dropping one the reviewer depends on creates a silent blind spot.

D5 §5.5

Send the mess to a side room

Discovery across 120 files will eat the window before implementation starts. An Explore subagent does it elsewhere and hands back a summary.

The tellIsolation for verbose discovery — not /compact mid-task, which destroys the precision the implementation phase still needs.

D5 §5.6 · KD#22

Months of history need retrieval, not summary

"What did we conclude about the theme of isolation?" is a needle question. Progressive summarisation has already ground that needle into "we discussed several themes".

The tellSemantic embeddings with retrieval over the full history. Rolling windows discard; summarisation abstracts away the exact conclusion you need to quote.

D5 §5.7 · KD#24

Escalate on structure, never on mood

An angry message can be trivially fixable and a calm one can be a genuine mess. Mood is not a complexity signal, and neither is the model's own confidence score.

The tellEscalate on: explicit request for a human · policy silent or ambiguous · repeated failures · above a financial threshold. First expression of frustration is not a request for a manager.

D5 §5.8

Two matches? Ask one more question

Same surname, same initial, two accounts. Picking the more recently active one is a coin flip with someone's money. One clarifying question settles it.

The tellAsk for another identifier — email, order number, account number. Do not pick by heuristic, do not take the first result, and do not escalate: this ambiguity is resolvable by asking.

D5 §5.8

97% overall can hide 40% wrong

The average is fine because the rare document type is rare. That type is still wrong four times in ten, and the aggregate is what is hiding it.

The tellBefore reducing human review: break accuracy down by document type and by field, and stratify-sample the high-confidence stream too. Raw self-reported confidence needs calibrating against a labelled set first.

D5 §5.9

Carry both numbers, and carry the date

Two credible sources, two different numbers. You do not pick a winner and you do not average them — you keep both with their names on and flag the disagreement upward.

The tellAnd attach publication dates: without them, a 2023 figure and a 2024 figure look like a contradiction instead of a year of growth. A bibliography at the end is not attribution.

D5 §5.10 · §5.11

Write the state down before the crash

Four hours of work dies eight minutes from the end. Each agent should have been exporting its state to a known place, with a manifest the coordinator reads on the way back up.

The tellStatus + findings + coverage + gaps per agent, plus a manifest. Conversation history does not survive a crash — the API is stateless. Scratchpad files serve the same role for long single-agent tasks.

D5 §5.12

Symptom → cause → fix, all on one screen

The last thing to read before you walk in. Most reliability questions describe a symptom and ask for the fix — so practise going symptom → cause → fix, in that order, rather than pattern-matching straight to an answer you like the look of.

The tellIf you can name the cause column, the fix column is forced. Most wrong answers are fixes for a cause the question never described.

The one that has cost you most

Numbers 1, 2 and 5 are the same instinct viewed from three sides, and they account for the single biggest cluster in your record — reaching for something that copes with the failure instead of something that removes it. Post-processing, a fallback, a retry, an extra layer, a firmer instruction. If one option adds a stage and another deletes the problem, take the one that deletes it.
