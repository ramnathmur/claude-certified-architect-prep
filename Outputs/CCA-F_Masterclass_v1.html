It just is not filed anywhere you can retrieve it under a two-minute clock, so a question arrives wearing a costume you have not seen, your search runs down the wrong shelf, and something you know perfectly well comes back wrong. Who this paper thinks you are

The credential is Claude Certified Architect – Foundations, and it is aimed squarely at a solution architect who designs and ships production systems: six months or more of hands-on time across the API, the agent-building kit, the coding assistant, and the tool protocol. It validates judgement about tradeoffs, not recall of documentation, and that is exactly the profile you fit. The paper is not written for a beginner and it does not reward beginner reflexes.

The building inspector

Picture a building inspector. She has never once asked a builder to recite a clause of the code. She walks into a finished building and asks four questions.

Is there a sign on the lift saying maximum eight people, or is there a sensor that will not close the doors at nine? Is every junction labelled, or does the next electrician have to guess? The lobby is enormous, which is impressive: does it help anyone find the fire exit? And when a crack shows in a wall, does the builder repair the wall, or hang a picture over it and add another storey?

That is your exam. Every question hands you a finished design and asks one thing: does it hold when real people use it at scale.

This paper does not ask what I know. It hands me a design and asks whether it holds.

What the paper actually is

Sixty questions, one hundred and twenty minutes. Two minutes each, and the clock is real. Every question is multiple choice with exactly four options: one correct, three incorrect, one answer only. No question ever has two right answers. So when two options both look right, one of them is wrong for a reason you have not found yet, and finding that reason is the entire game.

The questions arrive in four blocks of roughly fifteen, each block set inside one production situation. Those four situations are drawn at random from a set of six, so two of the six will not appear on your sitting, and you do not get to pick which two.

You cannot skip. The platform requires an answer before it will advance. And a wrong answer costs nothing beyond the mark you did not win, so guessing is free. Eliminate what you can, pick the best survivor, move on. There is no partial credit either: a question is scored whole, and there is no half mark for having the right instinct and choosing the wrong option.

The result comes back as one number on a scale from one hundred to one thousand, and seven hundred and twenty passes. You are told pass or fail. You will never be shown which areas cost you. Sit with that for a second, because it means the diagnosis you are doing right now, in these chapters, is the only diagnosis you are ever going to get.

The wrong answers were built for you

Now the thing that decides more marks than any single fact. Three of the four options are wrong, and they are written deliberately to be plausible to somebody who knows most of this but not all of it. That is stated openly in the official material. The wrong options are not filler. They are aimed.

In practice they come in four shapes. Learn the shapes and you can discard an option without arguing with it on its merits.

  • The symptom fix. It makes the reported number go away and leaves the cause exactly where it was.
  • The over-build. A correct idea, but it adds a layer, a classifier, a service, a bigger model, before the cheap fix at the source has been tried.
  • The wrong problem. A genuinely good solution to something the situation did not describe.
  • The thing that does not exist. A flag, a variable, a config key, a parameter, invented and planted to catch someone reciting from memory.

The one written for you specifically

The over-build is your trap. You have shipped real systems. A routing classifier, a trained model, a separate validation service: you have actually built these, and they all read as seniority. On this paper they are almost always wrong, because a cheaper fix at the point of failure was available and untried. When two options would both work, the smaller one wins.

Watch for the wording most effective first step and what change would most effectively address this. The words first and most are doing work. They are telling you that more than one option would help, and asking which one is proportionate. The tempting wrong answer is the bigger, more impressive one.

Now the spine. Four laws.

Everything else in this course hangs off four ideas. Learn them as sentences you can say out loud, because that is how you will use them at question forty with the clock running. I will show each one failing before I show it working.

Law one. Asking nicely is not a guarantee.

Here is the failing design. An agent handles refunds. Its instructions say, clearly and emphatically, always look the customer up and confirm identity before touching an order. It works. It works most of the time. Then production data shows that in a small percentage of cases the agent goes straight to the order lookup using only the name the customer typed, and occasionally refunds the wrong account.

Notice what did not happen: nobody wrote a bad instruction. The instruction was correct, explicit and forceful. The design failed anyway, because an instruction to a model is a request, and a request has a non-zero refusal rate. When money sits at the other end, non-zero is not acceptable.

The fix is not a better sentence. It is code. Block the order lookup and the refund until the identity check has actually returned a verified customer ID. Skipping the step is no longer unlikely; it is impossible. That is the sign on the lift versus the sensor on the doors.

The same law turns up in other costumes. A tool with a preview flag can always be called with the flag switched off, so a mandatory preview becomes optional in practice; split it into two tools, where the second refuses to run without a single-use token that only the first can issue. A rule that refunds above a threshold go to a human is a hook that fires before the tool runs, not a paragraph asking the model to be careful. And least privilege is this law worn inside out: if an agent must never do something, do not instruct it not to. Do not give it the tool.

If it must always happen, it goes in code. A prompt is a request, and requests get declined.
The stem will hand you a percentage. Twelve percent of cases skipped the step. A steady share of deletions ran without a preview. That number is the tell: a probabilistic mechanism is being used where a deterministic one is required. The correct option makes the bad path structurally impossible. The tempting wrong option makes it less likely, usually by writing the instruction again, only louder.

Law two. At every boundary, structure beats prose.

Failing design. A research coordinator sends a subtask to a search agent. The search times out. The subagent retries a few times internally, gives up, and returns the string "search unavailable".

What can the coordinator do with that? Two things. Retry the whole thing, or abandon it. It cannot retry with a narrower query, because it does not know what was tried. It cannot proceed on partial findings, because it does not know any exist. It cannot switch approach, because it was not told what kind of failure this was. One string of prose destroyed every recovery path that was available.

Now give the failure a shape. What kind of failure. What was attempted. What partial results came back. What alternatives exist. The coordinator can now make a decision, and the decision is informed. That is the labelled junction against the one the next electrician has to guess at.

Same law, other costumes, and they are everywhere in this material. A human taking over an escalated case sees your handoff summary and nothing else: no transcript, no tool history. So that summary carries the customer identifier, the root cause, the amount in question, what has already been done, why it is being escalated and what you recommend. A claim that arrives without its source attached cannot be cited later, however firmly you instruct the writing agent to cite sources, because nothing can recover metadata that was never passed. And when you need output your code can parse, you do not ask for JSON in prose and hope; you define a tool whose input schema is the shape you want and read the structure out of the tool call. That tool does not have to do anything. It exists to give the output a shape.

Anywhere one part hands to another, prose loses what the receiver needed. Give the handoff fields.
These arrive dressed as error-handling questions and are really information questions. The tempting wrong answers are the one that handles the failure tidily and reports it as a single word, and the one that tells a downstream component to try harder to remember something it was never given. Ask what the receiver can actually do with what it just got.

Law three. Room is not attention.

Failing design. A pull request touches fourteen files. One review pass reads all fourteen together. The output is uneven: some files reviewed in real depth, others skimmed, an obvious bug missed, and worst of all the same pattern flagged as a problem in one file and approved in another in the same run.

Contradictory verdicts on identical code is the tell: everything needed was inside the window, but the attention available was spread thin across it.

Which is why the option offering a bigger model with a larger context window is wrong, and it will feel wrong to reject the first few times, because more room genuinely does sound like it should help. Room and attention are different quantities, though, and the fix is to change the shape of the work: one focused pass per file for local problems, then a separate pass whose only job is how the files talk to each other. That is the enormous lobby that still does not help anyone find the fire exit.

Same law, other costumes. In a long input the beginning and the end are processed reliably and the middle degrades, so the findings that matter go at the top and headings go in so the model can find its way through the rest. A persona that has drifted by turn seven of a two-thousand-five-hundred-token conversation has not overflowed anything: the accumulated assistant turns are diluting the standing instructions and the model is pattern-matching its own recent answers. And a subagent whose only job is noisy exploration keeps that noise out of the main session entirely. That is not a cost saving. It is an attention saving.

A bigger container does not make it notice more. If the reading is uneven, cut the reading into smaller jobs.
The giveaway word is inconsistent. Uneven depth, contradictory verdicts on the same pattern, quality that decays late in a long input or late in a conversation. Wherever the symptom is inconsistency rather than a plain wrong answer, the wrong option offers you more room and the right option offers you a smaller job.

Law four. Fix the signal before you add a layer.

Failing design. Two tools: one fetches a customer, one fetches an order. Both carry a one-line description. Both accept similar-looking identifiers. Production shows the agent calling the customer tool when the user plainly asked about an order. Someone proposes a routing layer that reads each incoming message, detects keywords and identifier patterns, and pre-selects the tool.

That would probably work, which is exactly why it is on the paper: it builds a second decision-making system to compensate for the first one having been given nothing to decide with. The model selects tools by reading descriptions, and these descriptions say almost nothing. The signal is broken, and the proposal is to route around it rather than repair it.

Repair it instead: say what each tool does, what input it takes and in which formats, give example queries, say when to use it rather than the tool sitting next to it, and say when not to use it at all. That fix is cheap and lands at the exact point of failure; only when it fails do you start building infrastructure.

This is the picture hung over the crack, and it generalises hard, so hold on to it. When a multi-agent research system produces confident, well-executed output that misses whole parts of the topic, do not add more subagents and do not tell the writer to hunt for gaps — go up, because the coordinator decomposed the topic badly and every agent below it executed the wrong assignment perfectly. When escalation calibration is poor, write explicit criteria with worked examples before anyone trains a classifier. When the agent keeps reaching for a familiar built-in tool instead of your better custom one, improve your tool's description; do not remove the built-in.

Before I add anything, I ask what the model was actually given to decide with, and whether that thing is any good.

The shape of a single question

One more thing before the map. Every stem is built on the same skeleton. Once you can see the skeleton, the costume stops mattering.

  1. Two to five sentences of situation, set inside one of the six worlds.
  2. At least one hard number or concrete symptom: twelve percent of cases, fifty-five percent resolved on first contact, fourteen files, a log line, a config snippet.
  3. One decision question. What change would most effectively address this. What is the most likely root cause. How should you evaluate this proposal. What is the most effective first step.
  4. Four options, all written as full clauses, all grammatically parallel, none of them obviously foolish.

Read the number before you read the options. The number is the diagnosis. Fourteen files with contradictory verdicts is an attention problem. Twelve percent of a critical step being skipped is an enforcement problem. Two one-line tool descriptions and heavy misrouting is a signal problem. A subagent handing back a bare failure status is a boundary problem. The situation tells you which law is being tested before you have read a single option, and the options only exist to see whether you were listening.

Read the telemetry before the options. The number names the law.

Chapter two

The amnesiac genius

By the end of this you can open any request going to the model, name every part of it out loud, and say exactly which part is responsible when the thing forgets, slows down, or quietly stops behaving the way you configured it.

You hired a genius, and he forgets you the moment you leave the room

The consultant with no memory

Picture the sharpest consultant you have ever worked with. Hand her a file, any file, and in ninety seconds she finds the thing everyone else missed. You would hire her tomorrow.

She has one condition. The second she walks out of the room, everything goes. Not the skill: the skill stays. The file goes. Your name goes. What the two of you agreed goes. Next Tuesday she walks in bright and capable and has never met you before.

So you learn how to work with her. Before every meeting you hand over the whole file again: the brief, every earlier conversation written out in full, every answer she gave you last time. She reads it in seconds and picks up exactly where you left off. Anyone watching from the corridor would swear she remembers. She does not. You remembered, and you handed it to her.

Now build that badly, the way almost everyone builds it the first time. You wire up a chat box. The user types, you send that one message to the model, you print the reply. Turn one is clean. Turn two is clean. On turn three the user says 'go with the second option' and the assistant asks which options are being discussed. On turn five it asks for the account region the user already gave it on turn two.

Read that failure again, because it is the whole chapter and it is worth sitting with before you get the fix. The conversation is five turns old. Nothing was too long. No service went down. The model was not confused. Your application sent one sentence and quietly assumed somebody on the other end was holding the rest of the file. Nobody is holding it. There is no other end. Every call arrives at a machine that has never seen you before and will have forgotten you again before your next one.

The correct design is the boring one. You keep the transcript, and you hand the whole thing over on every single call. Your application is the memory — that is the design, not a patch over a shortcoming.

Notice what that buys you, because it is the hinge for everything that comes later. If your application decides what goes into the package, then your application decides what the model knows. You can leave things out. You can put things in that were never said out loud. You can reorder. Every context strategy you meet later in this course is a variation on one question: what goes in the package this time. None of them would be available to you if the remembering happened somewhere you could not reach.

The model keeps nothing between calls, so anything it looks like it remembers is something my own code put back into the request.

So look at what you are actually handing over

One call is one self-contained package. There are four parts of it you need to be able to name cold, because a good number of questions are built out of confusing one of them for another.

First, the standing instructions. Who the assistant is, how it should behave, the shape its answers should take. These sit in their own slot, outside the conversation. Second, the transcript: every turn so far, yours and its, in order. Third, the tools: descriptions of what it is allowed to reach for. Fourth, a ceiling on how long the reply may run. If a reply runs into that ceiling it stops there and the response tells you so, which is the next chapter's business.

The transcript is a list, and every entry in it carries a label saying who produced it. Your turns are marked one way, its turns another. That labelling is load bearing. It is how the model tells the difference between something you instructed and something it already said, and the gap between those two matters enormously by the end of this chapter.

Tool output comes home into that same list. When a tool runs, your application appends a result block to the conversation, and that block carries the identifier of the call it answers, so the pairing cannot get scrambled however long the transcript grows. The model reads that result on the next call the same way it reads everything else: because you sent it.

The tools slot deserves a second look too, because architects reading quickly treat it as configuration that was set up once at the start. It is not. The tool definitions travel in every request alongside everything else. The assistant has not acquired an ability that now belongs to it; it has been handed a list, this call, of what it may reach for. Stop sending a tool and the ability is gone on the next call, whatever the earlier transcript shows it doing.

One request carries the standing instructions, the whole transcript, the tool definitions and a length ceiling, and that package is everything the model gets.

Every turn, you hand over the whole file again

Follow the arithmetic, because it explains a bill. On the first call you send one message. On the second you send three: your first, its reply, your new one. On the third you send five. By turn twenty you are sending everything that has ever been said in that conversation, plus one new line, and you are paying to have all of it read again.

So a long conversation gets slower and dearer per turn, and the growth is in what goes in, not in what comes out. Watch for that in a question. When a scenario says latency and cost climbed as conversations passed fifty turns, the answer is that the entire history rides along on every request. The tempting wrong answers are that the model writes longer replies as it goes, or that some database is slowing down as the record grows. Neither is happening. The replies are the same length they always were, and there is no database in the picture at all.

There is a second reading of the same arithmetic, and it is the one that matters to you as an architect. Two beliefs about memory that will each cost you a mark

Both are believable. Both are common among competent engineers. Both are on the paper, and both have the same shape: they put the remembering on the far side of the wire.

They are attractive for a good reason. You have built systems that work exactly this way. Web sessions have identifiers. Long-lived assistants really do sit on top of retrieval. So when a scenario tells you the assistant forgot something, the shape of your own experience hands you an answer before you have finished reading the question. That is precisely what these options are engineered to catch: an experienced architect answering from the last system he built rather than from the one in front of him.

The first belief is that there is a session identifier: something you pass so the service can look up the earlier turns and stitch them back on. There is no such parameter. It does not exist. The paper plants convincing-looking parameter names on purpose, to see whether you will reach for one under time pressure. If an option offers you a knob that would make the service do your remembering, that option is wrong, whatever it is called.

The second belief is subtler and it catches good architects, because it sounds like something they already built. It is the idea that conversation memory means a vector database. It does not. Ordinary multi-turn memory is the transcript sitting in the request. Nothing is embedded. Nothing is retrieved. Nothing is scored for similarity. You append and you resend.

Retrieval does have a real home, and knowing where it lives stops you over-correcting into the opposite mistake. That home is a different question with a very different scale, and the drawer below draws the line for you.

Open the drawer: when retrieval genuinely is the answer

The retrieval option is not always wrong. It is wrong for ordinary conversation memory. It is right for one particular shape of problem, and the paper draws that shape carefully so you can tell them apart.

The shape: three months of sessions, history grown to around eighty-five thousand tokens, and the user asks what was concluded about one specific thread of discussion weeks ago. The assistant answers vaguely instead of pointing at the actual exchange.

  • Semantic retrieval over the full history, pulling back the specific relevant past exchanges on demand. This is the correct answer.
  • A rolling window keeps the recent and discards the early sessions, so the exact thing being asked about is already gone.
  • Progressive summarising keeps everything in compressed form, which is precisely how a specific quoted conclusion turns into a vague abstraction.
  • Marking conclusions with tags in the text does not solve retrieval at that size.

Tell the two apart by scale and by what is being asked for. Two turns ago and a thousand tokens: your application did not resend the history. Three months, eighty-five thousand tokens, one specific past conclusion: retrieval.

There is no server-side session and no vector store behind ordinary conversation memory; the only reason it knows turn two is that I resent turn two.

The standing instructions ride outside the transcript

Now the part people get wrong in a quieter way. You have a rule that has to hold for the whole conversation. Speak in this register. Always ask one clarifying question before proposing a refund. Never state a figure you have not looked up. Answer in this shape. Where does that rule live?

It lives in the standing instructions: the slot that is not part of the message list. It is sent again with every request. It is not something the assistant said, so it is not something the assistant can drift away from. And it is not buried in a turn that gets further away every time somebody speaks.

Learn the three failures by their consequences rather than as a list. Put the rule in the opening user message and it starts life as the oldest thing in the transcript and gets older every turn, while its pull quietly thins. Put it in an environment variable and it changes nothing whatsoever, because the model never sees your process environment; it sees the package you send. Put it in an opening assistant message, so the assistant appears to announce its own rules, and you have made your rule the same species as everything else it has said, which is to say something it can wander away from without contradicting anything.

And now the point that makes the last stretch of this chapter surprising rather than obvious. The standing instructions have every structural advantage available. They sit outside the transcript. They are resent with every request, at full length, unchanged. They carry more weight than any user turn. You would expect them to hold indefinitely. They do not, and the reason has nothing to do with any of that.

Every turn, the model's own earlier answers pile up in the transcript, and the model pattern-matches its own output. The instruction was outvoted.

The word to hold on to

Not dropped. Diluted. If you can hear yourself say dilution the moment a scenario shows behaviour degrading at a small token count, you will pick the right option in about four seconds. If you reach for the window instead, you lose the mark, and you lose it while feeling confident. That is the expensive kind.

The mechanism also tells you which fixes are real ones. Anything that puts a fresh, authoritative signal into the recent part of the conversation pushes back against the dilution. Anything that only makes the standing instructions longer and more abstract makes it worse, because abstract rules lose their grip faster than concrete demonstrations do.

Chapter three

Giving it hands

By the end of this you can take any agent question, find the loop hiding inside it, and say out loud who runs the tool, what ends the turn, and why three of the four stopping rules on the page are traps.

Who actually runs the tool

Before anything else, get one thing straight. When you give Claude a tool, Claude does not run it. Not once, not ever. The model writes down what it wants and stops talking. Your code runs the thing. Your code hands the answer back. Then the model picks up from where it left off.

The surgeon and the scrub nurse

A surgeon has both hands inside a patient. She says one word: scalpel. She does not turn around. She does not walk to the trolley. She does not go hunting through the drawer it lives in. She holds out her hand and keeps her eyes on the work.

The scrub nurse picks the right instrument off the tray and puts it into her palm, handle first. The surgeon carries on cutting. A minute later: forceps. Same again. Ask, receive, continue.

You are the scrub nurse. The model is the surgeon. It names what it wants and holds out its hand. Your code fetches, your code places it, and the model continues from where its attention already was. The nurse does not decide what the operation needs next. The surgeon does. And the surgeon does not fetch.

That division of labour is the whole design. It has a name you already use: the agentic loop. A sequence of API calls where the model's own response drives the next action, and your code sits in the middle running errands.

Notice where the work happens. Your tool does not run on Anthropic's side. You send a request that lists your tools by name, each with a description and a schema. The reply that comes back does not contain a row from your database. It contains a request: this tool, these arguments. The execution is yours. The credentials are yours. The timeout, when it times out, is yours to catch. That is why an agent can query your internal order system without anybody outside your network ever touching it.

There is a second thing hiding in the analogy and it matters more than it looks. The surgeon never leaves the table, and the model never waits. It does not sit there holding the line open while you go and query the database. It emits its request and that call is finished. Everything that happens next happens because you started another one. The continuity your users experience is manufactured entirely by your code, one round trip at a time, and the model rejoins each time with whatever you chose to put in front of it.

Claude asks for the tool. My code runs it and hands the result back. Claude never executes anything itself.
This rarely arrives as its own question. It arrives underneath one, as an assumption baked into an option. The tempting wrong answer is any option that quietly puts execution somewhere else: a tool that "runs" when Claude calls it, a result that appears without your application putting it there, a failure the platform is supposed to have handled for you. If the tool is one you defined, you executed it, and every consequence of that execution is yours to shape.

The design you would sketch on a whiteboard, and the day it breaks

Here is the version almost everyone writes first, and it is a reasonable thing to write. You know the job. A support request comes in, so you look up the customer, then you look up the order, then you decide about the refund. Three steps, in that order, in code. A branch or two for the obvious variations. It reads cleanly, it tests cleanly, and you can draw it on a whiteboard in forty seconds.

Then a real request arrives. The person has two problems in one message: they were billed twice, and a discount never applied. Your sequence has no shape for that. Or they open with an order number and no account details, so the second step is the one that should run first. Or the lookup returns nothing at all and the right next move is a question back to the customer, which is not a node on your tree. Every one of those is a normal Tuesday afternoon. Your tree covers none of them.

The reflex is to add branches. One more condition, one more path, one more step in the script. That is the move that makes it worse. Each branch is another case you predicted in advance, and the whole problem is that you cannot predict in advance. A script that breaks when reality deviates from it does not get better by having more script in it.

So you invert it. You stop deciding the order. You hand the model the tools, the descriptions, and the conversation so far, and you let it reason about what to call next. It has the actual message in front of it. It has the results of the last three calls in front of it. It picks. Your code now does exactly two jobs: run whatever was asked for, and look at why the model stopped.

What that buys you is the case you did not think of. Two problems in one message: the model calls the same tools in a different order and pulls both threads. An empty result: it reads it and reacts to it. A missing detail: it asks. Your code needed no node for any of that, because your code is not the thing making the call.

It costs you something, and you should be able to name the cost rather than pretending there is none. You give up the comfort of knowing the exact call sequence before the request arrives. Reproducibility gets softer. Two identical-looking requests can take slightly different routes to the same answer. That trade is worth making when the shape of the work is genuinely unknown up front, which is the situation an agent exists for in the first place. Where the steps really are fixed and known in advance, a sequence is a perfectly good design and the exam will not punish you for saying so. The failure being tested is a fixed sequence pointed at work that refuses to hold still.

The loop, slowly

Now walk the loop itself, one step at a time. Take it slowly, because most of what you revise later balances on this and nothing else.

  1. Send the conversation so far to Claude, with the tools available.
  2. Receive the response. Look at one field: the reason it stopped.
  3. If that reason is tool_use, pull out what it asked for, run it, append the result to the conversation, and go back to step one.
  4. If that reason is end_turn, the turn is over. Return the text to the person.

Step three has a part people skip, and it is the part that makes the whole thing work. You append the result to the conversation history. You do not stash it in a variable and paraphrase it into the next prompt. The tool's output goes back in as a block of the conversation, so that on the next pass the model is reasoning with that answer sitting in front of it, in the same place its own earlier words are sitting.

That is what lets it chain. The order lookup comes back showing a purchase date outside the return window, so the refund path is closed, so the next move is an explanation rather than a refund call. The model can only reach the third link if the first one is in the history. Break the appending and you get an agent that calls the same tool three times and never learns anything from the answers.

Say the shape out loud, because the shape is what you are being tested on. This is a loop, not a pipeline. A pipeline knows how many stages it has. This does not, and it must not, because the number of tool calls a task needs is a property of the task and the task is a stranger until it arrives. One request might go round once. Another might go round nine times as the model reads a result, finds it incomplete, and asks for something else. Both are healthy. Your code has no opinion about which is correct, and the moment it develops one, you have built one of the three false finish lines coming up.

Every reason it can stop, and what each one obliges you to do

Four values. Learn what each one obliges you to do, not what each one means. Meaning is easy. The questions put you inside a situation and ask what you do next, so obligation is the thing they are built out of.

tool_use is the surgeon holding out her hand. She wants an instrument. You execute the tool, you append the result, you call again. The loop continues. This is the only value that keeps you going round.

end_turn is the surgeon putting her hands down. Claude finished its response. You stop the loop and return the output to the user. This is the ordinary way a turn ends, and it is the only signal that should ever end one.

max_tokens means the response hit the token limit and got cut off. This is not a finish. It is an interruption dressed as a finish, which is precisely why it is dangerous. Handle it gracefully, and accept that you may need to continue the response rather than shipping half a sentence to a customer and calling it an answer.

stop_sequence means a custom stop sequence was hit. What that obliges you to do is application-defined, because you are the one who configured the sequence in the first place. There is no universal correct handling and the exam does not pretend there is. If you never set one, you will never see it.

Three false finish lines

Now the part that costs marks. There are three ways of deciding the loop is finished that feel completely reasonable at the moment you write them. All three are named anti-patterns. Each one is built to look right precisely on a day you know this material, because each is written in the register of careful engineering. Read them and notice exactly where each starts to sound sensible, because that moment is the trap and it will feel identical in the exam hall.

The first: reading the text for something that sounds like done

The reply comes back with prose in it. You scan the prose. If it says the task is complete, or here is the summary, or I have finished, you break the loop. It works on your first ten test cases. Of course it does. You wrote the test cases, and the model answered in the register you were expecting.

Then it writes that this completes the first half of the analysis, and your matcher sees the word completes and stops a job that was halfway through. Then on another turn it finishes properly with a table and no closing sentence at all, your matcher never fires, and you loop again on a model that has nothing left to say. You are inferring a machine state from a sentence. The machine already told you that state, in a field, with no ambiguity, and you looked straight past it at the prose.

The failure is not that your phrase list was too short. Adding phrases is the same mistake with more words in it, and every phrase you add widens the surface for a false stop.

The second: checking which content blocks came back

This one is more sophisticated, and it catches better engineers than the first one does. You do not read the prose at all. You inspect the structure of the reply. If there is a tool request in there, keep going. If all you got back was text, the model must be finished, so stop.

It is a structural check rather than a linguistic one, so it feels like the disciplined choice. It is still a guess. The presence of assistant text tells you nothing about whether the turn is over. Text can appear alongside a tool request in the same reply, as commentary about what the model is about to do. Text can appear on a turn that has further to run. Text is not a completion indicator, and that phrase is close to a direct quotation of how the anti-pattern is named.

There is a field whose entire purpose is to answer this exact question. You are standing next to it, weighing the parcel instead of reading the label on it.

The third: the iteration cap as the thing that decides

Here is the one that sounds most like good engineering, which is why it is the most expensive of the three. Loops can run away. Runaway loops cost real money. So you cap it at ten passes and return whatever you have. Now it cannot spiral. Defensive, cheap, and the kind of thing a reviewer nods at without reading twice.

And it is the anti-pattern, because you have made an arbitrary number the primary way you decide the work is finished. A job that genuinely needed twelve calls now returns truncated work with no error attached, which is the worst possible failure shape: it looks like a completed answer. A job that finished cleanly in three had a cap sitting there deciding nothing at all. The number is not connected to the work. It cannot be, because you chose it before the work existed.

A cap is not forbidden. Keep one. It is a circuit breaker. A circuit breaker is not how you decide the building has finished work for the day.

The three of them share one root. Each replaces a fact the API gave you with an inference you made yourself, and each inference is wrong in both directions at once: it stops work that was unfinished, and it continues work that was already done. The repair in all three cases is the same single move. Branch on the stop reason.

Notice something about how those three were written above. Each one was described sympathetically first, with the reason someone sensible would build it, and only then taken apart. That is not politeness. That is the exact experience of meeting them in a question. They will be phrased by somebody who understands why they are appealing, sitting next to an option that just says to check the stop reason and sounds almost too simple to be the answer. The simplicity is the tell. The right answer here has nothing to prove, because the API already did the work.

Say it once and keep it

Termination is decided by the stop reason. Never by text content. Never by iteration count. If an option's stopping logic mentions phrases, keywords, content blocks, or a number of passes, it is wrong, and it will have been written to sound careful. Careful-sounding is the whole point of a distractor.

The loop ends because the reply said end_turn, not because the words looked final or the counter ran out.
This arrives as an agent that stops early, or one that keeps going after it should have stopped, with a percentage attached to make it concrete. Four options follow: a better phrase list, a content-block check, a tighter or looser cap, and one that branches on the stop reason. Three of them will describe careful, defensive engineering. The stop-reason option will often read as the plainest sentence on the page, because it has nothing clever to say. Pick the plain one. This one will cost you a mark if you reward effort in the wording.
Open the drawer: where a cap is legitimate

The wording matters, so hold on to it exactly. The anti-pattern is setting arbitrary iteration caps as the primary stopping mechanism. Primary is the load-bearing word in that sentence.

  • Legitimate: a cap as a safety backstop, so a defect cannot bill you forever.
  • Anti-pattern: a cap as the thing that tells you the work is done.
  • Tell them apart by what happens when it fires: a backstop is an incident, a decider is a silent half answer.

In an option, read for the cap's role, not its presence. An option that keeps a limit as a guard while terminating on the stop reason is fine. An option where hitting the limit is how the loop knows to finish is wrong, however reasonable the number sounds and however much production experience the sentence implies.

Writing a job description

By the end of this you will be able to read a page of tool definitions and say, before a single request is sent, which two of them will be mistaken for each other and exactly what to rewrite.

Two people, and the same word on both doors

The two desks

Two colleagues sit at two desks. The same word is on both doors: Analyst. You have a job in your hand and you have to give it to one of them. You have never met either. All you have is the word on the door.

So you guess. You are right about half the time. Half your work lands on the wrong desk, gets half done, and comes back late. Nobody on either desk did anything wrong. They did what they were handed.

Now think about what actually fixes this. Not a receptionist standing in the corridor routing for you. Not a note to yourself saying think harder next time. What fixes it is somebody writing on the first door: takes uploaded files, pulls the numbers out of them. And on the second door: takes results that came back from a web search, pulls the useful part out of them. The doors were always doing the routing. They were just blank.

In your agent, the door is the tool description, not the code behind the tool and not the schema — the sentence you wrote about it. That is the whole chapter.

The description is not documentation, it is the chooser

When Claude has to pick a tool, the description is the primary input it uses to decide. That is the mechanism. There is no separate routing model, no hidden intent classifier, no lookup table sitting behind the scenes. The model reads the descriptions you supplied and picks one. So a description is not a comment left for the next engineer to read. It is live production logic written in English, and it is the piece of your system most likely to have been written in thirty seconds at the end of a Friday.

Watch the naive version fail before you learn the rule. A research setup has a web-search side holding a tool called analyze_content, described as analyzing content and extracting key information. It has a document side holding a tool called analyze_document, described as analyzing documents and extracting key information. Read those two out loud. One word apart in the name. One word apart in the description. Requests like analyze the uploaded quarterly report went to the web-search side 45% of the time.

Same failure, different costume. A support agent holds get_customer and lookup_order. Both carry a single line of description. Both accept identifiers that look alike on the page. The agent calls get_customer when the customer asked about an order. Nobody wrote anything false here. They wrote almost nothing, and almost nothing cannot discriminate between two things.

Read the fixes carefully, because the fix is where the marks are lost. For the two research tools, you rename one: analyze_content becomes extract_web_results, and its description says out loud that it handles information retrieved from web search and URLs. For the two support tools, you expand both descriptions with input formats, example queries, edge cases and boundaries. In both cases you went back to the signal and improved the signal. You built nothing.

Here is the rule underneath both, and it is worth more than one mark to you: fix the signal before you add a layer that compensates for a bad signal. A pre-routing classifier sitting in front of the agent is the tempting answer, because it looks like real architecture and it feels like progress. It leaves two ambiguous descriptions sitting exactly where they were, still ambiguous, for every other caller and every future turn.

The description is the selection mechanism, so when the wrong tool keeps getting called I go and read the descriptions before I build anything.
You will get a misrouting scenario with a number attached: 45% of uploaded-document requests reaching the wrong agent, or an agent reaching for the customer tool on order questions. Four options. One rewrites a description or renames a tool. The other three add a routing classifier before the agent decides, add few-shot examples to the prompt, or merge the two overlapping tools into one general lookup. The classifier is the most attractive wrong answer because it sounds like engineering. It is the symptom-level fix. Take the description, and take it first.

Always present, always pulling

Now the thing that goes wrong under pressure, so kill it properly. Tool descriptions are not fetched when needed. There is no moment where the model thinks I might need a refund tool, let me go and look one up. Every tool you give an agent travels with the request: its name, its description, its input schema. All of them. Every single turn. Connect a set of servers and their tools are discovered at connection time and are available to the agent simultaneously, from that point on.

Follow the consequence, because the consequence is what the exam actually asks about. A tool you added for one rare edge case is not sitting quietly in a drawer waiting to be needed. It is standing in the room on every request, being weighed, competing for selection against the tool you actually wanted. Adding a tool is never free. It costs you a little accuracy on everything else that agent does, forever.

The phrase to hold

Always present, always pulling. Say it out loud in the room. If an option implies that descriptions are loaded on demand, or that an unused tool costs nothing, that option is wrong. It is a quiet trap and it is an easy one to walk into at speed.

What a description actually has to say

Four things. Learn this list as a list, because the exam grades against it almost directly.

  • What the tool does, and what comes back from it.
  • What input it accepts: formats, identifier types, example values.
  • When to use it rather than the tool standing next to it.
  • When not to use it. The boundary, said out loud.

The fourth item is the one people skip and the one that does the most work. A description that only says what a tool is for leaves every ambiguous request as a coin flip between neighbours. A description that also says do not use this for uploaded files, use the document tool gives the model a line to sit on one side of. Drawing the line between this tool and its nearest neighbour is the useful part of the job.

One more thing, and it is subtle enough that it separates people who half-know this from people who own it. Write the description in the words a person would actually use to ask for the thing, rather than purely in terms of which identifier it swallows. A customer says I need help with my recent purchase. If your order tool's description says only that it accepts a numeric order identifier, that description is true and useless at selection time, because nobody speaks in identifiers. The description has to reach out and catch the phrasing that real requests arrive in.

Open the drawer: what a description that works looks like

A tool definition in the request carries three fields: name, description, and input_schema. Here is the customer lookup written properly, and it is worth reading clause by clause.

{
  "name": "get_customer",
  "description": "Finds a customer by email or ID. Returns the customer profile, including name, email, order history, and account status. Use this tool BEFORE lookup_order to verify the customer's identity. Accepts an email (format: user@example.com) or a numeric customer_id.",
  "input_schema": {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {
      "email": {"type": "string", "description": "Customer email"},
      "customer_id": {"type": "integer", "description": "Numeric customer ID"}

  • Finds a customer by email or ID: what it does.
  • Returns the customer profile, including name, email, order history, and account status: what comes back, so the model knows whether this call will give it what the next step needs.
  • Use this tool BEFORE lookup_order: the ordering relationship with its nearest neighbour, written into the description itself.
  • Accepts an email (format: user@example.com) or a numeric customer_id: input formats with an example value, which is what stops the model from handing an order number to a customer lookup.

Compare that with the version that failed: Gets customer information. Every one of the four jobs above is missing. That is why expanding a minimal description with formats, example queries, edge cases and boundaries is graded as the correct first step and not as busywork.

A description has to say what it does, what it takes, when to pick it over the tool next to it, and when to leave it alone.

It was probably written to be helpful. It is now overriding descriptions that are doing their job correctly. A split that clean, keyed to one word, with good descriptions in place, points at an instruction, not at the model.

Notice what this does to your diagnostic order. Descriptions first, always. But when the descriptions are already good and the misrouting has a systematic, word-shaped pattern, the next place you look is the standing instructions, not the tools. Both live in the same request. Both influence selection. You wrote both.

Open the drawer: the two wrong ways to win that argument

Both of these will appear as options, and both are graded wrong.

  • Remove or disable the built-in so the agent has no alternative. This breaks every legitimate content-search case the built-in was right for, and it treats the existence of a good tool as the problem when the actual problem is an under-specified description.
  • Add a system prompt rule saying to always prefer the server's tools. Blunt and keyword-sensitive, which is exactly the failure you just studied. It will misroute the cases where the built-in genuinely was the right choice.

The pattern is the same one from the misrouting scenario: fix the signal before you add a layer, and before you remove a tool, to compensate for a bad signal.

Parameters are part of the same job description

A tool is its sentence plus the shape of what you let a caller hand it. Every parameter is a place where the model can be right or wrong, and your job is to make wrong difficult to express.

Start with fixed lists. If a parameter has a known set of values, do not accept free text for it. Give it a fixed set of allowed values and a value outside that set becomes unsayable. When the set might grow, add a catch-all value plus a companion detail field, so a real case that falls outside your categories does not get filed confidently under the nearest wrong one. And when the model genuinely cannot tell which value applies, give it a value that means unclear. An honest unclear beats a confident wrong category, and that is the answer the exam wants every time.

Then identifiers. Act on stable identifiers, never on names. A name is ambiguous and an identifier is not. This is the reason the customer lookup is supposed to run before the order lookup and before any refund: it is the step that turns a human-supplied name into a verified identifier the next call can be trusted with. Look up, then act.

And when that lookup comes back with several matches, do not let anything pick one. Choosing the most recently active account selects the wrong one around 15% of the time in the scenario the exam draws from, and a wrong account means a wrong refund against a real person. You ask for one more identifier: an email, a phone number, an order number. One extra conversational turn and the ambiguity is gone. Escalating to a human here is also wrong, because this was resolvable without one.

Last piece of parameter design, and it grows into an architectural decision. When one parameter's value changes what another parameter means, or changes whether the tool acts at all, you have two tools wearing one coat. The classic is a removal tool with a preview switch on it. The switch is supposed to force a preview before anything is deleted. It does not, because the agent can set the switch to skip the preview and go straight to the removal, and in production it does. Split it into two tools, where the preview returns a single-use confirmation token and the execution requires that token as an input, and skipping the preview stops being discouraged and starts being impossible.

The same instinct applies to a tool that is simply too general to describe. A document analysis tool that does everything gets split into purpose-specific tools with defined inputs and outputs: pull the data points out, summarise the content, check one claim against the source. Each of those can now be described in a sentence that draws a line, which is where this chapter started.

What comes back is a design decision too

Turn the tool around and look at the other end. The result is not a payload destined for a log file. It is the next thing the model reads, and it decides what happens next. Design it the way you designed the description: for the model that will read it, and for what it has to do next.

First rule. Return the identifiers the next step will need. The customer lookup returns a verified customer identifier precisely because the order lookup and the refund both require one. If your result comes back as a pleasant human-readable summary with no identifier anywhere in it, the agent has to guess or ask again, and guessing is how the wrong account gets touched.

Second rule. Return what matters and stop. A single order lookup can come back carrying forty or more fields when five of them are relevant to the task in hand. Those fields do not evaporate after the turn. They sit in the conversation and are re-sent on every following request, spending tokens and attention every time. Trim the result to the fields that matter before it enters the conversation. Telling the model to ignore the irrelevant fields does nothing, because the tokens were already spent getting them there.

Third rule, and this one arrives on the paper dressed as an error-handling question. Finding nothing is a valid answer. A search that ran perfectly and matched zero rows is a success with an empty result set. Flag it as a failure and the agent will retry a query that worked and escalate a case that had a clean answer. The mirror image is worse. If the database is unreachable and you hand back an empty list, the agent will tell a customer they have no orders, which may simply be false. Two different things happened, so send two different signals.

Eighteen tools is the bug

Now the count, which is the last piece and the one people never think of as a design decision. An agent holding eighteen tools calls the wrong one often. A peer agent doing comparable work, holding four or five tools scoped to its job, selects correctly. Same model. Same standard of description. The only thing that differs is how large a choice each one is being asked to make.

This follows directly from always present, always pulling. Every tool is a live candidate on every turn. Eighteen candidates means more near-misses, more overlapping edges, more chances that two descriptions brush against each other in one particular phrasing on one particular request. You are asking the model to discriminate eighteen ways, repeatedly, all day.

Three tempting wrong answers, and you should be able to name the flaw in each before you read on. Keep the eighteen and write better descriptions for all of them: descriptions do help, and you should write them well regardless, but the count is the root cause in this scenario and better prose does not shrink the choice. Merge the similar tools into one general entity lookup: that throws away the semantic precision this whole chapter was about, and while it can be a legitimate architectural choice later, it is not the correct first step. Give every agent the full catalogue for flexibility: agents handed tools outside their speciality misuse them, and the synthesis agent quietly starts running its own web searches instead of synthesising what it was given.

The right move is to scope each agent to one area of work and give it the four or five tools that area needs. Restricting the tool set is the mechanism that separates responsibilities between your agents. It is not a limitation you apologise for in a design review. It is the design.

One sanctioned exception, and the exam likes to test it because it looks like a contradiction. When an agent has a high-frequency need that sits just outside its speciality, give it one narrowly scoped tool for that need rather than making it round-trip through the coordinator every single time. A synthesis agent that constantly has to confirm individual facts gets a narrow fact-verification tool. It does not get full web search, because that is the door to it doing open-ended research instead of the job it was created for. Complex questions still go back through the coordinator. Narrow tool for the simple high-volume case, coordinator for the hard case.

The stem gives you two agents and two numbers, usually eighteen against four or five, and asks what to do. The right answer restricts the tool set by role. The trap is the option that says keep them all and improve every description, because it uses the exact language you have been trained all chapter to reward. Read the stem again: if the scenario has already told you that a comparable agent with five tools performs correctly, the count is what is being tested.
Selection reliability is a function of how many candidates I put in the room, so I scope the agent to one job rather than merging tools or bolting on a router.

When the tool fails

By the end of this you can take any failure your agent hits, say whether the fault is in what you sent or in the world your call met, name which of four kinds it is, and say the one move it demands, without guessing.

This is the material that has cost you the most marks. Not because it is hard. Because four completely different events are wearing one word, and that word is error. Take the word apart and this becomes the easiest scoring ground on the paper. Leave it whole and you will keep picking the option that feels responsible and is wrong.

Four couriers come back

You send four couriers out with four packages. All four come back. If you have trained them to say only one thing when something goes wrong, you hear the same sentence four times, you make the same move four times, and three of those moves are wrong.

The first says: there was traffic, I could not get through this time. The second says: that address does not exist, it is not a real street. The third says: head office does not permit deliveries to that address, and the rule is the rule. The fourth says: my gate pass has been revoked, they turned me back at the door.

Four sentences, four different next moves. Send the first courier straight back out. Never send the second back with the same address written on it, correct the address first. Do not argue with the third, go and tell the customer what the rule says. And the fourth does not go anywhere until somebody has sorted out his pass. The disaster in this room is a courier who has been trained to say only two words: delivery failed.

A hook for the exam room

Whenever a question in this area feels slippery, put the failure back into the courier's mouth. Say it out loud as a sentence a person would actually say. The correct option usually falls straight out of the sentence.

One flag, and the thing it refuses to tell you

Start here, because this is the split nobody assembles for you, and everything downstream depends on it. One word is doing the work of four events, and the events want opposite things from you.

First, the signal itself. A tool result carries an optional flag, isError, set true when the call failed. That is the whole mechanism. A failed tool call and a successful tool call come back through the same door, in the same shape, and that flag in the payload is the difference between them.

If your tool never sets the flag, nothing about the shape of the response tells the agent that anything went wrong. The model reads your failure as data and reasons on top of it.

Second, the thing the flag will not tell you. It says something failed. It does not say what kind of failure, and the kind is the whole of the decision. That is why a bare flag is never the winning option in a question about error design. The flag opens the conversation. The category finishes it.

Third, the split that decides who owns the fix. Some failures are a defect in what you sent: a malformed identifier, an input the schema rejects. The socket in the wall

Before the standard socket, every appliance came with its own wiring. The lamp maker had to know your house. The house had to be opened up for the toaster. Every new thing was a new piece of custom work, and the number of pieces of custom work grew as fast as the number of things you owned times the number of houses.

Then somebody standardised the hole in the wall. Now anything with the right plug works, and neither side has to know anything about the other. That is the whole idea. One shape, agreed in advance, so strangers can connect without negotiating.

Now hold on to what the socket never promised. It does not make your appliance safe. It does not pay your bill. It does not stop you plugging nine heaters into one circuit and melting it. The plug fits. That is all the plug ever said.

Say the plain version out loud. It is an open protocol for connecting outside systems to Claude. What it standardises is the shape of the connection and the shape of the description, so a server written by somebody who has never heard of your agent can be plugged into your agent anyway.

That is a big deal, and it is genuinely all it is. Almost every question that catches people in this material comes from quietly expecting the socket to do more than fit.

It standardises how something connects, and guarantees nothing about what happens once it does.

C

Which file, and whose secret

Two files. Learn them by what they reach, not by their paths, and the paths will stick anyway.

The project file sits at the root of the repository. It is version controlled, so it reaches everybody who clones or pulls. That is the file for tooling the whole team is supposed to have. The personal file sits in your home directory and reaches only you. That is the file for your own overrides and the server you are trying out this week and have not inflicted on anyone.

Now the question that gets asked in some costume every single sitting. The team shares one server, but every developer holds their own credential. Where does it go?

It goes in the shared project file, with the credential written as a reference to an environment variable rather than a value. At runtime the variable is substituted from each person's own environment. The configuration is shared and version controlled; the secret never enters the repository. Document which variable people need and you are finished.

Both wrong answers are worth naming, because both are things real teams actually do. Letting every developer add the server to their own personal file gives you as many subtly different setups as you have developers, and a new joiner with nothing at all. Committing a placeholder credential puts credential-shaped material in the repository and invites somebody to paste a real one over it one tired afternoon.

Borrow before you build

The last piece follows straight from the socket. Once a shape is standard, other people have already made things that fit it. For a standard integration, an issue tracker, a code host, a chat system, use the community server that already exists. The distractor here always wears the same costume: build it ourselves for control. It reads as rigour. It is rebuilding a maintained standard integration by hand, and it spends your one budget of custom effort on the part that was already solved.

One more, because it costs people marks

Sometimes you connect a genuinely more capable server and the agent keeps using a familiar built-in instead. That is the same thing that bit you with the near-identical tool names: selection runs on descriptions, and a vague description loses to a familiar habit. Enrich the description so it spells out what the server can do that the built-in cannot. Do not disable the built-in, and do not add a blunt rule telling the model to always prefer one source of tools.

Community servers for standard integrations, custom servers only for workflows nobody else has.

Hold the whole thing in one picture

One connection shape. Three kinds of thing on the other side, sorted by who decides: the model acts, the application supplies readable data, a person runs a saved workflow. Connecting discovers what you can ask for and delivers none of your data. And then the long list of things you still build yourself, because the socket only ever promised that the plug would fit.

Across the paper, integration questions almost never reward the option that switches something on. They reward the option that builds the missing piece: the gate, the scoped tool set, the structured failure, the shared configuration file. When two options look equally sensible, pick the one that would still work if the model behaved unexpectedly on that turn.

Chapter seven

One brain isn't enough

By the end of this chapter you will be able to look at a multi-agent system that is producing the wrong answer and say, in one move, whether the fault sits inside a worker or in the way you divided the work.

You already know what a coordinator is. You have drawn the diagram. What you have not yet done reliably, under time pressure, with the question wearing an unfamiliar costume, is look at a broken multi-agent system and say in one move where the fault lives. That is the whole of this chapter.

The head chef and the brigade

Service starts at seven. The head chef does not cook. She reads the ticket, decides what the plate is, and calls the order down the line. Fish station takes the fish. Sauce takes the sauce. Garnish takes the garnish. Each one works alone, at its own bench, with its own pans.

Here is the part that matters. The fish station cannot hear what the sauce station was told. Nobody at a station has seen the ticket. They know the one sentence the chef shouted at them, and nothing else. Everything comes back to the pass, and the chef is the only person in that kitchen who ever sees the whole plate.

So if the chef calls the order badly, five excellent cooks will send out the wrong dish. Quickly. Cleanly. With no errors anywhere. That is the failure you have to learn to spot on sight.

What the chef actually owns

Start with the job description, because it is shorter than you think. The coordinator does four things. It splits the work. It writes the brief for each worker. It decides which workers to call at all. And it takes back what they produce, checks it, and turns it into one answer.

Notice what is missing. The coordinator does not do the research. It does not read the documents. And the workers never talk to each other. Every message in the system goes through the middle, the way every plate goes across the pass. That shape has a name you should be able to produce on demand: hub and spoke. One hub, spokes out to the workers, and no rim joining them to each other.

That looks like a bottleneck until you ask what you buy with it. Four things, and they are worth memorising as a set, because the exam likes to ask why this shape and not a mesh.

  • Visibility. The coordinator observes every exchange in the system. Nothing happens off to the side.
  • Uniform error handling. One place decides retry, skip, or escalate, so failures are handled the same way every time.
  • Information control. The coordinator decides what each worker is allowed to see.
  • Separation of responsibilities. Each worker has one narrow, defined scope and stays inside it.
The coordinator splits the work, briefs each worker, decides who runs, and puts the answer back together. Nobody else in the system sees the whole thing.

The fish station cannot hear the sauce station

Now watch a design fail. One worker searches the web. Another reads documents. A third writes the synthesis. The search worker comes back with good material. The document worker comes back with good material. The synthesis worker produces a report that is fluent, well shaped, and missing its sources. It cites nothing, or it cites the wrong things.

Your first instinct is to add a line to the synthesis worker's prompt telling it to remember to cite its sources, but it cannot cite what it never received.

A worker spawned by the coordinator starts empty. It does not inherit the coordinator's conversation, does not see what the other workers found, and does not remember what it did the last time it was invoked. It sees exactly one thing: the prompt the coordinator constructed for it.

So the fix sits upstream. Pass the complete prior findings into the synthesis worker's prompt, in a shape that keeps content and metadata apart, so the source URL, document name, page number and retrieval date still travel attached to the claim they belong to. Merge them into free prose on the way in and the attribution dissolves during aggregation, where no downstream instruction can rebuild it.

The re-search trap

The other tempting fix is to send the report worker back out to find a source for each claim. That is duplicated work, and worse than duplicated: it can attach a claim to a source that had nothing to do with it. You are not recovering the original mapping. You are inventing a new one that looks like it.

Open the drawer: how a worker actually gets spawned

Spawning is a tool, not a permission and not a persuasion. The coordinator spawns a subagent by calling Task, and it can only call Task if Task is present in its allowedTools.

coordinator_agent = AgentDefinition(
    allowed_tools=["Task", "get_customer"]
)

If a coordinator never delegates and grinds through everything itself, check that list first. Two wrong answers will be waiting for you. Rewriting the coordinator's system prompt to instruct it to delegate: a prompt cannot grant a tool the configuration withheld. Adding descriptions of the available subagents to that system prompt: a description tells the orchestration layer what a worker type is for, and it is not a spawn mechanism.

Each Task call produces an independent agent with its own context. The coordinator aggregates the results when the calls complete.

The stem describes a final step that produces something plausible but disconnected from what the earlier steps found. Then it asks for the root cause. The tempting wrong answer is always an instruction aimed at the last worker in the chain: tell it to cite sources, tell it to check for gaps, tell it to be careful. The right answer changes what that worker was handed, not what it was told.
A worker knows only what I wrote into its brief. If I did not put it in the prompt, it does not exist for that worker.

Calling the order badly

Here is the idea I want you carrying out of this chapter. A research system is asked about the impact of AI on the creative industries. The coordinator splits the job into subtasks about visual art: painting, illustration, image generation. Every worker executes cleanly. Every result is accurate and well sourced. The error log is empty. The final report is thorough on visual art and silent on music, literature and film.

Where is the bug?

Not in the search worker's query quality. Not in the document worker's filters. Not in the synthesis worker's gap detection. All three did their job well on the ground they were given. The bug is that the ground was cut too small. The coordinator's split never contained music. No amount of excellence downstream can cover a topic that was never assigned to anyone.

That is the chef calling the order badly. Five good cooks, one wrong plate.

So turn it into a habit you apply before you start reading options: when coverage is wrong but every worker executed cleanly, the fault sits in the division of the work, not in any one worker, and every option that adjusts a worker is a distractor.

The same responsibility explains a quieter kind of waste. Give two workers overlapping briefs and they investigate the same subtopics, spend tokens twice, and return no extra depth for it. The fix is to partition the space before delegating, handing out distinct subtopics or distinct source types. Deduplicating afterwards is repair work on a design fault.

The two-question diagnostic

Ask them in this order. Did the workers succeed? Is the output missing something it should have covered? If both answers are yes, you are looking at the split. You already know this. You just have not said it out loud under exam pressure yet.

Tell them the dish, not the knife strokes

The chef does not tell the fish station how to hold the knife. She tells it what has to be on the plate and how good it has to be. That is the difference between a worker brief that survives contact with reality and one that shatters on it.

An over-prescribed brief reads like a script. Search this phrase. Open the first five results. Summarise each in a hundred words. It works right up to the moment the third result is a paywall or the phrase returns nothing, and then the worker has no instruction covering the situation it is actually in.

A goal-oriented brief states what the finished work has to achieve and what bar it has to clear. Cover production, distribution and composition. At least three independent sources per claim. Now the worker decides how to get there, which is the same model-driven reasoning you already trust inside a single agent, applied one level up.

Two wrong answers cluster here. The first adds more steps to the script, which makes the rigidity worse. The second adds more workers, and this one deserves saying out loud: more agents running a bad script gives you more output produced by the same bad script.

You will see a coordinator producing shallow, checklist-shaped results. The options will offer you a longer procedure, extra workers, and a rewrite into goals plus quality criteria. Take the rewrite. Any option that adds volume to a design failing on shape is the wrong answer.

A prompt is a request. A tool list is a fact.

You have missed this one before, so I will put it bluntly. You do not tell a worker not to change files. You do not give it the ability to change files.

A worker's configuration has three levers and they do different kinds of work. Its name and description say what this worker type is for, so the orchestration layer can pick it. Its system prompt shapes how it behaves. Its tool list decides what it is physically able to touch. Only the third of those is a guarantee. The other two are requests with a non-zero failure rate.

Take the case that makes the difference visible. A document-analysis worker is handed a general URL-fetching tool and starts doing ad-hoc web searching, because it can. You can add a line to its prompt forbidding that. The line will hold most of the time, which is another way of saying it will fail some of the time. Replace the tool with one that loads documents and validates that the target is a document, and the wandering stops being discouraged and starts being impossible.

The same reasoning answers a question the exam enjoys: should every worker get the full tool set, for flexibility? No. Restricting tools per worker type is exactly how responsibilities stay separate. Widen every worker's access and you have deleted the separation, leaving prompts to hold the whole line on their own.

Least privilege does not mean starving a worker either. If a synthesis worker needs simple fact checks most of the time and real research occasionally, give it one narrow fact-checking tool for the common case and route the hard cases back through the coordinator. Giving it nothing and batching all verification to the end creates a blocking dependency, because later parts of the synthesis may need facts that were supposed to be verified earlier.

Everything at once, and the slowest plate sets the clock

Stations cook at the same time. That is the point of a brigade, and in your system it has an exact mechanical condition attached to it.

Multiple spawn calls emitted in a single coordinator response run in parallel. The same calls spread across separate turns run one after another. Same workers, same briefs, completely different wall clock. This detail reads like trivia and it decides questions.

Now the arithmetic, because it is the part people get backwards. When three investigations run side by side, you wait for the last one to finish. Elapsed time is the slowest of them, not the three added together. Run them one after another and you pay the sum, plus redundant fetching, because each sequential step often re-collects context the previous step already had.

The everyday shape of this is a request with several concerns folded inside it. A customer says they were billed twice, a discount was never applied, and they want an order cancelled. That is three investigations, not one conversation. Split it into three items, give each worker the shared customer context it needs, run them together, then synthesise one unified resolution. Handling them in sequence gives you a high tool-call count, repeated lookups of the same account, and a slower answer for the same work.

Watch the wording closely. If a question says the coordinator issues its spawn calls across several turns and then asks why the system is slow, the answer is not machine capacity and not worker count. It is that the calls were never in one response, so nothing ever ran in parallel.
Parallel means the calls go out in one response. Then I wait for the slowest one, not for all of them added up.

When to keep the pan yourself

The chef still plates the dish herself. Some jobs are not worth calling down the line, and knowing which ones is part of the same skill.

Delegation carries a fixed cost you have to price in. A worker starts empty. Everything it needs must be assembled into a prompt, sent out, worked on in a context you cannot see, and summarised back to you. If the coordinator already holds the context and the job is one small step, you have paid for a whole fresh start and a round trip to save nothing. Worse, you have introduced a place where detail can be lost on the way there and on the way back.

This is why the coordinator should choose which workers to invoke from what the request actually requires, rather than running the full pipeline every time. A simple lookup does not need a search worker, a document worker and a synthesis worker spinning up behind it. Deciding who runs is part of the coordinator's job in exactly the way deciding how the work splits is.

The other side of isolation

Isolation costs you a fresh start. Isolation is also a feature. When a phase produces a mountain of noisy output, sending it to a worker keeps that noise out of the main context and brings back a short summary instead. Same property, opposite use. Work out which one a question is about before you answer it, because the two look identical in the stem.

Two ways to plan the service

There are exactly two shapes of decomposition, and you choose between them on one property: do you know all the steps before you start?

When you do, use a fixed sequence, the pattern named prompt chaining. Each step's output feeds the next, the path is the same every time, and you get reproducibility out of it. A document pipeline running metadata extraction, then data extraction, then validation, then enrichment, then output is this shape. So is a review that always follows the same template.

When you do not, use an adaptive split, named dynamic adaptive decomposition, where each subtask is generated from what the previous step found. The pattern has a rhythm worth memorising: map the structure first, identify the high-impact areas, then build a prioritised plan that changes as dependencies surface.

Fourteen files in one pass

A pull request touches fourteen files. You send the whole thing to one review pass. What comes back is strange: detailed comments on some files, thin ones on others, obvious bugs missed, and the same pattern flagged as a problem in one file and quietly approved in another.

That last symptom is your diagnostic. One reviewer contradicting itself inside a single job is attention spread thin across too many items at once, and that has a name: attention dilution, not a shortage of knowledge or context space.

So split the work by attention rather than by size. Run a pass per file for local issues, then a separate pass across the relationships for the things only visible at the joins: inconsistent types, circular dependencies, data flowing between modules. Notice what this is. It is the same fixed-sequence shape you just met, applied to review.

And the trap, which will cost you a mark if you take it: a larger model with a bigger context window. It will not help. Window size governs how much text fits. It does not govern how evenly attention is spread across what fits. Those are two different problems and only one of them is yours.

Contradictory verdicts on the same pattern mean the attention was spread too thin, so I split the passes. A bigger window is a different problem.

The chef tastes it before it goes out

Synthesis is not necessarily one shot. When the combined answer comes back, the coordinator's job is to read it for gaps and decide whether it leaves the pass.

If there are gaps, the coordinator re-delegates: targeted queries aimed at the specific holes, then it re-invokes synthesis with the enriched findings, and repeats until coverage is good enough. Gap evaluation and the decision to stop belong to the coordinator, not the synthesis worker, because it is the only part of the system holding both the original request and the whole result.

Two things break this loop. Letting the synthesis worker go and fill its own gaps, which hands research capability to a worker whose scope does not include research. And running the loop with no definition of "sufficient", which means it has no way to decide it is finished.

Hold one distinction precisely here. Marking the report to show which conclusions are well supported and which are thin is the right move when a gap cannot be filled, say because a whole source category was unreachable. It is not a substitute for going back out while going back out is still possible. Shipping with a note saying further research is needed, when re-delegation was available, is a failure dressed up as honesty.

This arrives as "the synthesis output has coverage gaps, what should the architecture do". The options will include giving the synthesis worker its own search access, and shipping the report with a caveat attached. Both are wrong while re-delegation is still on the table.

The cook who made it cannot taste it straight

A model that has just generated something is carrying its own reasoning with it. It knows why it made each choice, and those justifications are sitting right there in its context. Ask it to review its own work and it re-reads its own case and agrees with it. That is anchoring, and it is structural.

Which kills three fixes that feel like they should work. Adding "review your work carefully before finishing" to the generation prompt keeps the same context and the same anchoring. Turning on extended thinking gives you more reasoning from the same anchored instance. Asking for a second pass later in the same conversation is that same instance reading its own justifications again and nodding.

Independence has a physical definition here: a separate request containing the artifact and the review criteria, and nothing at all from the generation conversation. Without the author's reasons in front of it, the reviewer judges what is actually on the plate. This two-stage shape has a name worth carrying: the evaluator-optimizer pattern, a generator followed by an independent critic.

Why this one keeps coming back

The same mechanism appears in the questions about multi-agent design and again in the questions about automated review inside a pipeline. Different costume, one idea: the reviewer must not be holding the generator's reasoning. If an option keeps them in one context, it is not independent, no matter how the option is worded.

Coming back to it tomorrow

Long work spans days. There are four moves, and one question picks between them: how much of yesterday's evidence is still true?

Resuming a named session brings back a specific prior conversation with its saved context. That is the right move when what you learned still holds. It carries one risk and it is the one they will test you on: a resumed session treats its saved tool results as current. If files changed overnight, it reasons over yesterday's contents as though nothing happened, confidently.

So when you resume after changes, name the changed files explicitly and ask for targeted re-analysis of just those. That beats trusting stale reads, and it beats re-exploring the whole codebase, which throws away a great deal of work that is still perfectly good.

Forking answers a different question. It creates independent branches from one shared baseline: both inherit the context up to the branch point, then diverge on their own. Use it when an expensive analysis is finished and you want to compare two approaches built on it. Evaluating them one after another in a single session lets the first evaluation bias the second. Opening two brand-new sessions instead makes each of them redo the expensive baseline from scratch.

And when the earlier evidence has genuinely gone stale, start clean and inject a structured summary of what was concluded. That carries the conclusions forward without dragging the dead evidence underneath them along too.

Rules that actually hold

By the end of this chapter you will be able to look at any safety rule in a design and say, in one breath, whether it is enforced or merely requested, and know exactly where the enforcing code has to sit.

A poster is not a rule

The poster and the turnstile

Two buildings. Both want to keep strangers out of the server room.

The first has a poster on the door: AUTHORISED PERSONNEL ONLY. Most people read it and turn around. Some are in a hurry. Some misread it. Some decide the rule was written for other people. The poster has no opinion about any of them, and it never finds out.

The second has a turnstile. No badge, no turn. It does not persuade. It does not care how urgent you are, or how good your reason is, or how many times you have been through before.

Both buildings have the same rule written down. Only one of them has a rule.

That is the whole chapter. Every safety idea on this paper is either a poster or a turnstile, and most of the marks are in telling them apart fast.

Start with the failure, because the failure is what makes this stick. You have a support agent with four tools: look up the customer, look up the order, issue a refund, hand off to a human. Your system prompt says identity is verified before anything else happens. It says it near the top, in capitals, with two worked examples underneath.

Now read a month of logs. The agent is taking the order number the customer typed into the chat and going straight to the order lookup, skipping identity entirely. In fifteen cases out of a hundred the record that comes back belongs to the wrong account, because nothing ever tied that order number to a verified customer. Sometimes a refund follows it onto a stranger's card.

Nothing is broken. The model is not malfunctioning. It read your instruction, weighed it against a conversation where the customer sounded rushed and had already handed over an order number, and did something reasonable. Your instruction is input. Input competes with other input.

The size of the gap

Prompt instructions land somewhere above ninety percent and below one hundred. That gap sounds academic until you write it in the units of the scenario: fifteen wrong accounts in a hundred lookups, with refunds behind some of them. Put a one-in-a-hundred error rate on money into any system you would sign your name to and it never ships. The model does not change that arithmetic. It just makes it easier to forget.

A prompt instruction is a strong bias, and a bias is not a guarantee.

Most of the time is not a design

Here is the line the whole paper is built on, and it is worth saying out loud before you meet it wearing a costume. Code that runs around a tool call is deterministic: it fires every time, whatever the model was thinking. A sentence in a prompt is probabilistic: it fires most of the time, and the misses cluster exactly where the conversation got complicated, which is exactly where the stakes were highest.

So the decision rule is short. If the rule is a preference, a house style, a tone, an ordering you would like, put it in the prompt. If breaking the rule has financial, legal or safety consequences, put it in code. Money, law, harm. Those three words are the trigger, and when one of them appears in a stem your hand should already be moving away from the wording answers.

Notice what this is not. It is not a claim that prompts are weak, or that you should stop writing careful instructions. It is a claim about where the guarantee lives. You keep the instruction, because a well-instructed agent runs into the gate far less often and the conversation stays sane. You add the gate, because it runs into it anyway.

The stem gives you telemetry, then a menu. One option strengthens the wording. One adds examples of the correct behaviour. One adds a whole classifier or routing layer. One puts a check in code at the moment of the call. When the scenario involves money, a policy limit, identity, deletion, or anything you cannot take back, the code check wins, and the two prompt options exist to catch people who read the situation as a wording problem. The examples option is the dangerous one, because examples genuinely are the right answer elsewhere on this paper. They are right for judgement and for format. They are never right for a guarantee.

Two places you can stand

Around every tool call there are exactly two places you can put your own code, and they do different jobs. Before the call, and after it.

Before the call, your code sees which tool the model wants and what arguments it wants to pass. It can do three things: let it through, change the arguments, or stop it. Stopping is the one you care about here. The nine-hundred-dollar refund never reaches the refund tool; the request goes to the escalation path instead. Same for the bulk deletion that would touch more than fifty records. The agent proposed it, your code declined it and sent it somewhere a human decides.

After the call, your code sees the result before the model does, and it can reshape it. The everyday use is the unglamorous one that saves you constantly. You are pulling from several tools you do not own: one returns Unix timestamps, one returns ISO dates, one returns numeric status codes. You normalise all of it in one place, after the call, before the model reads a single field.

The alternatives to that are all worse in ways worth naming, because they turn up as options. Wrapping each third-party tool in your own version scatters the work across as many wrappers as there are tools. Adding a normalise tool the agent is supposed to call after every retrieval buys you an extra model call and depends on the agent remembering. Describing the formats in the system prompt hands interpretation back to the model, which is the thing you were trying to stop doing.

What blocking actually looks like

Be precise about what happens when the gate says no, because you will be asked to separate prevention from detection. The call does not run. There is no tool result for a refund that never happened. What comes back to the model is the redirection: this went to escalation, here is why, carry on from there.

Look hard at the third branch, because it is the trap. Checking after the call feels like enforcement. It gives you a clean alert, a tidy audit trail, and the money back only if somebody chases it. A check that runs after the action is detection. Detection is not a rule.

The same shape covers ordering. The identity failure from the opening of this chapter is fixed this way: the order lookup is blocked until the customer lookup has returned a verified identifier. The code that is about to perform the sensitive step checks the precondition itself, rather than trusting that an earlier step happened, because trusting the sequence is precisely what failed. The agent claiming it verified the customer is not verification. The verified identifier coming back from the tool that does verification is.

Now guard against an option that looks close. A routing step that decides which tools an agent can see is about availability, not ordering. The support agent needs the order lookup on every case, so it stays available on every case, and it is still available in the wrong order. Availability and sequence are different problems, and they get paired on purpose.

Three layers, and each does something the others cannot

The prompt makes good behaviour likely. It is worth writing well, because an agent that has been told the limit attempts fewer things the gate has to refuse, and the customer gets a sensible explanation instead of a wall. What it cannot do is promise.

The code makes bad behaviour impossible at the point that matters. It is narrow by nature: it only knows about the calls it wraps and the values it can read. It cannot make the agent thoughtful, and it will never cover a case you did not think of.

The log makes what happened knowable afterwards. It changes nothing while it is happening. When an option offers monitoring, alerting, or a weekly review as the answer to a prevention question, that option is answering a different question, and it is answering it well, which is what makes it tempting.

You are rarely asked to build all three. You are asked which single layer belongs at the exact point where the scenario broke.

A limit the caller can set is not a limit

Make the dangerous action impossible without its preview

Now the pattern people miss, and the strongest single design idea in this chapter.

You have an action that removes a member from a workspace. You want a preview every time, without exception. The obvious design is one tool with a dry-run flag: call it with the flag on to see the impact, call it with the flag off to actually do it. The instructions say preview first.

The flag is a parameter, and the model fills in parameters. Nothing in the system requires that a preview call ever happened, so the first call of a conversation can be the real one, with the flag off, and it will look completely well-formed. This is not misbehaviour. This is the interface working exactly as specified, and the specification permits the thing you wanted to forbid.

Now the fix. Two tools. The first previews and returns the impact details plus a single-use confirmation token. The second performs the removal and takes that token as a required input. Without the token it does not run.

Nothing was made more likely. Something was made impossible. The token cannot be guessed or assumed, because it comes into existence only as the output of a preview call, and the removal tool checks it. Skipping the preview stops being a choice the model can make, in the way that skipping a step described in a prompt always is.

The alternatives look reasonable and each fails in its own way. A timing rule on your side, accepting a removal only if a preview happened moments earlier, is a guess about behaviour: slow turns, retries and a preview of some other member all break it. A confirmation step in the orchestration layer is more asking, with extra machinery holding it up. A stronger instruction with examples is the poster on the door.

Open the drawer: the two tools, named
  1. preview_remove_member: returns the impact details plus a single-use confirmation token.
  2. execute_remove_member: requires that token as a parameter, and refuses without it.

The phrase used for why this wins is worth carrying into the exam room verbatim: it makes execution without a prior preview architecturally impossible, because the token only exists after a preview call.

The three alternatives, and the one-line reason each is rejected:

  • Server-side timing heuristic: fragile to timing conditions.
  • Orchestration-layer confirmation prompt: requires extra infrastructure.
  • Prompt instruction plus examples: probabilistic, not guaranteed.

The design it replaces is the single tool carrying a dry_run boolean, which the agent bypasses by calling with the flag set to false.

One tool with a flag is a hope. Two tools bound by a token is a guarantee.

The same law, pointed at the output

Everything so far has been about actions. Turn the law around and point it at what comes back, and it is the same idea in different clothes.

You need JSON from the model. The poster version asks for it: reply with JSON only, matching this shape. Most of the time you get JSON. Sometimes you get a friendly sentence in front of it, or a code fence wrapped around it, or a trailing comma, and your parser falls over on a Tuesday afternoon.

The patch everyone reaches for first is a repair library that cleans up the malformed output after it arrives. The wrong shape is still being produced at the same rate; you have hired something to tidy up after it. That option turns up on the paper for exactly this reason, and it is wrong for the same reason the alert after the refund was wrong. It treats the symptom that the real fix removes at the source.

The turnstile version defines a tool whose input schema is the shape you want, then reads the data out of the tool call. The tool does not have to do anything at all. It exists so that the only way for the model to answer is in your shape. The whole class of syntax failure disappears, because the braces and commas are no longer something the model is typing out by hand and hoping to get right.

There is one more turn of the screw. Defining the tool does not mean it gets called: by default the model may still simply talk. So you say what must happen. You can require that some tool is called, which is what you want when a document could be an invoice, a receipt or a contract and you have a tool for each: you always get structured output, and the model still picks the right shape for what it is looking at. Or you can require that one named tool is called, which is what you want when a specific step has to run before anything else.

And the trap that rides along with it: if you leave the named tool forced on every turn, the follow-up steps can never run, because every turn is pushed back into the same tool. Force the first step, then relax.

Then the boundary, and this is where careful people lose a mark. A schema constrains shape, and shape is not meaning. A pipeline can produce perfectly valid, perfectly schema-shaped output claiming a total of one hundred and fifty when the line items sum to one hundred and forty-five. The schema had nothing to say about that and never will. The syntax class of error is dead and the meaning class of error is untouched, which is exactly what you should expect to see when parse failures drop to zero while reconciliation mismatches carry on at the old rate.

This is where defence in depth earns its place rather than being a slogan. The schema constrains the output at the moment it is generated. Your own validation, in your own code, checks the things no shape can express: that the numbers add up, that the start date precedes the end date, that the category is one your business actually recognises. Anyone who tells you the schema makes the code validator redundant has quietly swapped two different guarantees for each other.

One schema decision belongs here too, because it is the same principle aimed at honesty. Mark a field required and non-nullable only when the source always contains it. A required field with no data behind it puts pressure on the model to produce something, and something is what you get. Let it be empty and it will tell you it is empty. An honest gap is worth more than a confident invention, every time.

Chapter nine

Knowing when to stop

By the end of this chapter you will be able to look at any stuck agent and say in one breath whether it should hand over right now, ask one more question, or finish what it can and report the hole honestly.

Ninety seconds at the hospital door

A paramedic wheels a patient through the doors and gets about ninety seconds with the doctor taking over. She does not replay the drive. She says: this is who he is, this is what happened, this is what I found, this is what I did, this is what I gave him and when, and this is the thing I am worried about.

The doctor can act on that immediately. Nothing is re-asked. Nothing is re-tested. The ninety seconds work because they were compiled, not recorded.

Now picture the other version. She parks the trolley, hands over a two-hour audio file of the ambulance ride, and walks away. Everything is technically in there. The doctor has to go and find it while the patient waits. That is exactly what your agent does when it escalates by dumping the transcript.

Stopping well has two halves, and the exam tests both. One half is handing over to a person: knowing the moment, and compiling the ninety seconds. The other half is finishing partway and saying so honestly, because sometimes there is no person to hand to and part of the work simply cannot be done. Both halves fail in the same way when they are designed badly, and both fail because someone reached for a number instead of a judgement.

The naive rule that costs you the mark

A team is worried their agent gives up too early and floods the human queue. So they write a rule that sounds like discipline: do not escalate until you have made three genuine attempts to resolve the issue. Read it cold and it sounds responsible. Now watch it in production.

A customer's first message is "I want to speak to a manager." The agent, obedient, offers a replacement. Then a store credit. Then a callback. By the time it finally hands over, the customer has said the same sentence three times, the human inherits a furious person and a longer transcript, and the whole exchange has become the thing everyone hates about automated support. The rule did not measure how hard the case was. It measured how long the customer would put up with being ignored.

Second failure, same rule. A case arrives that the written policy does not cover. Three attempts will not conjure a policy. The agent burns three turns and arrives at exactly the position it was in on turn one, except now it has probably improvised an answer to fill the silence.

Third failure, same rule. A case the agent resolves cleanly on the first attempt, and should never have touched, because the action needed authority the agent does not have. Zero failed attempts. Counter never fires. Money moves.

So the instrument is wrong. A counter of failed attempts tells you how many times the agent swung. It tells you nothing about what it was swinging at. Judge by category and consequence, never by attempt count.

Where the counter is legitimate

Be precise here, because the exam is. "Cannot make meaningful progress after a reasonable number of attempts" is a real trigger. What is wrong is making a global attempt counter the gate for every escalation, so that it delays the triggers that should fire on the first turn. Repeated failure is one category among several. It is not the clock the others run on.

Five reasons to stop, and none of them is a counter

Learn these as categories, not as a list to recite. Each one describes a shape of situation, and you should be able to spot the shape when the question dresses it in different clothes.

  • Someone asks for a person. They said it. That is the whole test. Hand over now.
  • The action needs authority the agent does not have. A refund above the threshold, a bulk operation above the limit. Not a judgement call, a boundary.
  • The written rules do not cover the case. The policy is silent or ambiguous on this specific request.
  • An ambiguity you cannot resolve safely. A wrong call here is expensive and you have no way to settle it.
  • A state you cannot verify. The lookups keep failing, or the data you need to decide simply is not there.

The first one carries an ordering rule that the exam loves. When a customer explicitly asks for a human, you escalate immediately. You do not investigate first, you do not offer one quick fix first, you do not run a tool to see whether you could have handled it. The agent's own opinion about whether the case was easy is not relevant, because the customer already made the decision and the customer outranks the agent on this one.

There is one refinement worth holding, and it is not a loophole. "This is outrageous, I am extremely unhappy" is an expression of anger, not a request for a person. The right move there is to acknowledge it and put a real resolution on the table. If the customer then says "no, I want to talk to someone", that is the request, and you go immediately. First expression of dissatisfaction is not a demand for a manager. A repeated one is.

Not forbidden is not the same as safe

A customer asks you to match a competitor's advertised price. Your written policy covers price drops on your own site. It says nothing at all about other retailers. Two answers will look reasonable to a tired candidate, and both are wrong for the same reason.

The first is to apply the own-site rule by analogy, because the situations are similar. The second is to refuse, because the policy does not say you match competitors. One of those invents a policy in the customer's favour and the other invents a policy against them. Inventing is the error, and the direction of the invention is irrelevant. The absence of a rule is not permission and it is not prohibition. It is a missing decision, and a missing decision belongs to the person who is allowed to make it.

Now the harder shape, the one that wears a costume. The request is large, the situation is ambiguous, the consequences of getting it wrong are serious, and nothing in the written rules explicitly forbids it. The agent reasons its way to "I checked, nothing prohibits this", and proceeds. That reasoning is the failure. Not forbidden is not the same as safe. Run it through the two questions instead. Category: is the rule silent or ambiguous here? Consequence: how bad is a wrong call? Silent plus expensive is a handoff, every time, and it does not matter that the agent felt confident.

Silence in the policy is not a green light and it is not a red light; it is a decision nobody has made yet, so I stop and hand it to someone who can make it.

A customer lookup comes back with several matching records under the same name. This feels like ambiguity, and you have just learned that ambiguity is a trigger, so the reflex is to escalate. Do not.

This ambiguity is one question away from being resolved. The agent asks for another identifier: the email on the account, the order number, the phone number. That closes it. Escalating instead sends a human a piece of work that a single sentence would have finished, which is the same queue-flooding problem in a different coat.

And the far worse move is to guess. Picking the most recently active account, or simply taking the first result the tool returned, is a heuristic dressed as a decision. It works most of the time and then it refunds the wrong person's card. There is no version of this where a guess is acceptable, because the cost of being wrong lands on someone who was never part of the conversation.

So hold the boundary cleanly. Ambiguity you can close by asking, you close by asking. Ambiguity you cannot close, where a wrong call is expensive, you hand over. The word "ambiguous" in a question stem is not by itself an escalation signal, and the exam knows it.

If one more identifier would settle it, I ask; I only escalate the ambiguity that no question of mine can close.

Four signals that feel useful and are not

Every team that gets escalation wrong reaches for a proxy. It is always the same four, and each one fails for a reason worth knowing individually, because the exam will offer them to you as three of the four options.

Mood. Sentiment analysis on the customer's message, escalate above a threshold of negativity. Mood is not difficulty. A calm, polite paragraph can describe a case that needs a policy exception nobody has authority to grant. An all-caps tirade can be a mis-shipped item that a single tool call fixes. Route on mood and you will hand humans a queue of easy angry cases while the hard quiet ones stay stuck with the agent.

The model's own confidence. Have it rate itself out of ten and escalate below seven. This one is seductive because it sounds like self-awareness. It fails at the exact point you need it. The model can be confidently wrong, and uncalibrated self-report does not track actual difficulty. On the hard cases the agent is already incorrectly confident, which is the whole reason those cases go wrong, so the gate stays shut precisely where you needed it open.

A counter of failed attempts. Covered already, and worth repeating because it is the one that feels most like engineering. It measures the wrong thing and it delays the immediate triggers.

A trained classifier. A separate model, trained on historical tickets, predicting which requests need a person. Look at the size of that. Labelled data, a training pipeline, a deployment, a monitoring story, drift. All of it to answer a question the situation already answers out loud, and all of it built before anyone has tried writing the boundary down in the system prompt.

Which is the fix. When escalation calibration is bad, the proportionate first response is explicit criteria: state in the system prompt when to escalate and when to resolve autonomously, and attach a handful of examples aimed at exactly the cases the agent currently gets wrong. A small number of them, somewhere between two and six, all sitting on the genuinely ambiguous boundary. Ten or fifteen examples of obvious cases teach nothing, because the agent was never getting the obvious cases wrong.

What you actually hand over

Now the paramedic. The single most important fact about the human agent receiving your escalation is this: they do not have the conversation transcript. That is not a limitation you route around, it is the design assumption. Everything they know about this case is what your agent compiled into the handoff. If it is not in there, it does not exist.

Which means the test for a good handoff is behavioural, not stylistic. Can the person act on it, on its own, without asking the customer a single question that has already been answered? If they have to re-ask, the handoff failed, however well written it was.

Notice what is in there that people forget. What you did matters because otherwise the human offers the replacement the customer has already refused. Why you stopped matters because "the customer demanded a manager" and "the policy does not cover this" call for completely different opening sentences from the human. And what you advise matters because the agent has actually done the investigation, and throwing that judgement away is waste.

Three wrong answers cluster around this, and you should be able to name each one on sight. Giving human agents access to the raw transcript: they lack that access by design, and even granted it, they would have to re-read the whole conversation. Having the human re-run the agent's tools to rebuild context: duplicated work, slower resolution, and the agent's reasoning still lost. Escalating earlier so that less context has accumulated: this fixes nothing about the information loss and drags down the autonomous resolution rate at the same time. The problem was never how much context existed. It was that the context was never compiled.

The other way to stop: finish what you can and say so

Not every stop is a handoff to a person. In a multi-agent system there is often nobody to hand to, and part of the work genuinely cannot be completed: a source timed out, a database is unreachable, a document was never provided. Here the failure modes come in a matched pair, and they are opposites.

The first is fake success. A subagent hits an access failure, catches it, and returns an empty result marked successful. Downstream, the coordinator reads that as a finding. The agent then tells a customer they have no orders, which is a false statement produced by a system that believed it was working correctly. Nothing can recover from this, because from the outside it is indistinguishable from a true answer. Never suppress an error into a clean-looking empty result.

The second is the blank failure. One search times out, and the whole workflow terminates. Four of the five source categories came back fine and were thrown away because the fifth did not. Or, only slightly better, the subagent returns "search unavailable" after quietly retrying internally, which tells the coordinator nothing it can act on: not what failed, not what was tried, not what partial work survives, not what to try instead.

The right shape sits between them, and it is the paramedic again. Say what you verified. Say what you could not. Say what you recommend. Partial and honest beats a fake success and it beats a blank error, because it is the only one of the three that leaves the next actor able to make a decision.

One distinction sits inside this and gets tested on its own. A search that ran successfully and found nothing is not a failure. Zero results is a valid, informative finding, and the coordinator should treat it as one. A timeout on the same search is an access failure that needs a recovery decision. They look similar in a log and they demand opposite responses, so a system that collapses them into one status has thrown away the information that mattered.

Annotate the gap you cannot close, and only that one

Suppose a synthesis agent receives results for three of the five source categories it needed. The right behaviour is to complete the synthesis with the data that exists, and mark the output so that a reader can tell which conclusions are well supported and which are affected by the missing sources. Uncertainty gets propagated upward rather than hidden. It does not return an error because its input was incomplete, and it does not quietly proceed as though nothing were missing. The vocabulary for this is coverage annotation, and the behaviour it belongs to is called graceful degradation.

Here is the trap. Coverage annotation is for gaps you cannot close. It is not a substitute for going back and getting the data when going back would work.

The coordinator owns this. Its job when synthesis comes back with holes is to evaluate the output, send targeted queries back out to the search and analysis agents aimed at exactly those holes, and re-invoke synthesis with the enriched findings. That is the refinement loop, and gap detection belongs to the coordinator rather than to the synthesis agent. Shipping a report stamped "further research needed" when a re-delegation was available is a polite way of not doing the work, and the exam scores it as wrong.

Two guards on that loop. The synthesis agent does not go and search the internet itself to fill its own gaps, because that hands a narrow role a broad tool and breaks the separation the architecture was built on. And the loop needs a stated sufficiency criterion, because a coordinator with no definition of "enough" cannot decide when to stop refining, and you have simply traded a gap for an infinite loop.

I annotate the gap only after I have established that no amount of re-delegating would close it.
You will see a synthesis-with-holes scenario and four plausible-looking outputs. The distinguishing detail is almost always buried in the stem: whether the failed sources are still failing. If they can be retried, the answer is the coordinator re-delegating and re-running synthesis. If they genuinely cannot, the answer is annotated partial output. The two tempting wrong answers are erroring out because the input was incomplete, and letting the synthesis agent do its own searching.

An answer you can use

By the end of this chapter you can take any question about what comes back from the model and say in one breath whether it is a shape problem or a truth problem, then name the one move that fixes that kind.

Ask ten people and you get ten shapes

Everything so far has been about making the thing move. Now turn it around. Something comes back from the model, and a system downstream has to act on it. That system does not read prose. It reads fields.

The form and the blank sheet

Ask ten people to write their details on a blank sheet of paper. You get ten different shapes. One writes the date as the eleventh of March. One writes it the American way round. One writes "last Tuesday". One skips the date and gives you a middle name instead. Nobody did anything wrong. They never agreed on what you wanted.

Now hand those same ten people a printed form. Boxes, labels, one for day, one for month, one for year. Ten identical shapes come back, and your clerk can process all ten without reading a word.

Now look hard at what the form did not do. It did not check that the date in the date box is the person's actual birthday. A form can force a shape. A form cannot force a truth. Hold that sentence. A serious number of marks sit in that gap, and you have walked into it twice.

Two problems, and they need two machines. Getting the same shape every time is one. Getting values that are right is a different one. Every question in this stretch of the paper is quietly asking which of the two you are looking at.

Shape and truth are different problems, and the thing that fixes one does nothing at all for the other.

Stop telling it to be careful

Before the form, the instruction. Here is the naive design, and you have written it yourself. An automated review that catches comments lying about what the code does. So you write: check that comments are accurate.

Run it. It flags a comment for old-fashioned wording. It flags one where the function was renamed. It flags a terse but perfectly correct one. Next run, same file, it flags none of them. You did not get a review. You got a mood.

Look again. Every load-bearing word is an adjective. "Accurate" is a judgement you handed over without defining, so two careful people would disagree, and two runs disagree. Write it as something a person could apply: flag a comment only when the behaviour it claims contradicts what the code does. Hold that against one comment and you get the same answer twice.

Two more moves belong in the same breath. Say what to skip as clearly as what to catch: report bugs and security, skip minor style and local convention. Half of a precision problem is the model reporting things you never asked for. And define your severity words. "Rate severity appropriately" gets you four meanings of high in one run. Define each level with a code example and the classification stops wandering.

The humble-sounding trap

"Only report findings you are highly confident about" feels like the responsible answer. It is the same failure in a modest coat. Confidence is another adjective, and one the model judges badly. Specific criteria beat a request for restraint, every time.

A noisy review tool, four instructions to choose between. Two will be general appeals: be conservative, report only what you are sure of. One builds infrastructure you do not need yet. One names the exact condition under which a finding is legitimate. Take that one.

What a wrong flag actually costs

Now the consequence, because this is the part people underprice. Four categories. Style is wrong about half the time. Documentation, near enough the same. Performance, under one time in five. Security, about one in twelve, which is genuinely good.

Watch the humans. They do not score you category by category. They learn one thing: this bot is usually wrong. Then they close the security findings unread, because those arrive in the same list as the noise. Your best category is destroyed by your worst two.

So the fix is surgical. Switch the two noisy categories off today. Keep security and performance running, because they earn trust every day they stay on. Rewrite the criteria for the disabled two away from the critical path, and turn them back on when ready.

Know the three wrong answers by name. A confidence score beside each finding leaves every finding on the screen. Adding examples across all four categories over coming weeks is the right direction on the wrong clock. Reducing strictness uniformly damages the category already at one in twelve.

Two small levers before you move on

Two more things live in the prompt rather than in the schema, and each is a single move. First, when the task genuinely has steps that build on one another, telling it to think step by step before answering raises accuracy: arithmetic over several stages, an analysis that runs in phases, a comparison across a list of items, a transformation applied in order. Do not attach it to a one-step task like translating a sentence. There are no steps to lay out, so it buys nothing.

Second, when every reply opens with the same cheerful filler, you already know the three wrong answers, because they are the three that get offered. Turn the randomness setting down, which governs variety and not that particular phrase. Strip the greeting off afterwards, which is a patch on the output. Add a line to the standing instructions asking it to stop, which is a request. The move that works is to hand over the opening words of the reply yourself and let it continue from where you left off. Same principle as the form: you supplied the shape instead of asking for it.

Now the form itself

You asked politely for JSON. You wrote "respond only with valid JSON matching this schema" and you meant it. What came back, some of the time, was a friendly sentence, then a code fence, then JSON with a trailing comma. So somebody bolted a repair library on downstream.

That is a blank sheet with a polite request written across the top. Here is the form. Define a tool whose input schema is exactly the structure you want back, and read your data out of the tool call. The tool does not have to do anything. It exists so that what comes back is shaped.

That one move deletes a whole class of problem: missing braces, trailing commas, a number arriving as a string, a chatty preamble, a fence round the payload. Deleted, not reduced. The repair library is now something you maintain for a problem you no longer have.

Boxes that let it say nothing

Now design the form so it does not force lies. This is subtle and it is worth marks.

Mark a field required only when the information is genuinely always in the source. A required field with nothing to fill it is pressure. The model has to produce something, and what it produces will look exactly like a purchase order number and appear nowhere in the document. That is a fabrication, and it validates perfectly. "Make every field required so the output is complete" buys completeness by invention.

So build the escape hatches in deliberately. Value may be absent: keep it out of the required list and let it be null, so "not found" is something the model can say. Categories known but new ones coming: end the allowed list with an "other" value and a companion detail field. The model may not be able to tell: add "unclear" to the allowed values, because an honest unclear beats a confident wrong category. And carry a confidence number beside each field, not one for the whole document.

One last piece, and it belongs to the prompt rather than the schema. Real documents are messy: dates in four formats, currencies with and without symbols, "about two kilos" where you wanted a number. The schema constrains the box and says nothing about how a messy input maps into it. Put the normalisation rules in the prompt, beside the strict schema.

Check the meaning, then say exactly what was wrong

The form arrives, well-shaped. Your code runs next. Validation is your code's job and it happens after the response lands, never as something you assume.

Two layers, and people build only the first. The structural layer checks types, required fields and allowed values: deterministic and cheap. A retry that carries three things

When a check fails you go back to the model, and this is where it gets built wrong in a way that should feel familiar. A retry saying only "that was wrong, try again" is the same useless thing as a courier who comes back and says only "failed". You handed over no information, so you get a fresh guess.

A useful retry carries three things and you should be able to say them in your sleep. The original document. The extraction that came back and failed. And the specific error, named: total is 150 but the line items sum to 145, re-check the values. Your validator already produced that sentence. Use it as the feedback.

A retry is worth sending only if it carries the source, the thing that came back wrong, and the sentence naming what was wrong with it.

When no amount of asking again will help

Now the limit, which is the more interesting half. Retrying with feedback fixes errors the model can correct from what is in front of it: a date in the wrong format, a value in the neighbouring field, an arithmetic slip it can re-derive from the line items you gave it.

It fixes nothing when the information is not in the source. Forty documents fail on a purchase order number. Somebody raises the ceiling from three attempts to ten. Spend triples, then failures start disappearing, which looks like progress until you check and find plausible purchase order numbers that exist nowhere on those pages. You did not extract anything. You applied pressure to a required box until something filled it.

Same when the answer lives in a second document you never supplied. No wording conjures a missing file. So the fix is never in the loop, it is in the design: make the field optional so nothing is an expressible answer, and let the pipeline treat "not found" as a legitimate result. Raising the ceiling and sharpening the feedback are both the wrong layer.

One habit while you are in there. Record on each finding which construct actually triggered it. Then when developers dismiss things, group the dismissals by that construct and see which pattern generates your noise. Across retries the same field tells you whether you have a systematic weakness or forty separate accidents. A free-text box asking why they dismissed it gives you prose that never aggregates.

When you should stop and ask, and when you must not

You have fallen between these two, so let me set them side by side.

First move: have it interview you. You ask for a caching layer in an area you have not worked in. It generates and misses invalidation. You fix that, it misses stale reads. The misconception waiting for you is that interviewing wastes a turn. One interview turn replaces several correction rounds, and correction rounds interfere with each other.

Second move, the opposite one. Sometimes there is nobody to ask, or asking stalls a run that has to finish. Do not fire four clarifying questions at a vague request: thirty-five to forty per cent of people abandon rather than answer, and bundling all four into one tidy message demands the same effort. What kills you is the third thing, the one that looks like the second: quietly picking a default and saying nothing. The output is wrong in a way nobody can see. A stated assumption is correctable. A hidden one is a bug in a good suit. This is not only about people: a synthesis agent should not stop and ask its coordinator about every gap either. It proceeds, and it says what it assumed.

The clarification options look almost identical. One asks several questions, one bundles them into a single polite message, one proceeds silently on defaults, one proceeds with the assumptions written down. The bundled version is the tempting wrong answer because it feels considerate. It still puts the work back on the person.

The cheap lane, for work nobody is waiting on

A short one, and nearly free marks if you hold two constraints in your head.

There is a second way to submit work: bundle it and send it off. It costs half. It can take up to a full day. Usually it is quicker, and nothing promises it will be. That is the trade. Overnight technical-debt reports, weekly security audits, nightly test generation, ten thousand documents in bulk: take the discount. Anything a developer or a pipeline is blocked on: never. "Usually about an hour" is not a commitment you can build on.

So the first thing it cannot do is promise you a time, which means you budget the worst case. Results needed within thirty hours means submitting at least twenty-four hours before, leaving a six-hour window, so a recurring pipeline splits into four-hour submission windows. Planning around typical speed works until the one bundle that takes twenty-three hours takes your deadline with it.

The second thing it cannot do is hold a conversation inside one request. Each item is one shot: it goes in, one response comes back. If that response says the model wants a tool run, the item finishes there. Your code cannot run the tool and hand the result back inside the same item. So a review that fetches related files mid-loop cannot live in this lane.

Two passes are not the same as two opinions

Last piece. First a structural habit. When one prompt is doing several jobs at once, the output goes shallow across all of them. Break it into a chain of focused steps where each step's output feeds the next: identify the issues, then, separately, generate fixes for the issues you identified. Reach for this when the structure is predictable and you know the steps up front.

Now the sharper point. You generate some code, then add "review your own work carefully" to the same prompt, in the same conversation. Bugs keep reaching production anyway. That conversation still holds every justification it produced while writing the code. Asked to review, it re-reads its own reasoning and agrees with it. The blind spot that produced the bug sits right there, confirming itself.

More deliberate thinking does not fix this, because it is more reasoning from the same anchored position. A second pass in the same conversation does not fix it either, for the same reason. What fixes it is a fresh request holding only the artifact and the review criteria, and none of the conversation that produced it.

And if you want to spend human attention well on top of that, have the reviewing pass report its confidence beside each finding, so high-confidence findings post themselves and low-confidence ones go to a person. That is routing on a signal, which is different from asking the model to filter itself into silence.

A second pass in the same conversation is the same opinion twice; independence means the reviewer never saw the reasoning that produced the thing.

Chapter eleven

What survives the next turn

By the end of this chapter you can look at any long-running job and say, without hesitating, which pieces have to survive to the next turn and where each one has to live so that it does.

Before anything else: you already own this room

Nine out of nine. On your last paper, every question in this area came to you and every one of them went down. While other parts of the paper wobbled, this one did not move. So treat this chapter differently from the ones around it. You are not rebuilding anything here. You are walking round the thing you already built and checking the joins.

The reason it is still worth an hour is that this material almost never arrives wearing its own clothes. It turns up as a cost problem, as a support agent that forgot an amount, as a research report that cannot cite itself, as a pipeline that died at hour four. If you can see the same shape underneath all of those, you will pick them off.

The desk and the wall above it

Think of a desk. Everything you are working on right now has to fit on it: the brief, the printouts, the results that keep arriving from other people, the note you scribbled an hour ago.

A bigger desk does not make you a better worker. Give someone a desk twice the size and they will still not notice the note that got buried in the middle of the pile. They read what is right in front of them and what is on top.

So what does a careful person actually do? They pin the few things that must not be lost to the wall above the desk. The allergy. The agreed price. The order number. Those stay in view no matter how deep the pile gets, because they never went into the pile at all.

What is actually sitting on the desk

Four things occupy the window on every single call: the system prompt, the tool definitions, the whole message history, and every tool result that has ever come back. That is the complete list. Nothing is held for you between calls. Each request carries the lot again.

Two of those four are a fixed cost. The standing instructions and the tool menu are the same size on turn two and on turn fifty. The other two only grow. That single fact explains a question you will meet in some costume or other: a conversation past fifty turns gets slower and more expensive, and the reason is not that the answers got longer or that a database got slow. It is that you resend the whole desk every time.

Every call I send the entire desk again, and only two of the four piles stay the same size.

Room is not attention

Here is the idea the whole chapter hangs off. Space on the desk and attention paid to what is on it are two different quantities, and only one of them is fixed by the size of the window.

The start of a long input gets read properly. The end gets read properly. The middle is where things go missing. This is not a bug you can configure away, and it is not a capacity problem, so buying a bigger window does not touch it. The version you will meet on the paper: a synthesis step takes seventy-five thousand tokens of aggregated input, uses the first fifteen thousand and the last ten thousand well, and quietly loses the findings sitting in the fifty thousand in between.

Three moves fix it. Put a key-findings summary at the very top, where reading is reliable. Break the body with explicit section headings so the middle can be found rather than waded through. Put the thing you want acted on at the end. And there is a fourth move that beats all three when it is available: stop the volume at source. Make the upstream workers hand back key facts, quotes, citations and relevance scores instead of full page content and their own reasoning traces.

The three tempting wrong turns

Squeezing everything down to fit is the first, and it is quietly circular: the compression may destroy the exact finding you were protecting. Rotating which worker's output goes first is the second, and it changes nothing, because the pattern is about position, not about whose turn it is. Adding a middle agent to summarise before synthesis is the third and the most respectable-looking: it adds delay and one more thing that can break, when the verbosity should have been fixed where it was produced.

The stem gives you token counts and tells you which stretch was used well and which was dropped. The tempting answer is the bigger model with the bigger window, because all those numbers look like a capacity story. It is not one. If the option talks about size, it is answering a different question.

Results pile up, and most of each result is rubbish

An order lookup can hand back forty or more fields when five of them matter to the task in hand. Those forty fields do not evaporate after the turn that needed them. They sit in the history and get resent on every later call, paid for every time, and they crowd the attention of every decision that follows.

So cut results down as they arrive, before they enter the record at all. A hook that fires after a tool returns is the place to do it: it takes the result, keeps the fields the work needs, and drops the rest. What the model sees is the trimmed version. What accumulates is the trimmed version.

Three plausible non-fixes get offered for this. Telling the model in the system prompt to ignore the irrelevant fields does not help, because the tokens have already been spent and the attention cost is already paid. Compressing the conversation harder does not help either, because the fat results keep arriving. And a bigger window with a larger model raises the bill without reducing the noise by one field.

Open the drawer: the exact hook and the exact shape

The hook that fires after a tool returns is PostToolUse. Registered against a specific tool, it intercepts the result and returns a reduced version before the model ever sees it.

# PostToolUse hook: keep only the fields the task needs
@hook("PostToolUse", tool="lookup_order")
def trim_order_fields(result):
    return {
        "order_id": result["order_id"],
        "status": result["status"],
        "total": result["total"],
        "items": result["items"],
        "return_eligible": result["return_eligible"]
    }

The same hook has a second documented job, and it is worth knowing so you do not mis-slot it. When several tools you cannot modify return the same thing in different shapes, Unix timestamps from one, ISO 8601 from another, numeric status codes from a third, this is the one place to normalise them centrally. Not a wrapper per tool, not a normalize_data tool the model has to remember to call, and not a paragraph in the system prompt explaining the formats.

The hook that fires before a tool runs is the other one, and it is for blocking things, not for tidying results.

Squeezing history loses the exact things first

Compression does not degrade evenly. It eats precision before it eats topic. Numbers, dates and amounts are the first casualties, because they are the parts a summariser has the least reason to keep. The customer said 'the fifteen percent discount I mentioned'. Twenty turns later the record says 'promotional pricing was discussed': the topic survived, the fact did not.

This is the hinge of the whole chapter: because precision dies first under compression, the precise things must never enter the compressor at all, not even carefully, and not under a better instruction.

The wall above the desk: a facts block that is never squeezed

Pull the transactional facts out into their own block. Customer ID, order ID, amount, the issue, the current status. That block lives outside the summarised history, gets updated the moment a new fact appears, and is included in every prompt regardless of what is happening to the rest of the record. It is the wall above the desk.

The important word there is canonical. The status of the case is a thing you write down and update, not a thing you work out by re-reading the conversation. When a session is juggling several issues at once, the same discipline applies to each of them: the order IDs, the amounts and the statuses go into a separate structured layer that you maintain, and the conversation stays a conversation.

  • Raise the threshold at which history gets compressed. This defers the loss by a few turns. The facts still go through the compressor eventually.
  • Rewrite the summarising instruction to preserve numbers and dates. This now depends on a summariser being perfect on every pass, forever. One imperfect pass and the amount is gone.
  • Move the history to external storage and retrieve pieces on demand. Heavy machinery for facts that live and die inside a single session. Retrieval is the right answer to a different question, and you will meet that question shortly.

Windows, and what a window quietly drops

The simplest way to keep a conversation inside budget is to keep only the last few exchanges and drop the rest. It works right up until the moment something said early turns out to matter late. The allergy was mentioned in the first two minutes. The window has long since dropped it.

So the shape you want is a hybrid, in three parts. Recent exchanges stay word for word, because the current turn needs them intact. Older exchanges become a running compressed summary, because their information density is low. And the facts block is never dropped by either mechanism. That third part is what makes the first two safe.

Recent stays verbatim, old gets compressed, and the facts block sits outside both and never moves.

When summarising is the wrong tool entirely

Now the case where none of the above is the answer. Months of conversation across many sessions, eighty-five thousand tokens of it, and somebody asks what you concluded about one particular thing back in the spring. That is not a compression problem. It is a finding problem.

Progressive summarisation is precisely wrong here: it takes the specific quoted conclusion you want and turns it into an abstraction, which is the one transformation that guarantees you cannot recover it. A rolling window is worse, because it threw the early sessions out. And marking conclusions with tags as you go does not help either, since the difficulty at that scale is retrieving the right exchange, not labelling it.

What works is retrieval over the full history: embeddings across everything that was said, pulling back the specific past exchanges that are relevant to the question being asked, on demand. Nothing was compressed, so nothing was lost, and only the relevant few exchanges come back into the window.

The word that decides this one is 'specific'. When the stem asks to recall a particular conclusion, a particular quote, a particular decision from a long time ago, retrieval is the answer and every compression option is wrong. When the stem asks to keep a live session coherent and affordable, compression plus a pinned facts block is the answer and retrieval is over-engineering. Same family, opposite answers, and the tell is one word.

Coming back to something old

Old conclusions usually survive. Old evidence usually does not. Hold those apart and a whole cluster of questions becomes easy.

If the prior context is mostly still valid, resume the named session. If you resume after things have changed, say exactly which files changed so it re-checks those and only those, rather than trusting stale results or starting the whole exploration again. And when the evidence really is stale, because a lot changed, or a lot of time passed, or the session had already started to degrade, do not resume. Start fresh and inject a structured summary of what was found. That carries the conclusions forward without dragging the stale evidence with them.

The failure this prevents is subtle and worth naming: a resumed session reasons over old tool results as though they were current. It is not confused. It is confidently working from a photograph of a room that has since been rearranged.

When news arrives in the middle of a live conversation

Related, and frequently muddled. Something happens outside the conversation while the conversation is running: the package ships, the payment clears. That update has to get in. There is exactly one clean way to do it, and two attractive wrong ways.

The clean way is to attach it to the front of the next real user message as context, so the model reads 'your package has shipped' and then reads what the person actually said. The first wrong way is to rewrite the standing instructions, which means rebuilding the whole session for a piece of transient news. The second is to send a standalone user message that nobody actually sent, which breaks the natural flow of the dialogue. Both are surgery where a prefix would have done the job.

The notebook, and what survives a crash

The confidence number, and where you look for what it hides

Two ideas, tightly coupled. Confidence is reported per field, not per document, because a document is not right or wrong as a unit: the date can be certain and the total a guess in the same extraction. And a raw self-reported confidence number is worth nothing until it has been calibrated against a set of examples you labelled by hand. Uncalibrated, it is a number the model made up about itself.

Once it is calibrated, route on it. High confidence in a segment whose accuracy you have actually measured goes through automatically. Low confidence goes to a person, and so does anything where the source itself is ambiguous or contradicts itself, regardless of what the confidence number says.

Then the trap, and it is a good one. An overall accuracy of ninety-seven percent can sit comfortably on top of one rare document type failing forty percent of the time. The average absorbed it. So before you reduce human review, break the accuracy down by document type and by field, and make every segment clear the bar on its own. Then keep auditing a random sample stratified across document types and field categories, and that sample must include the high-confidence stream you already automated. That stream is exactly where a brand new failure mode grows without anybody seeing it.

Why 'random' is not enough

A flat ten percent sampled at random spends most of your reviewer's day on the easy, common documents and barely touches the rare type that is quietly failing. Stratified means you deliberately take from every segment, including the small ones, so the rare failure has a chance of showing up in your sample instead of showing up in a customer complaint.

A claim and its source travel together

Last piece, and it is the facts block wearing different clothes. Attribution dies during compression for the same reason amounts die: it is detail, and detail is what gets dropped. So a claim is never stored as a sentence. It is stored as a record: the claim, the source name, the source link, the publication date, a confidence, and where in the source it came from. Re-searching at the end to rebuild the attributions is worse, because it is expensive and it can confidently bolt a claim onto the wrong source.

When two good sources disagree

Do not pick. Finish the analysis carrying both values, mark the disagreement explicitly with who said what, and hand the reconciliation upward to the coordinator or to a person. Choosing by heuristic takes a decision that sits above the worker's remit. Stopping to ask before finishing the analysis blocks the pipeline for no reason: you can complete the work and flag the conflict in the same pass.

And carry the dates, because most apparent contradictions are not contradictions. One source says ten percent, another says fifteen: a conflict. Add the dates and it reads as growth over a year. A pipeline that flags every numeric disagreement without dates spends its life reporting ordinary change as inconsistency. The dates are what tell a conflict apart from a trend.

The lock is the only thing here that refuses

Permission rules come in three kinds: allow, ask and deny. They are checked in a fixed order and the first match ends the walk. Deny first, then ask, then allow. If nothing matches, the active mode decides.

Follow that to its consequence. Deny is checked first, so a deny is final. Denied at one level, allowed at none. No combination of permissive rules anywhere else can undo it. That is the point of a lock: one that can be talked out of is not a lock.

A rule takes one of two shapes. A bare tool name removes the tool from Claude's context, so it never sees the tool exists. A scoped pattern leaves it available and blocks only matching calls.

Now the one that will cost you a mark. Your settings file holds two kinds of content and they behave differently. Ordinary keys are decided by precedence: the same key in two scopes means the higher scope replaces the lower and exactly one value survives. Permission rules do not do that. They merge. Every scope's allow, ask and deny lists are pooled into one set, and that set is walked in the fixed order.

Work the example. Project settings say allow for a shell tool and set the model to one value. Your user settings say deny for one shape of that shell command and set the model to another. Under pure precedence the project wins twice and your deny evaporates. It does not. The model setting resolves by precedence, so the project takes that one. Both permission rules sit in the pool, deny is checked first, and your deny bites.

Running the workshop with nobody in it

In a pipeline there is nobody to answer a prompt. Without the flag that makes the run non-interactive, the process waits for input that is never coming and the build hangs until something kills it. Three of the four options on that question will not exist: a batch flag, a convincingly headless environment variable, an empty input stream. Never pick a flag you have not seen with your own eyes.

The second flag is the one people miss. Making a run non-interactive does not stop it loading everything on the machine: memory files, procedures, hooks, plugins, connected servers, remembered state. So a pipeline quietly picks up somebody's personal convention and behaves differently from the same command on your laptop. A separate flag skips all of that local discovery, and that is what a reproducible run wants.

If a pipeline is going to act on the result, prose is useless to it. The same instinct, one level up. You are dropped into an unfamiliar codebase and asked to explain how refunds work, and the tempting move is to read everything first. Watch it fail. The correct loop is small and repeats. Search the contents for an entry point. Read those files. Search for who uses what you found. Read those consumers and follow their imports. Repeat. Every read is paid for by something a search told you, and your context ends up holding only the parts of the system genuinely on the path.

First find every name the thing is exported under: the original, plus every wrapper and every re-export alias, usually by searching the file that does the wrapping or the index file that does the re-exporting. Then search for each of those names on its own, and combine what comes back. Searching only the original name leaves every wrapper's callers outside the search entirely, and the miss stays invisible until one of those callers breaks.

  1. The loop carries on because the model asked for a tool and it ends because the model ended its turn, and I never decide that from the wording of a reply or from a counter I set myself.
  2. My code runs every tool, appends the result into the conversation and sends the whole thing up again, and the model chooses what to reach for next by reading what is in front of it rather than following a sequence I hard-coded.
  3. Everything between workers goes through the coordinator, and a worker begins with nothing in its head except what I wrote into its prompt.
  4. When every worker returned successfully and whole parts of the answer are still missing, the fault is in how I divided the work, not in the workers.
  5. If a step must always happen, it lives in code as a gate before the call runs, because a sentence in a prompt is a request and requests get declined.

Tools, and what they say when they break

  1. The model chooses a tool by reading its description, so when it chooses wrong I rewrite the description before I build anything new.
  2. A description that works says what the tool does, what input it takes and in what format, when to use it rather than the one sitting beside it, and when not to use it at all.
  3. Zero results is a success with an empty set and a lookup that could not run is an error with a category on it, and confusing those two makes the agent either tell a customer something false or retry an answer that will never change.
  4. I hand over to a person the moment they ask for one, or when policy is silent on the case, or when repeated attempts have got nowhere, or when a wrong call would do real harm, and never because someone sounded upset or because the model rated its own confidence low.

The workshop

  1. Every instruction file found on the way down is joined together, so a file deeper in the tree adds to the one above it and never replaces it.
  2. A convention that three teammates out of four follow is sitting in somebody's personal file and has to be moved into the project.
  3. Path-matched rule files apply a convention wherever the matching files happen to live, a skill is something invoked on demand, and the always-loaded instruction file is for standards that apply everywhere.
  4. In an automated pipeline the assistant needs -p or it waits for a human forever, and the shape of what it prints comes from the output-format and schema flags, not from asking for JSON in prose.

Words, examples, and output you can parse

  1. When prose instructions have already failed, more prose will not rescue them, so I show two to four examples aimed exactly at the cases it gets wrong, each one showing why that action was chosen over the plausible alternative, because a handful aimed at the genuinely confusing cases beats ten or fifteen of the obvious ones.
  2. To get output my code can parse I define a tool whose input schema is the shape I want and read the structure out of the tool call, and that tool does not have to do anything at all.
  3. A schema guarantees the shape and promises nothing about whether the values are true, so line items that fail to sum to the total are exactly what I should still expect, and that is a job for code, not for a tighter schema.
  4. Retrying with the specific error fixes what the model can re-derive from what it was given, and never fixes information that was simply not in the document.

What survives the turn

  1. Nothing is held for me between calls, so every request carries the entire conversation up again, and that is why cost and delay climb with the turn count.
  2. A bigger container does not make it notice more, so when the reading comes back uneven I cut the work into smaller passes and put what matters at the top and at the end.
  3. The numbers that must not move get pinned into a facts block that sits outside the summarised history, and a verbose tool result gets trimmed on the way in rather than apologised for later.

Part two. How to spend two minutes.

Knowing the material and scoring on the material are two different skills, and the second one is worth several marks to you on the day. Five habits. None of them requires you to know anything you do not already know.

Read the situation once, slowly, and then leave it alone

Each scenario holds about fifteen questions before you move to the next one. So the minute you spend reading that opening scenario carefully is not a minute spent on question one. It is spread across fifteen questions and costs you four seconds each. That is the cheapest minute on the whole paper.

Read it slowly, once. Fix the world in your head: what is being built, which tools exist and what they are called, what the numbers are. Then do not keep going back to it. Later questions in the block will add to that world, and what they add is what you read next. The scenario itself does not change.

Decide what the question wants before your eyes go down the list

There are two shapes and on the page they look identical. One asks what is wrong. The other asks what would fix it. This is where you have been losing marks you did not need to lose.

Here is why it bites. The wrong answers to a diagnosis question are very often perfect descriptions of the correct repair. The wrong answers to a repair question are very often accurate diagnoses. Both read as true. You look at the option, you check it against what you know, it survives the check, and you pick it. It was never eligible. It answered the other question.

So say the question back to yourself in three words before you look at anything. What is broken. Or what do I change. Then only options of that kind are candidates, and two of the four usually stop being candidates before you have evaluated either of them on its merits.

The wording tells you which. What is the most likely root cause is a diagnosis, and every fix-shaped option in it is out, however good the fix would be. What change would most effectively address this is a repair, and every accurate description of the fault is out, however accurate it is. In both cases the tempting wrong answer is a completely true sentence. That is what makes it work on you.

When two survive, take the mechanism over the direction

You will regularly get down to two and both will look right. There is a reliable tiebreak. One of them names a mechanism and the other names a direction of travel. Improve the tool descriptions against state what each tool accepts, when to use it instead of its neighbour, and when not to use it at all. Handle errors better against return the failure type, what was attempted, what partial results exist and what could be tried instead. Take the specific one. This paper is written to find out whether you know the mechanism or only the slogan.

One guard on that. Specific is not the same as impressive. An option can name a precise mechanism and still be the over-build: a classifier, a trained model, an extra service, standing in front of a cheap repair at the source that nobody tried. Precision decides between two fixes of the same size. It does not license the bigger one.

Your hesitation is information

An option arrives sounding authoritative. It names a flag. It has a number in it. It is written with more confidence than the three around it. And something in you pauses for half a second.

That pause is not nerves. That is a piece of knowledge trying to reach you before your reading brain talks over it. It has two usual causes: either the thing named does not exist, or it exists and does something else. Both are worth ten seconds of your time. What you must not do is push past your own hesitation because you want the question finished. You already know this. You just have not said it out loud yet, and the hesitation was you starting to.

Two minutes each, and never a blank

The platform will not let you advance without an answer, so there is nothing to come back to and nothing to protect. A wrong answer costs you the mark and not a thing more. Which makes the arithmetic simple: four minutes spent on question nine were taken from question fifty-two, and fifty-two might have been one you could have answered in forty seconds. Eliminate what you can, pick the best survivor, move. If you are still circling at ninety seconds, choose and go.

Now watch the opening rather than the end, because the record says the end is not your problem.
