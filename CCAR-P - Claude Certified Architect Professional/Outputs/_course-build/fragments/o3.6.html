Field technicians use an assistant built on 8,000 troubleshooting articles. One asks why ERR_5521 happens after an upgrade. Search returns five articles about upgrade errors, each on topic and none mentioning ERR_5521. The one article that explains the code ranks far down, because every upgrade article sits in the same tight cluster of meaning and the code itself carries almost none.

On queries that mix plain description with an exact code, the dense search finds the right article 34% of the time. With a second search that matches exact terms, and the two rankings fused, an invented test of the same queries reaches 89%, a gain of 55 points. The same assistant also gets questions that no search over articles can answer.

Pick the mechanism from the data shape and the query pattern§3.11

Choose on two axes: what the data is, and what the query does. A single mechanism across a mixed corpus is wrong for most of its query types. "RAG means a vector database" is a habit, and the vector search is one mechanism among several.

Dense retrieval is strong on paraphrase and conceptual similarity, and weak on exact identifiers, codes, part numbers and rare proper nouns. A rare identifier carries almost no distributional meaning, so its vector is near-noise. Lexical retrieval is the opposite: strong on exact terms and identifiers, weak on paraphrase.

Hybrid retrieval earns its place when the corpus and the query mix contain both prose and exact identifiers: run both searches and combine them with fused ranking, one ordering built from the two. Where the corpus is pure narrative with no identifiers, queried in natural language, use dense only. A lexical index and a fusion step there buy nothing.

Some questions are not retrieval problems. Counting, summing, filtering and joining belong to a structured query against the relational source, because aggregation has no vector-space answer. A relationship lookup such as what superseded a part is foreign-key or graph traversal. A graph is for multi-hop relationship questions only, and it is expensive to build and maintain. A metadata filter is part of the query itself.

Go deeper: one assistant, three sources, a router

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and its sources and numbers are invented. The assistant reaches three sources: 8,000 troubleshooting articles in prose with codes inline, a 45,000-row parts catalogue in a relational database, and an equipment hierarchy with sub-assemblies, compatible parts and superseded-by links.

A router classifies each query by shape and sends it on. "Compressor is short-cycling on a model 400" goes to dense retrieval over the articles, filtered to model 400. "What does ERR_5521 mean" goes to lexical retrieval. "Is part 88-2210J still in stock" is a SQL lookup on the catalogue and no retrieval at all. "What replaced the 88-2210J" is a traversal over the hierarchy.

Only "why does the compressor fail after the ERR_5521 sequence" justifies hybrid, since it mixes prose and a code.

The 34% against 89% from the opening is what justifies the second index to a stakeholder. A general claim that hybrid is better does not.

Rerank when the right document is there but ranked low§3.12

The standard pattern is to retrieve wide, with a fast approximate search returning the top 20 to 50 candidates, and rerank narrow. A reranker rescores the candidates, and the top 3 to 5 go into context. The discriminator is whether the right document is absent from the candidate set or present and ranked low.

Raising top-k costs more tokens, more money, more latency and a worse attention gradient, the uneven attention across a longer context (§2.6). Reranking costs a latency stage that must fit the budget. If the correct document is consistently at rank 8 to 15, add a reranking stage over the existing candidates. If it is not in the top 50 at all, change the retrieval mechanism or the chunking, and leave the depth alone.

If top-k has already been raised to 40 and the context is crowded, reduce the number of chunks passed on and rerank to 3 to 5, because fewer, better chunks beat more, noisier ones. If reranking would breach a stated latency SLA, reduce the candidate width, or fund the stage by recovering latency elsewhere. The budget is the constraint, and reranking is the technique.

Go deeper: what raising k costs against reranking

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and the chunk size and volume are assumptions. At 500 tokens a chunk, raising k from 5 to 25 adds 20 chunks, which is 10,000 tokens a request. At 30,000 requests a day that is 300,000,000 extra input tokens a day, and the document that was ranked eighth still sits among 20 weaker neighbours.

Retrieving 20 and reranking to the top 5 keeps the context at 2,500 tokens and puts the right document near the top. The price is one more stage. If that stage takes 300 ms and the budget has 200 ms of slack, the stage needs 100 ms recovered elsewhere, or a narrower candidate set.
