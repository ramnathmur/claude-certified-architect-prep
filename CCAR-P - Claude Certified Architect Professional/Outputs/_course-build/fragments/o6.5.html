A claims assistant has had a successful pilot with 40 users, and the plan is to roll it out to 800, which is twenty times as many. The pilot's failure rate was low, its users were happy, and the build team sat next to them. A manager asks for a capacity plan: how many requests a day, what rate limits, what the bill will be.

The capacity plan is a fair request, and it is not the first thing to produce. Scaling changes who sends requests and what they send, as well as how many. What has the pilot been quietly assuming?

List the assumptions the pilot relied on before planning capacity§6.11

The corpus discriminator is which small-scale assumptions stop holding at large scale. There are three classes. The input distribution widens, the support and human-review load grows, and rare failures become routine. The sequence is to surface the assumptions first, and the cost model and model choice follow from them. The failure mode is to extrapolate pilot cost and pilot accuracy linearly onto a wider population.

For a successful 40-user pilot going to 800 users, list which input, support-load and edge-case assumptions may not hold, then rebuild the cost model. The wider population brings input types the pilot never sampled. A 1-in-500 failure seen twice in a 200-case pilot is a routine event at scale: rare is relative to volume, and at that volume it is several events a day.

If human review absorbed 12% of pilot volume, convert it into a staffing line at projected volume before rollout. The pilot review was done by the build team and is invisible in the pilot cost. Rate limits and infrastructure capacity for the new volume are necessary, and they are not the binding constraint. They are the visible constraint, and the input distribution is the one that bites.

Go deeper: the staffing and failure sums

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and the volumes are invented. Suppose 800 users send 50 requests a day each, which is 40,000 requests. A failure at 1 in 500 happens 80 times a day. The pilot saw it twice in 200 cases, which looked like a footnote, and at this volume it is daily work.

At 12% review, 4,800 items a day need a person. At three minutes each that is 240 hours, or 30 people on eight-hour shifts, a line that appeared in no pilot budget.

Hand over the means to detect degradation, and not only the code§6.12

The corpus discriminator is whether the receiving team can answer "is it still working?" without the original team. A handover has four items: the evaluation suite, the acceptance criteria, a named owner who is a person with a deputy, and the re-check routine.

Monitoring has two layers. Operational telemetry covers latency, errors, cost and volume. Quality telemetry covers evaluation scores, the human-override rate and the confidence distribution, which is how often the system reports high or low confidence.

If a system is transferring to an internal operations team, hand over the evaluation suite, the thresholds, a named owner and a deputy, the re-check cadence and the implementation guidance. Then the receiving team can detect and act without the original team.

A handover of repository access, documentation and training sessions is insufficient, since nothing in it detects a change in behaviour. Ownership assigned to a team and not to a person is insufficient too, because a distributed owner is no owner.

If complaints rise after launch while error rates stay flat, check for a shift in the input distribution and re-run the evaluation suite, since silent quality degradation does not show in operational telemetry. If the evaluation set is unchanged twelve months after launch, iteration is broken, because the set has drifted away from what the system actually processes.

KnownThe guide's three samples sit in Domains 2, 3 and 4, so none of them is a sample for this domain. InferenceExpect options that do a real and necessary task in the wrong order, or that transfer capability without the means to detect failure.

A successful pilot is to be rolled out to twenty times the user base.

  • CorrectIdentify which assumptions held at pilot scale and may not hold at full scale (input variety, support and review load, edge-case frequency), and rebuild the cost and model-tier decisions on those findings.
  • WrongHALF-MOVEProject infrastructure capacity and rate limits for the new request volume. It is a real and necessary step that skips the assumptions which determine what to project.
  • WrongARCHITECTEDMove to a higher-capability model ahead of rollout to absorb the wider variety of inputs. It sounds prudent and spends money before anyone has characterised the inputs it is meant to handle.

A delivered system is being handed to the client's internal team.

  • CorrectTransfer the evaluation suite, the acceptance thresholds and the trigger for action, a named owner with a deputy, and the routine for re-running the suite on a stated cadence and after any model or input-format change.
  • WrongARCHITECTEDTransfer the repository, full architecture documentation and training sessions. It is thorough and effortful, and silent on how the team detects that the system has stopped working.
  • WrongREPAIRRetain a support arrangement with the delivery team for the first year. It keeps the capability with the wrong party and defers the handover instead of completing it.
Say it backA 40-user pilot is going to 800 users, and the delivery team will hand the system to operations after launch. What do you produce before the rollout, and what do you hand over?

Before the rollout, an assumption audit: which input, support-load and edge-case assumptions may not hold, then the cost model rebuilt on that list, with review converted to a staffing line. At handover, the evaluation suite, the acceptance thresholds, a named owner with a deputy, and the re-check routine, plus the implementation guidance, so that the operations team can tell when quality drifts.

  2. A rare failure is routine at volume. Convert pilot review into a staffing line, and do not extrapolate pilot cost or accuracy linearly.

  4. A handover that can detect degradation holds the evaluation suite, the acceptance criteria, a named person as owner with a deputy, and the re-check routine.
