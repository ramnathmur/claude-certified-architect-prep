A document-triage assistant has a contractual p95 limit of 3,000 ms end to end, meaning 95% of requests must finish within it. At p95 the stages take 120 ms for the edge and identity, 180 for the query embedding, 240 for the vector search, 300 for reranking, 1,900 for generation and 60 for assembly. That totals 2,800 ms, leaving 200 ms of slack.

Quality is 91% on the evaluation set, and the business wants 95%. The figures are illustrative.

Three proposals arrive, each with a good accuracy story and a time cost. Which of them can the assistant afford, and what should it do with the 200 ms?

Spend latency only where the stated requirement pays for it§3.4

Accuracy and latency are both budgets, and most levers that buy accuracy spend latency: a larger model, more retrieved chunks, reranking, reasoning cues, self-critique passes and multi-hop retrieval. The method is a stage-level budget table at the percentile the SLA names. The corpus discriminator is whether an option satisfies the stated requirement or maximises an unstated one. The most accurate option is often the one that breaks the SLA.

Take the proposals in turn. A self-critique pass costs about 1,400 ms and breaks the SLA. A proposed change that exceeds the stated SLA is rejected, or the SLA is reopened explicitly with the stakeholder. A breach is a decision to make with the business, never one to absorb silently. Reducing the retrieved chunks to make room for the pass removes a working mechanism and usually loses more accuracy than it buys.

Look first for latency that is being wasted. One lever improves two things at once: put the static content first and enable prompt caching, which cuts time-to-first-token and cost together with no accuracy cost (§2.8). When the budget is tight and no slack exists, look for wasted latency in ordering, caching and redundant hops before cutting retrieved context. Cutting context loses more accuracy per millisecond saved than restructuring does.

When there is slack and accuracy is short of target, spend the slack on the stage with the highest accuracy per millisecond, usually reranking. If accuracy is already above the target and an option offers more at triple the latency, reject it, because accuracy bought outside a requirement is unpriced cost. To a stakeholder, present the stage table and the measured accuracy change, not a preference.

Go deeper: the three proposals priced, and what caching has to free

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and every timing is invented. The self-critique pass adds 1,400 ms, so the total is 4,200 ms, which is 40% over the limit. A larger generation model raises generation from 1,900 to 2,600 ms, which adds 700 and gives 3,500 ms. Retrieving the top 50 instead of the top 20 adds 90 ms to the search and 210 to the rerank, giving 3,100 ms.

The larger model needs 700 ms and the slack is 200, so the other changes must free at least 500 ms. Moving a 3,100-token static preamble to the front and caching it is the candidate. No source here says it frees that much. The saving has to be measured on the real system, and if it falls short, the model change does not fit.

One caution that is not a corpus rule: stage p95 figures do not add up to the end-to-end p95, because the p95 of a total is not the sum of the stage p95s. Use the table to see where time goes, then measure the end-to-end p95 itself.

Match the fix to the consumer and the percentile§3.5

Streaming changes time-to-first-token, how long a reader waits before the first words appear. It does not change total end-to-end latency. The discriminator is whether the consumer reads incrementally or waits for the complete response.

For a human-facing chat surface where users say the system "feels slow", enable streaming. The complaint is about time-to-first-token. For a downstream service that consumes the whole response before it acts, streaming buys nothing, because the consumer cannot start early. Reduce total latency instead. When an interactive surface and a batch path share the pipeline, stream the interactive path only and optimise total time for the batch path.

Budget at the percentile the SLA names, usually p95 or p99, and not at the mean. If a dashboard shows mean latency improved and users still complain, measure and optimise p95 or p99, because the SLA and the complaint both live in the tail.

Go deeper: a mean that improves while the tail stays put

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and its numbers are invented. Of 1,000 requests, 940 take 1.0 second and 60 take 9.0 seconds. The mean is 1.48 seconds. The p95 is 9.0 seconds, because 95% of requests (950) are only covered once the 9-second requests are included.

A change makes the 940 fast requests 0.2 seconds quicker. The mean falls to 1.292 seconds, a visible improvement on a dashboard. The p95 is still 9.0 seconds, and the 60 users in the tail notice nothing.

A system runs at 2,800 ms against a 3,000 ms p95 SLA and needs 4 more accuracy points. A proposed self-critique pass costs 1,400 ms.

Say it backA service is at 2,900 ms against a 3,000 ms p95 limit, and a teammate proposes a critique pass for 1,200 ms. Another proposes streaming for a downstream parser that reads only complete responses. What do you say to each?

Reject the critique pass, since 2,900 plus 1,200 is 4,100 and the SLA is a stated requirement. Reopen the SLA with the business if the accuracy is truly needed. Reject streaming for the parser: it waits for the whole response, so only total latency matters, and the work is to cut it at the slowest stage. Report p95, the percentile the SLA names.
