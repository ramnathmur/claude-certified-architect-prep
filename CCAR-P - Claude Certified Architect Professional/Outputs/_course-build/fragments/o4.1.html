A business sponsor says a new support assistant "must be fast, accurate, and safe". The team's dashboard tracks three numbers: average response time, average response length and daily request volume. None of the three says whether the assistant is accurate or safe, and none can stop a launch. The sponsor has given three adjectives, and an evaluation needs numbers that can pass or fail.

Suppose the assistant handles 12,000 tickets a month at a model spend of $3,600, and 9,000 of the tickets end resolved. The cost per resolved ticket is $0.40. A report in cost per token would not show that. The figures are illustrative.

Fix the bar before any score is seen§4.1

A finished metric has four parts: a named failure, a denominator (the count the failures are measured against), a segment (the slice of traffic it is reported on), and a threshold set before results are seen. The corpus discriminator is when the number was chosen. A bar fixed before any score gates a decision. A bar chosen afterwards only describes the result.

Where does a bar come from before there are results? From the success criterion, from frontier reality (what the best systems achieve), or from the error rate of the process being replaced. If an existing manual process has a measured 8% error rate, the bar is tied to that figure and beats it. An anchored bar is still a bar, and "we'll decide later" is not one.

If a stakeholder asks to "calibrate the threshold once we see baseline numbers", reject it. Set the bar now and revise it later as a recorded decision, because a post-hoc bar is only ever revised downward.

A bar of 0% ("0 per N" means zero failures in the N outputs checked) is allowed only where a deterministic check can enforce it, such as a PII field present or a citation span missing. For an open-ended, model-judged property, re-anchor 0% to a small achievable rate, for example under 0.1% of 10,000 samples. An unachievable bar becomes non-binding at the first miss.

Latency is reported at p95 or p99 (the time that 95% or 99% of requests come in at or under) and not as a mean, with time-to-first-token for an interactive surface and total time for a batch one (§3.5). Cost is reported per unit of business work, such as per resolved ticket or per claim, and not per token.

Go deeper: a bar set after the result, and what 0.1% of 10,000 means

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and its numbers are invented. A team plans a 95% accuracy bar, then measures a baseline of 91% and proposes to calibrate the bar to the baseline, at 90%. The bar has moved down 5 points, and the system that was going to fail now passes.

A bar fixed at 95% before the run gates the launch, and lowering it becomes a recorded decision with a name against it.

A bar of under 0.1% of 10,000 sampled outputs allows fewer than 10 failures in the sample. A bar of 0% fails on the first miss in a model-judged property, where the grader itself is imperfect, so the team stops treating it as binding.

The cost line in the opening shows the denominator at work. Divided by all 12,000 tickets, $3,600 gives $0.30 a ticket. Divided by the 9,000 resolved tickets, it gives $0.40, which is the figure the business pays per unit of work.

Score what the model says apart from what the system permits§4.2

Safety measures what the model says: policy-violating, toxic or non-compliant output. Security measures what the system permits: an injection reaching a tool, an over-scoped credential, or data leaving through a tool result. The corpus discriminator is whether the risk is realised by what the model emits or by what the system allows it to do.

The instruments differ with the families. Safety is a rate over a stated denominator, graded by a model or a classifier. Security is an adversarial suite: the block rate on attempted injections, the count of unauthorized tool invocations, and a least-privilege audit. A system can pass every safety metric and fail every security control.

Classify each requirement by where the harm sits. "Must not produce discriminatory language" is safety, since the harm is in the emitted text. "A malicious instruction inside a retrieved document must not trigger a refund tool call" is security, because the harm is a permitted action. "Must not be able to read records outside the requesting customer's account" is security too, an authorization scope measured by audit and adversarial probe.

"Outputs must not include another policyholder's identifier" is both. A deterministic PII check is the enforcing control, a content check that enforces a security property, so count it in both the safety and the security metrics, and neither report leaves it out. If someone proposes to cover a prompt-injection risk with a tone rubric, reject it, because a quality rubric cannot measure whether a tool fired.

Go deeper: reading an adversarial suite

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and its numbers are invented. A suite of 250 documents, each hiding an instruction that tries to trigger a refund call, is run against the agent. It blocks 247 of them, a block rate of 98.8%, and 3 documents trigger an unauthorized refund call. Against a bar of zero unauthorized calls, that run fails, whatever the 98.8% says.

Inference: the count of unauthorized tool invocations is a deterministic check on the tool log, so a bar of zero is allowed for it under §4.1. The block rate across varied attacks is a rate over a suite, so it takes a threshold set before the run. The same suite shows nothing about tone. That is why a tone rubric cannot stand in for it.
