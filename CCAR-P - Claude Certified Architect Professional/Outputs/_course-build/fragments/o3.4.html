A claims assistant handles 200,000 requests a day, and its escalation rate is rising. A full trace of one request, with the prompt, the retrieved chunks, the tool arguments and the tool results, averages 30 KB. Tracing everything stores 6 GB a day, about 2.2 TB a year, and copies claimant data into a second store. The figures are illustrative.

Tracing 1% of all requests at random stores 60 MB a day and catches about 20 of the 2,000 escalations that happen daily. Neither policy answers the team's question, which is why the escalation rate is rising.

Trace the decision path, not only the output§3.6

The unit of observability is the trace, not the log line. A useful trace captures the tool calls with their arguments and results, the retrieved chunk IDs with document versions and scores, the decision points and guardrail firings, and one correlation ID from the first hop to the last.

The corpus discriminator is whether the capture explains why the output was produced or only that it was. Without the decision path, a confidently wrong answer looks the same as a correct one at the response layer. A log of requests and responses says what happened and nothing about why.

When wrong answers are intermittent and the team sees only inputs and final outputs, instrument the tool calls, the retrieved context with document versions, and the decision points. That tells you which stage produced the error. When there is a latency regression and only end-to-end timing exists, add per-stage timing. That turns "the system got slow" into "the reranker got slow".

A request that spans several agents and tools needs one correlation ID propagated through every hop. Without it the trace fragments and cannot be reassembled. Traces also hold prompts and retrieved documents, which makes the trace store a second copy of sensitive data. Design its redaction, retention and access control at the same time as the pipeline, because the observability system inherits the sensitivity of what it captures.

Sample by stratum so the rare events are all kept§3.7

The corpus discriminator is whether the events you need to see are common or rare. Uniform sampling is calibrated to the common case, and failures are rare. So capture all of the high-information events and only a slice of the routine traffic. The stratified policy keeps 100% of errors, escalations, low-confidence paths and audit-relevant requests, and a small percentage of routine successes.

For baselines and regression detection, a small uniform sample of routine successes is enough, since baselines need only enough samples to be stable. For regulated data, audit needs the fact of access and not the content, so keep 100% of access metadata with the payload redacted. Retention is set per stratum: short for routine samples, regulation-length for audit metadata.

Metrics follow a separate rule. They carry low-cardinality dimensions (a small, fixed set of values) such as tool name, model, tenant tier and outcome class. User, session and document IDs belong in traces. If someone proposes labelling metrics by user ID for per-user slicing, reject it and serve that need from traces. High-cardinality labels make the metrics backend slow and expensive.

Reject a proposal for 100% full-fidelity capture "and we'll optimise later". At these volumes it is unaffordable, and it creates a second sensitive store.

Go deeper: the storage arithmetic of the three policies

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and the 30 KB trace and the stratum sizes are assumptions. Tracing everything stores 200,000 traces of 30 KB, which is 6 GB a day, and 2,190 GB, about 2.2 TB, over a year. Tracing 1% uniformly stores 60 MB a day and keeps 1% of the 2,000 escalations, which is 20.

The stratified policy traces 10,860 requests. At 30 KB each that is 325.8 MB a day, a bit over five times the uniform sample, and it holds all 2,000 escalations, all 1,000 errors and all 4,000 low-confidence paths. Alongside it, metrics with low-cardinality labels show that something moved. The traces show why, for the strata kept.

Inference: a request is known to have failed or escalated only when it ends, so the keep-or-drop decision for those strata is made after the request finishes, and its trace has to be held until then.
