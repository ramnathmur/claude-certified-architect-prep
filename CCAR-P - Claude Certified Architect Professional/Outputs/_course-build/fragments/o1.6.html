An invoice pipeline processes 50,000 invoices a month, and three people judge it in three different units. The finance director asks what an invoice costs: $2.10 by hand, now $0.49, made of $0.11 of compute and $0.38 of residual human review.

The operations director asks how long an invoice takes a person: 11 minutes before, 2 now. The CIO asks whether the pipeline keeps its promise: a p95 of 7.5 seconds against a limit of 10.

Those three answers belong to three of the five pillars the guide names: cost, efficiency and performance SLAs, meaning stated commitments such as a response-time limit. A scenario names one pillar, and the design that moves that pillar is the answer. This chapter separates the pillars and shows how the named one constrains the architecture.

Name the pillar first, then let it constrain the design§1.12

The guide names five pillars, and each is a different unit. The unit is what lets you tell them apart, because the same project reads differently under each.

PillarThe unitThe question it answers
EfficiencyInput per unit of output, such as minutes per invoiceIs the same work done with less?
TransformationA capability that did not exist beforeCan we now do something we could not do at all?
ProductivityOutput per person, or what the freed time goes toAre the same people producing more, or better?
CostTotal unit economics, such as cost per transactionWhat does the solution itself cost to run?
Performance SLAA stated commitment on speed, availability or accuracyIs the promise kept?

Efficiency and productivity sit close together. Efficiency reduces the input for a fixed output: an invoice takes 2 minutes of a person where it took 11. Productivity changes what the freed capacity does: the team that stopped keying invoices now works exceptions and vendor disputes. Which one to lead with depends on who is listening.

Cost is more than the inference bill. Total cost includes inference, retrieval infrastructure, human review time, error remediation and maintenance. A design that halves inference cost and doubles human review time has made the total worse. At high volume the lever that moves cost is routing by difficulty, with the smallest model that clears the bar for each tier, the split described in O1.5.

A performance SLA behaves differently from the other four. It is a filter applied before designs are compared, not a tiebreaker after. It is written against the tail, the slowest few percent of requests, so a design whose average meets it can still miss it.

A workflow's cost per request is close to one number because its steps are fixed. An agent's cost is a distribution, since the number of calls varies, so the budget has to cover the tail.

Here is a worked example with invented figures. The commitment is a p95 of 3 seconds, and each model call takes about 0.8 seconds at p95. Treat the times as additive, which is a simplification. The chain has five calls: two enrichment calls (E1 and E2) that depend only on the stored document, then retrieval (R), a draft (D) and a check (V), each using the result before it.

In series the chain takes 4.0 seconds and fails. Running E1 and E2 together saves one slot and leaves 3.2 seconds, still over. Precomputing them at ingest takes them off the request path. Three calls remain, taking 2.4 seconds, with 0.6 seconds to spare.

The SLA removed designs before quality came up. A commitment of a 3-second p95 rules out a nine-call agent at the start, because tuning does not bring a nine-call agent to a 3-second p95. The same logic removes whole patterns from the ladder of O1.3, which is why a stated figure in a scenario is read first.

The scenario names one pillar, and the correct design moves that pillar. An answer that cites a general virtue, such as accuracy or elegance, instead of the stated requirement is the distractor. When two designs both work and only one meets the stated commitment, pick that one. The technically superior design that misses the SLA is the trap.

Transformation is argued differently. If the capability did not exist before, there is no baseline cost to compare against, so efficiency has no denominator and forcing the comparison understates the case. Finally, state the architecture in the unit the listener measures in: minutes, dollars, throughput or a commitment met. Accuracy and other model metrics are inputs to the business case.

Go deeper: the invoice pipeline in dollars, and a cost trap

A worked example with invented figures, not a corpus rule. At 50,000 invoices a month, handling by hand at $2.10 an invoice costs $105,000 a month, or $1,260,000 a year. The pipeline at $0.49 an invoice costs $24,500 a month, or $294,000 a year, with the human review already inside that figure.

Both costs price a minute of a person at about $0.19: 11 minutes cost $2.10, and the 2 minutes of review cost $0.38.

Now the trap. Halve the compute, from $0.11 to $0.055, and double the review, from $0.38 to $0.76. An invoice then costs $0.815 against today's $0.49, a rise of 66%. The inference line looks cheaper while the total is dearer.

A team must meet a stated p95 latency commitment. Its agentic design has a mean latency that fits, and a tail that does not.

  • CorrectRestructure to a workflow with the independent steps parallelised and enrichment precomputed at ingest, keeping the agent only for the exception population.
  • WrongHALF-MOVEKeep the agentic design and tune prompts to reduce the average number of tool calls. It improves the mean, while the commitment is written against the tail.
  • WrongREPAIRAdd caching, retries and a monitoring alert on SLA breaches. It instruments and mitigates a breach that the architecture will keep producing, and leaves its cause in place.
Say it backA team proposes an agent that averages nine model calls for a request with a p95 commitment of 3 seconds, and the average looks fine in testing. What do you check, and what do you change?

The commitment is written against the tail, so the average does not settle it, and nine calls will not reach 3 seconds by tuning. Move to a workflow: run the independent steps together, precompute enrichment at ingest, and keep the agent only for the exception population.

Remember

  1. Five pillars, five units: efficiency (input per output), transformation (a capability that did not exist), productivity (output per person), cost (total unit economics) and performance SLA (a stated commitment).
  2. The scenario names one pillar, and the design that moves it wins. An answer that cites a general virtue in its place is the distractor.
  3. An SLA is a filter applied before designs are compared. It is written against the tail, so a good average does not meet it.
  4. Cost is total: inference, retrieval infrastructure, human review, error remediation and maintenance. Halving inference while doubling review is a regression.
  5. At volume, route by confidence tier with the smallest model that clears the bar for each tier. When the capability is new, argue transformation.
  6. Answer a stakeholder in their own unit: minutes, dollars, throughput or the commitment met.
