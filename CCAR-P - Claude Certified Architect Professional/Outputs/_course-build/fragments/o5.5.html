A screening system reports 91% overall accuracy, and complaints come from one group of applicants. The applicants fall into three groups: 85% of them score 94% accuracy, 12% score 80%, and the last 3% score 50%. The team's reflex is to raise the overall target and retrain. The figures are illustrative.

The 91% is a weighted average, and it can be pulled up by the 85% that works well while the 3% that does not is wrong half the time. The same averaging can hide the problem from the evaluation set as well as from the dashboard.

Measure by subgroup, with enough cases in each§5.10

Aggregate accuracy is a weighted average that hides subgroup failure. Evaluate on a set stratified by the subgroups the organisation is accountable for, with adequate N per group, meaning enough cases in each, and not a population-proportional sample. If the set holds 400 cases in proportion to the groups above, the 3% group has 12 of them, too few to measure a rate, against the working floor of about 30 per segment in §4.4.

Measure by subgroup. Removing the protected attribute from the input does not remove the bias, since proxy variables carry the same signal: postal code, school name, employment gaps, name morphology and phrasing register.

It also removes the ability to detect the disparity, so a proposal to drop the attribute is rejected as the fairness control. For a small subgroup under a proportional sample, rebalance to equal N per group, since proportional sampling gives the least power where the risk is highest.

Fairness metrics can conflict. Equal selection rate, equal false-negative rate and equal calibration cannot generally hold together when base rates differ. The conflict is mathematical, and the resolution is a stated decision: choose by which harm is being controlled, and record the rationale. Declare which harm is controlled, and therefore which definition of fairness is in force.

Three terms in that rule need a gloss. The false-negative rate is the share of people who qualify and are wrongly rejected. Calibration means that a given score carries the same chance of the outcome for every group. The base rate is how common the outcome is within a group.

Fairness is a measurement that moves with the population and not a property measured once. If it was measured at launch and signed off, recompute it on live traffic on a schedule.

Go deeper: why equal N costs fewer cases and shows more

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and the shares are invented. A proportional sample of 400 cases gives groups of 340, 48 and 12. Equal N of 100 per group needs 300 cases in all, which is fewer, and it gives the weakest group 100 cases against 12.

The aggregate from an equal-N set no longer mirrors the applicant population. That is acceptable, since the set exists to measure each group, and the corpus sets the threshold against the worst-performing group. The same reasoning appears in §4.4, where a rate needs about 30 cases per segment. The 100 per group here is this example's choice, comfortably above that floor.

Keep a record of the decision, and tell people AI was involved§5.11

Transparency has two distinct meanings, and a third need, contestability, sits beside them. Disclosure means the affected person knows AI was involved and how to reach a human. Explainability means a specific decision can be accounted for afterwards. The two need different mechanisms, and a design can meet one and miss the other.

The explainability artefact is a logged, reconstructable trace: the inputs used, the evidence retrieved, the prompt and model version, the score, the threshold in force, the rule applied and any human decision. A model-generated rationale is not an explainability artefact. It is plausible text about the decision and not a record of what produced it, and it can be confidently wrong about its own causes.

If a regulator asks why a specific applicant was declined, produce the stored trace. Attaching a model-written explanation to each decision is useful for the end user and does not serve as the audit record. Where the requirement is that people know they are interacting with an AI system, provide disclosure at the point of interaction plus a stated route to a human. That is disclosure and not explainability.

A consequential automated decision with no challenge path needs a contestation route, with a queue and a status field. The trace is cheap at write time and expensive to reconstruct after the fact, and the same holds for a route to a human, which is cheap at design time and costly after the first complaint.
