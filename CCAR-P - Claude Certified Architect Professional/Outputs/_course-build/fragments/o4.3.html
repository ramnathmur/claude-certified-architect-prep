A support team rewrites its assistant's prompt. On ten examples that someone picked by hand, the new version reads better, and the team wants to ship it to every user. Ten hand-picked examples are persuasive, and they are not evidence. Nobody chose them at random, nobody declared in advance what "better" means, and nothing says the edit did not break a rare case.

What does a prompt change need before it reaches all users? The corpus answers with an order, and every step in it exists because the step before it cannot answer one particular question.

Regression first, then A/B, then staged rollout§4.9

The order is a regression evaluation on the fixed set, then a controlled A/B test on metrics defined in advance, then a staged rollout.

A regression evaluation replays a fixed test set to check that nothing that worked has got worse. A controlled A/B test splits live traffic between the current prompt and the new one. Regression comes first because the A/B costs live traffic and only the fixed set over-samples rare segments. The A/B comes second because the fixed set cannot report on the live distribution, real load or production latency.

The corpus discriminator is whether the change was checked before it touched traffic, and whether a result could be attributed to it. Change one variable per variant. If a variant changes the prompt, the model and the retrieval depth together, split it into separate variants. Otherwise a win is unattributable, and the next regression has three suspects.

Randomise by user or session and never per request. A per-request split flips a conversation between prompts mid-thread, which corrupts the behaviour and the metric together. Declare the primary metric and the guardrails before launch, and let the guardrails only block. A variant that improves the primary metric and breaches the declared latency guardrail is not shipped.

Fix the duration and the sample size in advance. A test that looks favourable at day 4 of a planned 14 runs to day 14, because stopping on good news inflates the false-positive rate, the chance of declaring a win that is only noise.

The corpus gives no rule for sizing the duration or the sample, and a real test sizes them from the smallest difference worth detecting.

Report by segment as well as in aggregate. If the aggregate improves by 1.5 points and one segment regresses by 12, do not ship on the aggregate, since segment masking applies to A/B results as it does to accuracy.

If a change has already shipped to 100% with no control arm, state that no clean comparison exists. A before-and-after reading is confounded by everything else that changed in the window.

Go deeper: how an aggregate gain and a segment loss fit together

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and its shares are invented. Suppose the regressing segment carries 10% of traffic and falls by 12 points. For the aggregate to rise by 1.5 points, the other 90% of traffic has to gain 3.0 points on average, because 1.5 is the weighted sum of the two changes.

Nothing in the aggregate hints at the 12-point loss. Inference: looking at the result every day for 14 days gives noise 14 chances to cross the threshold, which is why the corpus fixes the duration in advance.
