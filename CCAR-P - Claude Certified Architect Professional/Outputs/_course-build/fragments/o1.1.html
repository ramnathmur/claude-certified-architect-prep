The sales director of a commercial insurance broker asks, "Can Claude write our quotes?" The broker issues 400 quotes a week, and each one takes an underwriter 22 minutes on average. An underwriter is a person who decides whether to accept an insurance risk and at what price. The week holds 8,800 minutes of quote work, about 147 hours.

Three underwriters have about 35 productive hours a week each, 105 hours between them, so the team is about 42 hours a week short and quotes wait. Turnaround, the time from a request arriving to the quote going out, averages 3.2 business days, and the broker loses business on any quote that takes longer than 2 days.

Before anyone picks a design, each step of the work needs scoring for what a model can own (§1.1), and today's cost needs writing down in a unit the business already uses (§1.2).

Score every step on its own§1.1

What you automate inside a workflow, the chain of steps from a request to a result, is a decision: a recurring point where someone reads inputs and forms a judgment. An outcome such as "quote faster" is too broad to build, and a task such as copying a field into another system involves no judgment.

Four fit signals favour automating a step. More cases arrive than people can handle. The same kind of judgment recurs, and someone eventually sees whether it was right. That result is the feedback. The inputs are in a form a machine can read. The volume is high enough to repay the cost of building.

Five wrong-stance signals show that handing the decision itself to the model is the wrong stance. The decision is irreversible and rare. A regulation requires a human decision-maker. The output is novel and creative, with low repeatability. The human relationship is the product. A human must gate every iteration, which means approving each pass before the next begins.

Apply the signals to one step at a time, never to the whole workflow. A workflow almost never scores the same all the way through, and the split that results is the design. The broker's process has five steps, and the figure scores each one.

Classifying the risk tier, which places a risk in a band, is the slow-feedback case. The loss ratio, claims paid against premiums earned, shows whether a tier was right only after 12 months. A slow result does not rule a step out. It needs a proxy signal, a fast measure that stands in for the slow one. How often underwriters override the model's tier is one, because it shows the day someone disagrees.

Pricing and signing is the regulated step. The quote needs a licensed underwriter's signature, so the model prepares a complete draft and the underwriter decides. That step holds 4 of the 22 minutes. The three automated steps hold 16, or 73% of the total, and a verdict of "not a fit" for the whole workflow would give those up.

Where the human relationship is the product, as in relationship-managed sales, the model prepares the conversation and a person holds it.

Go deeper: the broker's week in numbers

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule. The design automates the three steps worth 9 + 3 + 4 = 16 minutes a quote. That leaves 22 minus 16 = 6 minutes, made up of 4 for pricing and signing and 2 for formatting and sending.

Across 400 quotes a week, the automated steps remove 400 × 16 = 6,400 minutes of human work, about 107 hours. The underwriters' remaining work is 400 × 6 = 2,400 minutes, or 40 hours, against the 105 they have.

The 6 minutes is a projection. Whether turnaround falls below 2 days is a separate question, and only a measurement against the baseline in §1.2 can answer it.

Fix the baseline and the unit before you design§1.2

A request names a mechanism, and the pain behind it names the requirement. The director asks for quotes written by Claude, while the pain is a turnaround of 3.2 days with business lost beyond 2 days. The target is turnaround. Quality of prose is not the binding constraint, the factor that actually limits the result.

Before designing, state what the process costs today in a value unit, a unit the business already uses: minutes per item, FTE-hours (hours of full-time-equivalent staff time) per 1,000 items, dollars per transaction, or days of turnaround. That statement is the baseline. For the broker it reads 22 minutes and 3.2 business days per quote, and the business case is written in that sentence.

Without a baseline no design can be compared with another and no value can be reported. The system ships and works, and nobody can show it helped. An improvement that was never measured cannot be defended to the person who funded it.

If no baseline exists, measure the current process before committing to a design. A plan to "measure the improvement after launch" produces a number with nothing before it to compare with.

One case has no baseline to find: a capability that never existed. A per-unit comparison needs a per-unit figure from before, and a new capability has none, so there is no efficiency baseline. The value is argued as transformation, one of the five value pillars, the kinds of business value that objective 1.6 lists.

InferenceExpect options that all automate something and differ in which step they target, with the disqualifying constraint placed in the setup of the stem.

A broker wants faster quotes. One step in its process requires a licensed underwriter's signature.

  • WrongOVERSPECAutomate the whole quote, sign-off included, with an audit log and a confidence threshold for oversight. The scenario requires a human decision-maker, and no threshold satisfies that requirement. The option puts a monitoring guarantee where the required person should be.

Say it backA broker's quotes take 3.2 days and it loses business above 2. One step needs a licensed underwriter's signature. What do you measure first, and what do you automate?

Measure the baseline first: minutes per quote and days of turnaround, because turnaround is the requirement. Then score the steps one by one. Automate extraction, lookup and a risk-tier recommendation, use the override rate as a proxy while the tier waits 12 months for its outcome, and give the underwriter a complete draft to sign.

Remember

  1. The unit of automation is a recurring decision inside a workflow. Score each step, never the whole workflow.
  2. Four fit signals: scale past human capacity, a repeatable pattern with observable feedback, machine-readable context, volume that repays the build. Five wrong-stance signals: irreversible and rare, regulation requiring a human, novel low-repeatability output, the relationship as the product, a human gating every iteration.
  3. Slow feedback, such as a 12-month loss ratio, still means automate. Add a fast proxy: the override rate or the reopen rate.
  4. A licensed or statutory decision stays human, and the model prepares the draft. One regulated step does not make the whole workflow a non-fit. If the relationship is the product, automate the preparation.
  5. A request names a mechanism and the pain names the requirement. Design against the metric the pain names.
  6. Baseline first, in a business unit: minutes per item, FTE-hours per 1,000, dollars per transaction, days of turnaround. Measuring after launch has no before. A capability that never existed has no efficiency baseline, so argue transformation.
