A document pipeline processes 40,000 documents a day, and its extraction accuracy is 94%, so it gets about 2,400 of them wrong each day. A wrong formatting detail costs almost nothing. A wrong financial figure is expensive. The team proposes to have people review every output at first and to relax the review as confidence in the system grows. The figures are illustrative.

At three minutes a review, 40,000 reviews is 2,000 hours of work a day, or 250 people on eight-hour shifts. Where should the scarce human attention go?

Route by confidence and consequence together§5.8

Route on two variables together: confidence and consequence. A strategy that names only one, or none, is incomplete by construction. The absolute rule is that above a consequence threshold the item goes to a human regardless of confidence. For any irreversible or externally visible action, such as a denial, a payment or a message to a customer, the human supplies accountability and not additional accuracy.

The corpus lists the valid confidence signals: agreement from an independent verifier, a retrieval score, a schema validation result, a calibrated classifier, and business rules such as amount, tenure or jurisdiction. The business rules mark the consequence side of the routing as much as the confidence side. The invalid signal is the model's own statement of how sure it is.

For high volume, high accuracy and mixed consequence, auto-approve the high-confidence, low-consequence items. Route the rest by threshold, and always route the high-consequence ones. That puts a scarce resource where being wrong is expensive.

To check that the auto-approved threshold is still right, take a continuous small sample of the auto-approved stream and audit it on a schedule. That measures the unsupervised path, and it differs from routing by sample, which would send a random slice of everything to review. A random 10% would reach only 10% of the costly financial items.

The cost is review percentage times volume times minutes per review, which equals headcount, and the answer should state it. A scenario that states a volume is there to make blanket review unstaffable, so reject 100% review. Where work is genuinely low in volume and uniformly high in consequence, such as a handful of regulatory filings a month, reviewing every item is correct, because blanket review is wrong because of scale and not on principle.

The failure mode is automation bias: a reviewer who is shown a fluent conclusion without its evidence approves it. Inference: a reviewer needs the evidence set beside the conclusion, or the human gate adds a signature and no check.

Go deeper: the headcount sums behind the figure

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and every count is invented. Suppose 15% of the 40,000 documents carry financial figures, which is 6,000, and 10% of the other 34,000 are low-confidence, which is 3,400. Together that is 9,400 reviews a day, 23.5% of the volume.

At three minutes each, 9,400 reviews take 470 hours, which is about 59 people. Reviewing everything takes 250. The sampled audit adds 306 reviews, or about 15.3 hours, and it is what tells the team whether the 30,600 auto-approved items are still accurate.

Say it backA system handles 40,000 items a day, a few of which authorise payments. A teammate proposes full review at first, and another proposes reviewing a random 10%. What routing do you propose, and what do you add to check it?

Route on consequence and confidence together. Send every payment-related item to a human whatever the confidence, send low-confidence items by a calibrated threshold, and auto-approve the rest. Add a continuous small sample of the auto-approved stream, audited on a schedule, to check that the threshold still holds. Full review at 40,000 items a day cannot be staffed, and a random 10% meets the costly cases only as often as they occur.

Remember

  1. Route on confidence and consequence together. Above a consequence threshold, a human reviews whatever the confidence.
  2. Confidence comes from independent signals: a verifier, a retrieval score, schema validation, a calibrated classifier, business rules. The model's own statement of sureness is not one.
  3. Auto-approve high-confidence, low-consequence items, route the rest by threshold, and sample the auto-approved stream on a schedule.
  4. State the cost: review percentage times volume times minutes per review equals headcount. A stated volume rejects 100% review, and low volume with uniform high consequence accepts it.
  5. Automation bias means approving a fluent conclusion that arrives without its evidence.
