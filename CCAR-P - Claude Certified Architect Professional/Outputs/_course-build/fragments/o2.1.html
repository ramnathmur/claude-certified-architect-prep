A support team classifies 2,000,000 tickets a month, each into one of 12 queues. The answer is a single label that must come back in under a second. A test on 500 hand-labelled tickets shows that a small model reaches the accuracy the team set as its bar. A stakeholder still asks for the biggest model, to be safe.

On Anthropic's models page, read on 2026-10-25, the largest current model lists at $10 per million input tokens and the smallest at $0.10, a ratio of 100 to 1. At 2,000,000 tickets a month, that ratio is the whole argument. The chapter is about when the gap buys something and when it is headroom nobody asked for.

Match the model to the task, not to the ceiling§2.1

The discriminator is the task's complexity and ambiguity, set against cost and latency at the volume the task actually runs. A narrow, bounded task such as choosing one of 12 labels does not need the most capable tier. The answer is the smallest, fastest model that clears the accuracy bar, because cost and latency compound at volume.

Complexity can run the other way. Multi-step synthesis across ambiguous sources, at low volume, calls for the higher-capability model. Here task complexity, not volume, is the binding constraint, and a small model would be the wrong saving.

Suppose a stakeholder asks for "the biggest model, to be safe" and names no complexity driver. Push back and ask what specifically requires it. Headroom bought without a requirement is unpriced cost.

A workload that already meets its accuracy bar does not need a newer, more capable model because one exists. Upgrade only when a specific unmet requirement drives it, such as an accuracy gap or a missing capability, and run a benchmark before switching: a test of the candidate on representative inputs.

A switch with no gap behind it adds cost and latency risk for no proven gain, and can silently change behaviour on edge cases.

A second rule is about reasoning depth. Suppose one step needs deep multi-step reasoning across ambiguous, high-stakes trade-offs, and latency is not gated. Enable extended thinking for that step. Do not move the whole workload to a larger model, because extended thinking targets the bottleneck, reasoning depth, without paying to upsize every other call.

Current API versions expose this capability as adaptive thinking with an effort setting. The fold at the end of this section gives the dated detail.

Go deeper: the ticket classifier in dollars, and what the docs say about thinking now

A worked example, not a corpus rule. Assume each ticket is 500 input tokens and 20 output tokens (invented figures). Two million tickets a month is 1,000 million input tokens and 40 million output tokens. At the prices on the models page (read 2026-10-25), Haiku 5.5 charges $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, so the month costs $120.

Sonnet 5.5 ($2 and $10) costs $2,400 on the same traffic, Opus 5.5 ($4 and $20) costs $4,800, and Fable 5.1 ($10 and $50) costs $12,000. The page lists Haiku 5.5 as "from" its price, so treat $120 as the floor of the range.

On thinking, the same page describes the current models as using adaptive thinking, where the model decides how much to think and an effort setting steers it. The manual form of extended thinking, a fixed thinking budget, is deprecated on the 4.6 models and rejected on later ones (extended-thinking page, read 2026-10-25). The corpus rule stands as written: spend reasoning on the one step where it is the bottleneck.
