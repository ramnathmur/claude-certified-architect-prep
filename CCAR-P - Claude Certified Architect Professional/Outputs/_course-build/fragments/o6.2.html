In a steering meeting, the sponsor of a document-processing system asks two questions. "Give me one accuracy number I can take to the board." And, later, "why can't we automate the last 20%?" The system scores 94% on standard documents and 61% on complex ones. Neither question can be answered well with a single short sentence, and both can be answered badly in a few words.

If 80% of the documents are standard, the headline is 87%. The sponsor will take that to the board. What happens to it next quarter?

Report performance in the shape it actually has§6.4

The corpus discriminator is whether performance varies sharply by input type. If it does, an aggregate misleads. The professional answer has three components: break performance out by case type, state the measurement method and the set it was measured on, and give the end-to-end outcome including human review. The sponsor is deciding a budget, and the end-to-end outcome is the number that decision needs.

Both failure directions are real. A flattering aggregate misleads, and refusing to quantify fails the sponsor too, since a budget decision cannot be made on a qualitative answer. An architect who says "it depends on the case" is not acceptable. If the sponsor has been handed the model metric and is planning against it, supply the end-to-end outcome with the human steps included, because the sponsor is accountable for the process and not the component.

If performance is genuinely uniform across input types, a single figure is honest, and the method and set are still stated. Segmentation without variance is noise.

Go deeper: what an end-to-end figure can look like

This is a worked example, not a corpus rule, and its volumes are invented. Take 10,000 documents a month, with 8,000 standard and 2,000 complex. All complex documents go to a person. Then 2,000 documents a month get human review, and the standard stream gives 7,520 documents right without review and 480 wrong ones.

That is a statement the sponsor can budget against: the number of reviews, the staff time they need, and the count of errors that remain. It says more than either 87% or 94%. The per-segment rates hold when the mix shifts, and the counts are recomputed from the new mix, assuming review corrects what it sees.

Name the decisions that resist automation, and what automating them would risk§6.6

The corpus discriminator is whether the specific decisions that resist automation can be named, along with what automating them would risk. Two classes resist it: decisions that carry accountability, which someone must attest to, and decisions the system cannot verify it got right. A general appeal to model imperfection, or to a human in the loop for safety, is the register that fails, because it gives the stakeholder nothing to decide with.

If a stakeholder asks why the last 20% still needs people, name the decisions in that 20% that carry accountability or are unverifiable, and the risk of automating each. That hands the stakeholder a costed choice and not a caution.

If the residual work is unverifiable and not regulated, say so: the system cannot check its own output on those cases against ground truth. That is a different limit with a different remedy, and better evaluation data may close it.

If the residual work is small, low-risk and merely unbuilt, say so and price it, since not every human step is a principled limit. If a stakeholder proposes removing review from a regulated segment to hit a savings target, refuse on the named attestation requirement and quantify what the segment is worth. Where a regulator requires an accountable person, the constraint is the attestation and not the accuracy, and it cannot be traded against savings.

InferenceExpect options at both ends: an answer that gives the number requested and misleads, and one that refuses to give a number or to be specific.

An executive demands a single accuracy figure for a system whose performance varies sharply by document type.

  • CorrectReport accuracy broken out by document type, state how it was measured and on what set, and give the end-to-end business outcome including the human review step.
  • WrongHALF-MOVEReport the weighted average across all document types as the headline figure. It answers the question asked with a number that is unstable and conceals the weak segment.
  • WrongDISCARDExplain that accuracy varies too much by case for a single figure to be meaningful. It is technically defensible and it abandons the quantification the sponsor's decision requires.

A stakeholder asks why the system cannot be fully automated.

  • CorrectIdentify the specific decisions that require human accountability or cannot be verified by the system, and state what automating each would risk.
  • WrongHALF-MOVEExplain that AI systems are probabilistic and that a human in the loop is a safety practice. It is true and unspecific, and the stakeholder still cannot tell which work could be automated and which cannot.
  • WrongOVERSPECCommit to full automation once accuracy improves past a threshold. It promises a future guarantee on decisions whose obstacle is accountability, which accuracy does not remove.
Say it backA sponsor wants one accuracy figure for a system that scores 94% on standard documents and 61% on complex ones, and asks why the last 20% still needs staff. What do you give, and what do you name?

Give the two segment figures, say how and on what set they were measured, and add the end-to-end outcome with the human review step, so the sponsor can budget. For the last 20%, name the decisions that need a named person to attest or that the system cannot verify, and say what automating each would risk. Do not cite model imperfection in general.
