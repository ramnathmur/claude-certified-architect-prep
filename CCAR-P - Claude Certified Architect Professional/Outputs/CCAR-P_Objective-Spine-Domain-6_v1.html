A claims director wants routing "as accurately as possible"

A misrouted claim costs a mean 1.4 days of cycle time against an 11% baseline nobody has formally measured. 78% of volume is two clean claim types; the rest is a long tail. Five objectives turn that sentence into a signed commitment, a documented decision, and a handover somebody else can run without you.

6.1Bound the requirement before you design

Three questions turn the ask into a specification. What happens today when a claim is misrouted, and what does it cost: a mean 1.4 days. What accuracy do handlers achieve now: roughly 11%, never formally measured. What is the input mix: 78% two clean claim types, 22% a long tail of forty edge types. The sponsor signs a target, a method, an evaluation set and a stated error cost.

A requirement that cannot be falsified cannot be designed against. "Fast," "accurate," "as good as a human" describe nothing a design can be shown to have met or missed. Quantifying precedes benchmarking, and it precedes design.

The trapRunning a quick evaluation first produces a number nobody can interpret, because only the cost of an error says whether it is good enough. "First" in an exam stem is usually load-bearing.

6.2Segment the report before you headline it

In the steering meeting the sponsor asks for one number. The answer gives three: 94% on the standard segment, 61% on the complex tail, 91% end-to-end with human triage against 89% today, all measured on unseen claims scored by senior handlers. Mean routing time falls from 4.2 hours to 11 minutes. Asked why the tail cannot also be automated, the answer names the three queues carrying a regulatory-reporting obligation.

Where performance varies sharply by case type, the aggregate is a weighted average over a mix that will change. Both failure directions are tested: the flattering aggregate, and refusing to quantify at all.

The COO's planA staffing plan built on 87% breaks when the tail grows to 35% of volume: the aggregate falls to about 82%, and nothing about the system changed. The mix moved.

6.3Name the population, or the number cannot hold

The commitment: routing accuracy at least 90%, on standard motor and household claims only, scored quarterly on a 400-claim stratified sample by two senior handlers, with the complex tail explicitly excluded and routed to human triage. The handler-override log feeds the evaluation set fortnightly, so a recurring pattern reaches the set before the next quarterly score.

An accuracy commitment is a promise about the customer's future input distribution, which the provider does not control. A flat figure with no stated population cannot be kept once the mix moves. Name the population, the method, the cadence, and what is excluded.

Both wrong turnsA flat 95% promises data nobody controls. Refusing to commit at all pushes every performance risk onto the client. The signable middle names all four parts.

6.4Record the constraint, not only the choice

ADR-014 chooses the mid-tier model, low-confidence cases to triage. The frontier model scored 1.3 points higher and cost 2.1 extra seconds and roughly £18,000 a month at 40,000 requests a day. Constraints: p95 under 6 seconds, no labelled training corpus, the complex tail already has a human path for regulatory reasons. Revisit trigger: volume below 15,000 a day, or the latency ceiling relaxed.

Trade-offs are stable; constraints are the part that expires. A record without its constraints is a decision that can never safely be reopened. Add the evaluation numbers behind it, not only the choice.

Eighteen months laterThe ceiling relaxes to 12 seconds. A well-written ADR lets a successor check the recorded constraint in ten minutes. A design document alone forces a full re-derivation from scratch.

6.5Owe the boundary a list, and a way to detect drift

The handover carries the evaluation suite, per-segment criteria, an override-rate trigger, a named owner with a deputy, and the re-run routine. Six months later complaints rise while the error rate stays flat. The override log shows the increase concentrated in one region that changed its claim form. The suite is re-run, the new form's cases join the evaluation set, and ADR-014's revisit trigger is checked: volume is still above 15,000 a day, so the model decision stands.

Source code and a demonstration let a team run the system, not tell whether it is still right. A 1-in-500 failure invisible in a 200-case pilot is dozens of events a day at scale. Rare is only rare relative to volume.

Why flat is not fineOperational telemetry cannot see distribution shift; outputs stay fluent while they get quietly wrong. The override rate is the cheapest early signal, and it is already being generated.

One carve-out runs through all five. The complex tail identified in discovery (6.1) is why the aggregate needed segmenting in 6.2, why the SLA in 6.3 names its population and excludes that tail by name, why ADR-014 in 6.4 lists it as a constraint that already has a human path, and why the override log in 6.5 is what catches it when a regional form change shifts the mix underneath the same carve-out.
