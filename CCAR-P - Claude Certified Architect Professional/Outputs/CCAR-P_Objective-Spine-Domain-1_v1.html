A regional insurer, 8,000 claims a month

The business wants claims cycle time down. Today: 8,000 first-notice-of-loss claims a month, 6.4 days to a first decision, 40 handlers. Denials require a licensed adjuster; approvals under $2,500 do not. A 24-hour acknowledgement commitment is missed 30% of the time. Six objectives, one system, decided in order.

1.1Translate the outcome into a decision

The outcome is cycle time. Decomposing a handler's work locates four decisions: intake extraction from a mixed bundle of forms, photos and emails; coverage verification against the policy; severity triage; and approve or deny. The first three automate or assist. Denial stays human.

A business states an outcome. You find the recurring decision inside the workflow that a model can own. Translation almost always produces a split across the steps rather than a yes or no on the whole workflow.

What decided itNot difficulty. A regulatory clause in the setup paragraph: denials require a licensed adjuster. Professional-tier items hide the disqualifying constraint in the setup, not in the options.

1.2Wire the decision into a system

Claim bundles arrive by email or portal. Attachments are extracted, anything unparseable routes to a human queue rather than proceeding empty, and the policy number is validated before any model call. Output is a JSON contract carrying coverage_status, severity_tier, confidence, citations and prompt_version, built for two consumers: the routing service and the adjuster.

A feedback loop is closed only when the signal reaches something that changes behaviour. Here that is three signals with three destinations: severity overrides into the eval set, edit distance into the prompt, reopen rate into the routing thresholds.

Exam shapeA deployed system, accuracy flat for six months, two reviewers correcting output daily. The corrections are being made and thrown away. The cheapest fix captures the override event; it does not buy a larger model.

1.3Pick the cheapest rung that holds

A standard auto claim runs five steps, every time. That is a workflow. A complex liability claim with multiple parties is not enumerable, because the number of parties, documents and lookups is discovered during the work. Those route to an agent, behind a validation gate that decides which population a claim belongs to.

The discriminator is enumerability: can you write down, in advance, the set of paths a request might take? The ladder is a price list, not a maturity model. Move right only when a stated requirement makes the cheaper rung impossible.

Tested both waysDrilling only "do not over-engineer" loses the mirror item, where the scenario says the path cannot be known in advance and the workflow option quietly drops the requirement that made it non-enumerable.

1.4Give the hard path a coordinator

The complex path runs a coordinator with three specialists: document analysis, policy interpretation, precedent lookup. They dispatch in parallel from a single coordinator turn, because they are independent and the 24-hour acknowledgement commitment makes wall clock a stated requirement.

The coordinator owns decomposition, context passing, error handling and termination. It partitions before delegating, passes findings as structured content plus metadata so the summary can cite the clause it relied on, and checks for gaps before accepting.

Root-cause reflexWhen every subagent's output was individually correct and the report still has holes, the fault is partitioning, upstream of all of them. Adding subagents runs the same bad partition harder.

1.5Split on the seams that matter

Three splits, on three different criteria. A functional split along the pipeline. A confidence-tier split routing simple auto claims down the cheap path. And a risk-tier split putting every denial and every approval above $2,500 behind a human gate, regardless of confidence.

Split until each unit has one evaluation criterion and one failure mode. Stop when coordination overhead exceeds the benefit. Both halves are examinable, and over-decomposition is a real item: every split adds a handoff, and every handoff loses what was implicit in the shared context.

Where the seam came fromRegulation set the third one, not engineering judgement. Confidence-tier is the cost lever; risk-tier is where governance meets architecture.

1.6State it in the unit the business measures

To the COO: 6.4 days to a projected 1.8, with 24-hour acknowledgement moving from 70% to a designed 98%. To the CFO: about $0.31 per standard claim and $1.20 per complex one, against $47 in current handling cost. To the Chief Risk Officer: no denial issued without a licensed adjuster, every coverage determination citing the clause it relied on, an audit trail carrying the prompt version.

Five pillars, five different units, and the one the scenario names decides which architecture is correct. A stated SLA is a filter applied to the option set before comparison, not a tiebreaker reached for once two designs look close.

The trapChoosing the technically superior design that fails the named pillar. Its accuracy figure will be real, and it will already be disqualified.

Follow one thread back. The regulatory constraint found in 1.1 set the risk-tier seam in 1.5, which set the human gate in 1.2's output routing, which is the sentence that satisfies the Chief Risk Officer in 1.6. That chain is what "end-to-end architecture" means, and it is what the exam is testing when it buries a constraint in the setup paragraph.
