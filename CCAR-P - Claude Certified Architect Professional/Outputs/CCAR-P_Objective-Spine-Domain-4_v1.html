4.1Decide the threshold before the score exists

Six metrics, with thresholds agreed with the business sponsor before anyone knows what the system will score. That is the only time the conversation is honest, which is the entire reason for the sequencing. Determination accuracy clears 0.95 on every claim type, grounding is a hard 1.00, escalation recall is 0.98, and PII containment is zero leaked identifiers out of 12,000 outputs.

A real metric names a specific failure, a denominator, a segment, and a threshold set before the result was known. Grounding and PII are allowed to be hard zeros because a deterministic check enforces them; safety and accuracy are not.

What decided itSetting the bar after the scores come in has not set a bar, it has described what you got. An aggregate accuracy figure with no per-type clause lets a rare claim type fail 40% of the time unnoticed.

4.2Stratify for the question you must answer

400 cases pulled from real submitted claims, hand-verified by an adjuster. They are stratified so that the 2% that are handwritten remittance advices get real coverage. That 2% carries 30% of the actual risk, which is the whole argument against sampling at natural frequency: at one case, a segment cannot report a failure rate at all.

An eval set is a measuring stick, and a stick only works if it stays the same length. Real production traces first, synthetic second, hand-written only for shapes you expect but have not seen. Twenty to fifty hand-verified cases beat several hundred unverified ones.

The masking trapAn aggregate accuracy figure can hide a 40% failure rate on a rare segment. Every segment has to clear its bar independently before oversight is reduced.

4.3Run every change through all three gates

Every prompt edit runs the regression set, then a declared A/B, then a staged rollout. No exceptions, including for changes that look obviously safe. A prompt has no type system, no compiler, and no unit of change smaller than its entire behaviour, because the model conditions on all of it at once.

Each gate's blind spot is the next gate's entire reason for existing. Regression cannot see live-traffic behaviour; the A/B cannot see a seasonal pattern or the Monday spike. Change one variable at a time, and randomize at the user, never the request.

Exam shapeA variant bundling a prompt, model, and retrieval-depth change together can win cleanly and still teach you nothing: three suspects, one result, and nothing safely removable later.

4.4Diagnose retrieval before the model

The diagnosis order runs as designed. Retrieval first, and retrieval has in fact collapsed following a corpus refresh that broke table chunking. The model is never touched. The fix is a chunking change, verified against the regression set. The newly discovered failure mode becomes twelve new cases in the eval set: production just taught evaluation something its authors did not know a month ago.

Every layer upstream poisons everything downstream, so diagnose data and retrieval first, then prompt and context, then model mismatch. The cheapest check is also the most likely cause, so the order costs nothing to follow.

The clueConfidently wrong right after a corpus refresh or re-index points at retrieval, not the model. A bigger model would only phrase the same wrong passage more articulately.

4.5Reorder the prefix before you cut it

Profiling shows the policy preamble dominates both cost and time-to-first-token, so it is reordered and cached. A separate proposal to also trim it by 5,000 tokens is rejected outright. The removed sections are required by regulation, and the quality bar from week one is not something you renegotiate for a cost saving.

Measure which stage dominates before optimizing anything, and reach for caching first: it buys cost and latency together without spending quality. A prefix has to be byte-identical and genuinely first, or the cache buys nothing.

The trapTrimming "the parts we rarely need" produces a bigger number on a slide and removes required policy content. That is a compliance regression wearing an optimization's clothes.

4.6Log what lets you attribute a drift

Continuously, underneath all of it: dashboards tracking accuracy by claim type, escalation rate, cost per claim, cache-hit rate, and grader-versus-human agreement over time. Every surfaced failure is a candidate for the next version of the eval set. Model identifier and prompt version on every request is the field that turns a week of archaeology into a four-minute diagnosis.

An eval set tells you what happened on cases you chose. Monitoring tells you what is happening on traffic you did not. Attribution is the organizing requirement: a metric moving is useful only if you can say what moved it.

The patternCost and latency moving together while quality stays flat points at the token or caching layer. Cost improving while quality drops points at something removed from context, not the model.

The loop closes at 4.6 and reopens at 4.2. Week one's thresholds in 4.1 are what the regression gate in 4.3 checks against. The diagnosis in 4.4 does not end with a fix: the failure mode it finds becomes twelve new cases in the eval set from 4.2, so 4.4's incident is 4.2's next version. Nothing in the year depends on how the system "feels." Every decision traces to a number and the layer that produced it.
