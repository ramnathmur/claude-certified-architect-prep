Every one of the five objectives here is a version of one question: where does a property of this system actually come from? A model is a conditional probability distribution over text. Given a well-phrased instruction it will usually comply, not always — enough to build extraordinary products on, not enough to found a guarantee on. Governance is the discipline of guarantees.

Anything that must tend to happen can live in the prompt. Anything that must always happen has to live somewhere deterministic.

That distinction is the discriminator behind most of what this domain tests. Guardrails convert a tendency into a boundary. Failure modes are the catalogue of what breaks when you mistake one for the other. Human-in-the-loop is the mechanism for decisions no automated control can settle alone. Compliance is the case where an external authority already decided a property is a guarantee and you don't get a vote. Ethics is the case where nobody wrote the property down yet and the architect has to name it. A Foundations answer names the right control; a Professional answer states what it costs, what it doesn't cover, and how you'd explain it to the person signing the contract.

1. Guardrails and the four layers

A guardrail is a constraint that holds independently of what the model generates. Put the constraint in the prompt and it's subject to the same probabilistic process it's meant to restrain — it can be argued with, out-competed by a longer instruction elsewhere in context, or simply skipped on the tail of the distribution. Independence from the model's output is the entire point, and it gives four layers rather than four alternatives, because each one fails differently.

Input validation, before the call, catches what can be decided without the model at all: schema checks, rate limits, policy classification, screening retrieved content for injected instructions. Output filtering, after the call, catches what the model actually produced, and its limit is baked into its timing — if generating the content was itself the harm, filtering is already too late. Scoped permissions is the strongest layer, because it doesn't constrain behavior, it removes the possibility: a model can't misuse a capability it doesn't have. And human approval on consequential actions closes the set.

Why layer rather than perfect one control: the failure modes are uncorrelated. Input validation fails on novel phrasing it wasn't written for. Output filtering fails on content that's harmful but well-formed. Permission scoping fails when a legitimate capability is used toward an illegitimate end. Human review fails to inattention. An attack, or an ordinary accident, has to clear all four independently — and the probability of that is the product of each layer's failure rate, not the sum.

The reflex worth having cold

The exam guide's own sample material states this flatly: least privilege means removing an unneeded capability, not logging its use or asking for confirmation before it runs. That's one of the very few pieces of verified answer logic this project holds directly from the guide, and it generalizes well past tool configuration. A log tells you the boundary was already crossed.

Worked example

An insurance carrier's claims assistant reads a claim, drafts an adjuster note, and originally could issue payment directly. The design that survives review removes issue_payment from its tool list entirely; the assistant emits a structured recommendation instead, and a separate settlement service — holding the credential the model can never reach — validates and executes. The claim narrative is wrapped as untrusted data rather than instructions, with bank-account and national-ID patterns tokenized before assembly. Output is schema-validated and every cited policy clause is checked against the actual document. Recommendations above $5,000, and every recommendation to deny, route to a named adjuster.

Notice what the strongest control actually did. It didn't teach the model to use issue_payment responsibly — it deleted the capability and moved the decision into code that already existed to make it. The entire failure class of "the model issued a payment it shouldn't have" disappeared rather than got mitigated.

Why the weaker answers still tempt

A strengthened system prompt is fast, demos well, and produces a measurable improvement in testing — a tendency going from 97% to 99.4% is still a tendency. Logging and alerting presented as a control is genuinely valuable observability that acts after the fact, so on any "how do you prevent this" item, an option that detects rather than prevents is wrong-axis. Requiring confirmation before a dangerous call is a half-move, better than nothing and worse than removal, and it fails the moment the confirming party is itself an automated pipeline. And one sophisticated layer will always read as more impressive than four modest ones — but boring checks that fail independently beat one elegant check that fails alone.

2. Diagnosing what actually broke

Two root facts generate the entire failure catalogue here: the model samples from a distribution over plausible continuations, and instructions and data arrive through the same channel. Confabulation follows directly from the first — plausibility correlates with truth without being identical to it, and when they diverge the output is fluent, specific, and wrong, with the fluency itself carrying zero information about correctness. A model's stated confidence is never a calibrated signal for the same reason: "I'm highly confident" is generated by the exact process that generated the claim it's attached to. Retrieval doesn't fix this, it just changes what the model conditions on — feed it a stale chunk and it reports the contents faithfully and confidently, which is why a RAG system turning confidently wrong right after a document refresh points at retrieval and indexing, not the model. The timing is the evidence.

The shared-channel fact produces prompt injection, and the indirect case is the serious one: hostile text sitting inside a retrieved page, a PDF, a ticket body, never typed by the user at all. This is architecture, not a bug with a patch, which is why the fix is structural — treat retrieved content as untrusted and keep privilege out of the model's reach, rather than filtering phrasings. Long context adds its own mechanical failure: material buried mid-document gets used less reliably than material at the start or end, and the fix is positional and structural, not simply shortening the input, since shortening risks cutting exactly the content that was being missed.

The one that surprises architects most

Per-step accuracy compounds multiplicatively in an agentic chain: a ten-step agent at 96% per step lands around 0.96¹⁰ ≈ 66% end to end, and every single step passes its own unit test. A hospital's clinical documentation pipeline shows this at real scale — retrieve, extract, reconcile, draft, check against formulary, produce a patient-facing version, six model-touching steps, each measured at 96% after tuning. End to end that's 0.96⁶ ≈ 78%, and nothing in per-step testing looked broken. A better model moves each step a point or two and moves the product less than you'd hope. The fix is to interrupt the compounding: validate every extracted lab value against the structured source deterministically right after extraction, and validate against the actual formulary database rather than trusting the model's recollection of it right after that check. Two deterministic checkpoints turn six probabilistic steps into two short chains with a hard boundary between them.

The same multiplication runs in the opposite direction back in Objective 1's four guardrail layers: there, each layer's failure rate multiplies, so defense-in-depth is this exact widget's math working for you instead of against you. One mediocre layer barely helps; four independent layers compound into a very small breach probability, the same way four independent pipeline steps compound into a large failure probability.

Two habits cost points here. Attributing every failure to the model and reaching for a bigger one requires no diagnosis and is easy to justify to a sponsor, but it converts a fixable engineering problem into a permanent cost increase and does nothing at all for a retrieval fault. And treating confabulation or injection as something a better prompt fixes reduces the rate without creating the guarantee a genuinely required control needs.

3. Spending the review budget

Human review is scarce, expensive, and imperfect — treat it as a budget to allocate, not a switch to flip. Two variables decide the allocation. Consequence is what it costs if the output is wrong, and reversibility matters more than raw magnitude: a draft a person reads before sending is recoverable, a payment issued or a record deleted is not. Confidence is how likely this specific item is to be wrong, and it has to come from somewhere genuinely independent of the model — an independent verifier, a retrieval score, schema validation, a calibrated classifier, a plain business rule. The model's own stated confidence never qualifies, since it's text produced by the same process being assessed.

Cross the two variables and you get the routing logic the exam actually wants:

Routing by confidence and consequence
Low consequenceHigh consequence
High confidenceAuto-approve, with continuous samplingHuman approves, always
Low confidenceAuto-approve or queue, by costHuman approves, always

The high-consequence row is identical on purpose. Past a consequence threshold, confidence stops being the routing variable at all, because the human's job there is accountability, not accuracy — someone has to own the decision. Four properties separate a real review layer from a decorative one: the reviewer sees the source evidence beside the conclusion, not just the conclusion, since a reviewer shown only a fluent output approves it; every approve/reject/edit decision gets captured as labeled data, because the edits are the most valuable dataset the system will ever produce; the auto-approved stream is sampled continuously on a fixed schedule as the actual calibration signal for whether the threshold still holds; and review capacity is a stated number, not an assumption.

Worked example

A claims carrier at 12,000 claims a day shows the full policy. Claims under $500 with agreeing independent verification auto-settle — about 70% of volume, no human. Claims from $500 to $5,000, or any disagreement between the model and the independent check, route to an adjuster who sees the claim, the extracted fields, and the disagreement: about 25% of volume, 200 reviewer-hours a day at four minutes each, roughly 25 full-time reviewers. Anything above $5,000, and every denial regardless of confidence, always goes to a human, because denial is what the customer experiences and what a regulator asks about. Two percent of the auto-settled stream gets audited weekly as the control signal; if that error rate climbs, the $500 threshold comes down.

The Professional-tier move is naming that 25-reviewer cost explicitly and naming the two levers that shrink it: raising the auto-settle threshold, which raises the unsupervised error rate, or improving the independent verifier, engineering work with an uncertain payoff. Stating the trade with a number attached is the actual architecture; a routing diagram without it is half a design.

The traps in this objective

"Review everything at launch, then relax as confidence grows" sounds prudent and staged, and it's the classic architected trap — no exit criterion, no capacity plan, no defined measurement that would ever justify relaxing it. If a scenario gives you a volume figure at all, an option requiring review of all of it is almost always wrong, and the figure is there precisely so you can rule it out. Random sampling as the routing mechanism is statistically respectable and the wrong tool, since uniform sampling encounters rare, risky cases exactly in proportion to their rarity — sampling belongs on the auto-approved stream as monitoring, not on the whole stream as routing. And routing on the model's self-reported confidence is the purest wrong-axis distractor in the domain, because the option will literally use the word "confidence" and so will the correct answer.

4. Compliance as an architecture problem

A regulation is an obligation that has to hold regardless of how the system behaves on any given day, and "regardless" can only ever be enforced architecturally. Every compliance scenario reduces to three questions: where is the boundary between systems you control and systems you don't, what exactly crosses it field by field, and what proves it a year later to an auditor. The pattern that answers most of these scenarios runs in a fixed order: classify which fields are regulated before assembling anything, de-identify or tokenize at the boundary so the narrative goes out but the identifier doesn't, re-associate locally once the response returns, log the crossing immutably, and bound retention to a stated purpose and duration.

Compare that to an instruction like "do not repeat any patient identifiers" — it's evaluated by the model after the identifiers are already in the request, meaning the transmission the regulation actually cares about has already happened, and it depends on the model complying.

The sentence worth having ready

A required control cannot rest on probabilistic behavior. High accuracy, a documented residual risk, and a more capable model are three different-sounding wrong answers that all fail the same way: none of them converts a tendency into a guarantee.

What each regime forces into the design

GDPR forces a lawful purpose stated before collection, with processing confined to it — a prompt built for support triage can't quietly become a marketing dataset — plus data minimization, erasure that has to reach every derived store including logs, eval datasets, and vector indexes (an embedding is still derived from the person's record), and constraints on where processing physically happens. HIPAA forces a choice between de-identifying PHI before it leaves the covered boundary or bringing the processor inside it under the right agreement, plus "minimum necessary" and audit trails over PHI access — and it's worth keeping de-identified and pseudonymized straight, since the second keeps a re-identification key that has to stay inside the boundary either way. FedRAMP forces the processing environment itself to be authorized at the right impact level, which is a procurement fact rather than a configuration setting: the deployment topology you'd otherwise pick may simply be unavailable, and no configuration makes an unauthorized environment compliant.

Worked example

A hospital's discharge-summary drafter shows the pattern end to end. The EHR and hospital VPC are in scope; the inference endpoint is outside it. Name, medical record number, and provider identifiers get tokenized and dates get shifted by a per-patient offset before the clinical narrative is sent, with the token map living in a store that has no network path out. The draft returns, gets re-associated locally, and a clinician signs before anything enters the chart. De-identified request/response pairs retain for thirty days under a stated incident-investigation purpose; the token map follows the EHR's own retention policy and never leaves the network. Every crossing writes an immutable record.

What got rejected: a system-prompt instruction not to repeat identifiers, and a plan to redact logs nightly. The first acts after transmission. The second fixes the record and not the transmission that already happened.

The prompt-level instruction is the single most attractive wrong answer in this whole domain, precisely because it's usually phrased in the regulation's own words, which makes it read as exactly responsive. A signed processing agreement is a close second trap — genuinely necessary, allocates liability, and does nothing to stop data crossing a boundary. Post-hoc log redaction fixes the record after the transmission already happened. And an enterprise tier or a no-training-on-your-data commitment is the subtlest trap of all, because everything said about it is true — it's just not the boundary control the question is asking for.

5. Fairness as a system property

Models train on data that encodes the distribution of past decisions, so where those decisions were skewed, the skew reappears — in fluent, confident prose, which makes it considerably harder to notice than a skewed coefficient. Fairness belongs to the whole system, not the model alone: the model, the eval data, the threshold, and the population it's applied to all contribute, and a model can stay unchanged while a system's fairness properties shift because the applicant pool moved.

An aggregate accuracy figure is a weighted average, and it hides exactly the disparity you're looking for. A screener at 91% overall can be 94% on the majority group and 78% on a minority one, invisible in the aggregate forever, which is why the eval set needs adequate sample size per group rather than population-proportional sampling — proportional sampling gives the smallest group the least statistical power, exactly backwards from where the risk sits.

Removing a protected attribute doesn't remove the signal, because postal code, school name, employment gaps, and phrasing register all carry it, and a capable model recovers the association without being told — blindness isn't fairness, and it destroys your ability to detect the disparity you were trying to avoid. Fairness definitions genuinely conflict when base rates differ: equal selection rates, equal false-negative rates, and equal calibration can't all hold simultaneously, so the design has to declare which one it's holding itself to, in terms of which harm it's controlling, as a written stakeholder decision rather than a default setting.

Two different things called "transparency"

Disclosure is the affected person knowing AI was involved and how to reach a human. Explainability is being able to account for a specific decision after the fact. Explainability is served by a reconstructable trace — inputs, retrieved evidence, model and prompt versions, score, threshold, the rule that fired — and it is not served by asking the model to explain itself, since a generated rationale is plausible text about the decision rather than a record of what produced it.

Worked example

A recruiting screener shows the shape: a held-out set stratified with equal N per demographic category rather than proportional sampling, deliberately including near-pairs identical in substance but differing in a proxy signal like school name. The declared metric is false-negative-rate parity, chosen because the harm being controlled is a qualified candidate never reaching a human, written down because someone will ask why this metric in twelve months. The threshold is set against the worst-performing group's error rate rather than the mean, which costs throughput on purpose. Every decision persists the fields used, rubric version, model version, score, and the human decision that followed — that trace is the explainability artifact, distinct from disclosure, which is telling candidates AI is used and giving them a route to request human review. Subgroup metrics get recomputed monthly against live traffic, since a fairness result is a measurement, not a fixed property.

Watch for removing the protected attribute presented as the fix — it feels principled and is often even legally encouraged as a floor, and it fails for the reason above. A model-generated explanation attached to each decision reads beautifully for a demo and is generated text, not evidence. A single aggregate fairness score as a release gate is better than nothing while obscuring which definition is being enforced and which group absorbs the error. And a fairness statement added to the system prompt is the same category mistake as every other prompt-level control in this domain: a tendency, standing in for what the question actually required, which was a measurement.

6. One regulated system, all five objectives

A European bank deploys an assistant drafting hardship-plan offers for customers in arrears: it reads account history and correspondence, proposes a restructuring offer, drafts the letter. All five objectives have a claim on it at once. EU personal data is in scope. The offers are consequential decisions reaching individuals. The assistant touches a system that can amend an account. Volume rules out reviewing everything. The correspondence it reads is third-party text, making it untrusted input.

The payload is arrears history, account terms, and stated circumstances, not the full customer record; name, account number, and national ID are tokenized before the call and re-associated locally, processing runs against an EU-region endpoint, and logs from this purpose are walled off from the marketing pipeline as an access control rather than a policy statement. The assistant holds no tool that can amend an account; it emits a structured offer object that a separate service validates against term limits and eligibility, holding the only credential that matters, with correspondence wrapped as untrusted data so the eligibility decision never depends on instructions hidden inside it. Confabulated policy terms are caught by validating every cited term against the actual policy database; injection is neutralized by the plain absence of privilege; version drift is handled by pinning the model and running a 200-case regression set before any change; a deterministic validation step in the middle interrupts compounding across the pipeline's model-touching steps.

Every declined offer, and every offer above a defined concession value, goes to a case handler regardless of confidence, because those are the outcomes a customer feels and a regulator asks about; offers inside standard bands where the model and validator agree auto-issue, with 2% audited weekly and the reviewer's screen showing arrears history, the specific policy clause, and the proposed terms side by side. Decline-rate parity is measured monthly across the bank's accountable subgroups on a stratified set, chosen because wrongful decline is the harm being controlled, and every decision carries a full trace.

The trades are where the Professional tier actually lives, and none of them has a context-free correct answer. Tightening the human-review band buys accountability and costs case-handler headcount — that number belongs in the business case. Aggressive minimization improves the compliance posture and can strip context that mattered to offer quality. Longer log retention helps fairness auditing and enlarges the erasure surface. Pinning the model version protects against drift and defers capability gains. The architect's job is surfacing each trade with a number attached, not resolving it silently.

And the sponsor who isn't an engineer needs the whole design in five plain sentences: the assistant can't change an account, it proposes and the bank's existing rules engine decides; customer identifiers never leave the bank's systems; every decline is seen by a person; outcomes are checked across customer groups every month; the reasoning behind any individual offer can be shown. Being able to say those five sentences without jargon is itself a tested competence, one this domain shares with its neighbor on stakeholder communication.

The one question behind every wrong answer

Nearly every wrong answer across this whole domain reduces to the same mistake stated five different ways: a prompt instruction, a documented residual risk, a bigger model, a self-reported confidence score, an aggregate accuracy figure — each one sounds like it addresses the requirement, and each one is a tendency standing in for a guarantee. Before picking an answer, ask whether the control holds regardless of what the model happens to generate that day.

Common misconceptions

The mythLeast privilege means logging or confirming when a risky tool is used.
Why it's temptingLogging and confirmation are real, checkable practices, and they show up in every security checklist — keeping the capability and watching it feels responsible.
The realityLeast privilege means removing the capability the system doesn't need. A log tells you the boundary was already crossed — it doesn't prevent the crossing.
The one testDoes the scenario say the capability isn't needed? If yes, the answer removes it — logging or confirming it is the designated wrong answer. (The same reflex Domain 3's authorization objective tests.)
The mythWe're at 99.2% accuracy, so we meet the compliance requirement.
Why it's tempting99.2% sounds like a finished, defensible number, and it's the kind of figure a sponsor wants to hear.
The realityA percentage is a tendency. A required control needs a mechanism that cannot fail probabilistically — code, permissions, or a person, not a score.
The one testDoes the requirement use a word like "always," "never," or "must"? If so, no accuracy figure, however high, satisfies it on its own.
The myth91% accuracy overall means the system works for everyone.
Why it's temptingAn aggregate figure sounds complete and is easy to report in one line to a stakeholder.
The realityAn aggregate is a weighted average that hides subgroup failure — 94% for the majority group can sit beside 78% for a minority one. Measure by subgroup, with adequate sample size per group.
The one testHas the number been broken out by the groups the organization is accountable for? If not, the 91% hasn't earned its confidence yet. (The same masking pattern Domain 4's evaluation objective tests.)
