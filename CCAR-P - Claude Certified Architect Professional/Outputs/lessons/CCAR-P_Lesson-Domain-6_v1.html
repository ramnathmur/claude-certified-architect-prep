Domain 6 is 14% of the blueprint. Domain 5 is another 14%. Together that is 28%, which is more than Integration at 19% and more than Solution Design at 17%.

There is no per-domain floor, so a weak Domain 6 does not fail you by itself. It quietly takes 14% of your ceiling with it.

Technically strong candidates lose marks here, and the reason is structural rather than careless. The vocabulary of this domain, discovery and alignment and expectations and handoff, is the vocabulary of soft skills, and soft skills feel like the part of an exam where several answers could be defended.

That intuition is wrong for this paper. A discovery question either establishes a testable requirement or it does not. A metric either informs the decision the stakeholder is about to make or it does not. A handover package either lets the receiving team detect degradation next year or it does not.

These are binary properties of an artifact, and the exam tests them as such.

The through-line

All five objectives are one discipline seen at five moments.

An AI system's behaviour is probabilistic and drifting, so every commitment made about it must be bounded, measured, and re-checkable.

1. Structured discovery

By the end of this section you will be able to name the four parts a requirement needs, and you will notice that "first" in an exam stem is usually load-bearing.

Discovery exists to convert a stakeholder's stated want into a specification an engineer can build against and a tester can verify.

It has one failure mode: a requirement that cannot be falsified. "Fast," "accurate," "reliable," "as good as a human." None of these can be designed against, because no design can be shown to have met them and no design can be shown to have missed them.

Three things change when the system being specified is an LLM system.

Acceptance criteria become statistical. A deterministic system either produces the right output or it has a bug. An LLM system has an accuracy distribution.

So the requirement is not "the system classifies claims correctly" but "the system reaches at least 92% top-label accuracy on a held-out set of 400 claims, stratified to match last quarter's claim mix." Every word there is doing work: the number, the sample, the stratification, and the fact that the set is held out.

The cost of an error becomes a design input. Errors in a probabilistic system are certain; only their rate and direction are negotiable.

Establishing what a false positive costs and what a false negative costs is what determines whether the design biases toward recall or precision, where human review sits, and what the confidence threshold should be. A sponsor who cannot tell you what a wrong answer costs has not yet given you enough to design a system.

The input distribution has to be interrogated, not assumed. Ask what fraction of inputs are the clean standard case the sponsor has in mind, and what the long tail actually contains. Almost every unpleasant surprise in an AI deployment is a tail nobody characterised during discovery.

Worked example

An insurer's claims director asks for "an AI that reads incoming claims and routes them to the right handler, as accurately as possible."

Three questions turn that into a specification, and each one is asked for a reason.

"When a claim is routed to the wrong handler today, what happens next and what does it cost?" This establishes the error cost and reveals whether errors self-correct. A misrouted claim spotted by the receiving handler in ten minutes tolerates a much higher error rate than one sitting in a queue for four days.

"What accuracy do your handlers achieve on this routing today, and how do you know?" This establishes the baseline and, more usefully, reveals whether anyone measures it. Often nobody does, which means the real requirement is not "beat the humans" but "be measurable at all," and that is a materially different engagement.

"Of last quarter's claims, what proportion were the standard motor and household types, and what was in the remainder?" This characterises the input distribution. If 78% are two clean categories and the remaining 22% span forty edge types, the design and the pilot both have to be built around that split rather than around the 78%.

How the exam probes it

The scenario shape: a stakeholder states a requirement in unbounded or purely qualitative terms and the question asks what the architect should do first.

The key converts the requirement into something measurable before design proceeds. Several options will describe things a competent architect genuinely does, so the discriminator is sequence, and quantifying the requirement precedes all of them.

A subtler shape gives you a stakeholder who arrives with a solution already chosen, "we want a multi-agent system that...", and asks how to proceed. The key surfaces the underlying business problem and the decision the system is meant to serve, without either rejecting the idea outright or simply building it.

Checkpoint 1 · before you read on

A sponsor wants a document-summarisation system that is "at least as good as our analysts." A colleague proposes running a quick evaluation on 200 sample documents this week to see where a baseline model lands.

Is that the right first move? Say what the resulting number would and would not tell you.

Answer first, then open
No. The number arrives uninterpretable.

Suppose it comes back at 89%. Is that excellent or unusable? Nothing in the engagement can answer that yet, because only the cost of an error tells you, and nobody has established it.

What is missing before benchmarking is worth doing: the target the use case requires, the measurement method, the evaluation set, and what a wrong summary actually costs downstream. Also worth asking here: has anyone ever measured the analysts? Often not, which changes the engagement from "beat the humans" to "be measurable at all."

Why this is a good distractor: benchmarking is a real activity, it is fast, and it produces a number that feels like progress. The word "first" in an exam stem is doing exactly this work.

The wrong turns

Designing against the unbounded requirement anyway, aiming high. Choosing the strongest model and the most thorough pipeline "to maximise accuracy" feels responsible and fails on its own terms. There is no basis for declaring the system finished.

Benchmarking first. Attractive because it produces a number fast, and the number is uninterpretable without the error cost.

Escalating to the sponsor's superior, or refusing to start. Both appear as options and both look like the answer to an impasse. Establishing the requirement is your job, not an obstacle to it.

2. Communicating decisions and trade-offs

By the end of this section you will know both failure directions here, because the exam tests both and most people drill only one.

An architectural decision made by one person and understood by one person is a liability.

It cannot be challenged before it is expensive, it cannot be revisited when the constraint that drove it changes, and it cannot be defended when a stakeholder asks why the system costs what it costs. Communicating the decision is what makes it an organisational asset rather than a personal one.

The professional standard has three parts.

State the trade-off, not only the choice. Every architectural decision spends something to buy something. A stakeholder told only what was chosen has a conclusion without the reasoning, and will treat the next constraint change as a reason to distrust the architect rather than a reason to revisit the decision.

Report performance in the shape the system actually has. This is where AI systems break the usual consulting advice most sharply.

A single headline number is normally good communication practice. It is the pyramid principle working correctly. For a system whose performance varies sharply by case type, an aggregate is actively misleading, because it is a weighted average over a mix that will change.

Note the two failure directions, because the exam tests both.

A flattering aggregate misleads. And refusing to quantify, "accuracy varies, it depends on the case," fails the sponsor, who has a budget decision to make and cannot make it on a shrug.

Translate without diluting. Explaining a trade-off to a non-technical audience means changing the vocabulary, not softening the content.

"We chose the smaller model because latency at 40,000 daily requests is the binding constraint" becomes "at your volume, the more capable model would add about two seconds to every request and roughly £18,000 a month, and it improves accuracy on about 4% of cases." Same decision, same trade-off, priced in the units the audience owns.

How the exam probes it

The dominant scenario: an executive demands a single accuracy figure, or has been given one and is now planning against it, and performance varies by input type. Choose the option that segments by type, states the measurement basis, and reports the end-to-end outcome including human review.

A second shape asks why a system cannot simply be fully automated. The key names which specific decisions carry accountability or cannot be verified, and what automating them would risk.

Generic appeals to "AI isn't perfect" or "we need a human in the loop for safety" are the wrong register. They are true, unspecific, and give the stakeholder nothing to decide with. The useful answer sounds like this:

The register that works

"Three of the nine queues cover claims with a regulatory-reporting obligation. The system can identify the claim type, but it cannot attest that the reporting decision was made by an accountable person, and that attestation is what your regulator asks for. Automating those three would save about 4% of volume and would remove the audit trail on the part you are most examined on."

Checkpoint 2 · before you read on

A COO has built next year's staffing plan on "the system is 87% accurate." The complex tail is about to grow from 22% to 35% of volume because of a new product line.

What happens to the 87%, and what should have been said instead?

Answer first, then open
It falls to about 82%, and nothing about the system changed.

Work it: 0.65 × 94 + 0.35 × 61 is roughly 82.4. The model is performing exactly as it always did. The aggregate moved because the mix moved, which is precisely the instability that makes it the wrong number to plan against.

What should have been said: 94% on the standard segment, 61% on the complex tail, and 91% end-to-end with triage, all measured on unseen claims. The COO would then have known that a growing complex tail raises the triage queue rather than degrading the system, and would have staffed for it.

The damage is not the number, it is the surprise. The COO will experience this as the system getting worse, and no explanation offered after the fact will fully undo that.

The wrong turns

The flattering aggregate. Not dishonest in intent, and it is what the stakeholder asked for. It is wrong because the number is unstable and conceals the segment where the risk lives.

Declining to quantify. Attractive because it is technically defensible and feels appropriately humble. It leaves the sponsor unable to decide, which is a failure of the architect's role.

Reporting only the model metric. The sponsor is accountable for the process, not the component.

Explaining the mechanism instead of the trade-off. Describing how retrieval-augmented generation works to a CFO who asked why the system costs £30,000 a month is a category error, and a common one among strong engineers.

3. Feedback loops and SLAs

By the end of this section you will be able to write an accuracy commitment that is signable, and you will know which of its four parts everyone omits.

Expectations about an AI system decay in one direction: toward believing it is more capable, more consistent and more finished than it is.

Demonstrations show the good case. Early usage is on the clean subset. Every conversation that does not actively correct the picture lets it drift optimistic.

A feedback loop is the mechanism that keeps the stakeholder's mental model attached to the system's actual behaviour. Two components: the loop and the commitment.

The loop

It has to be structured to be useful. Ad hoc feedback, a channel where users post complaints or a monthly call where someone recounts a bad experience, produces anecdotes, and anecdotes cannot be prioritised because they carry no frequency.

A structured loop captures the input, the output, the expected output, and the reviewer, so a reported failure becomes a case that can be added to the evaluation set.

Feedback that does not become an evaluation case is entertainment.

The commitment

A conventional SLA commits to availability and latency, both properties of infrastructure and both under the provider's control.

A commitment about accuracy is different in kind, because accuracy depends on the input distribution, which the provider does not control. Committing to "95% accuracy" without qualification is a promise about the customer's future data, and you cannot keep it.

The analogy

An accuracy SLA without a stated population is a benchmark score with no test set named. You would never accept "our model gets 94%" without immediately asking on what, measured how. A flat contractual accuracy figure is that same unqualified claim, except that somebody has signed it and it is now enforceable.

Where it breaks A benchmark's test set is fixed, and it is yours to choose. An SLA's population is your customer's future data, which drifts on its own schedule and which you do not control. So naming the population is necessary and not sufficient: you also need the measurement cadence, because a population defined once and never re-sampled becomes wrong quietly. That extra requirement has no equivalent in benchmarking, and it is why the four-part form below has a "how often" column.

Worked example

A service commitment for the claims-routing system, written to be signed.

A sponsor reading that cannot later be surprised by the complex tail, which is the entire purpose.

The feedback loop behind it, in one sentence a client will accept: "Every claim a handler re-routes is logged with the original claim text, the system's assignment and the handler's correction; the operations lead reviews the log fortnightly and any recurring pattern is added to the evaluation set before the next quarterly scoring run."

Checkpoint 3 · before you read on

A client asks for a simple contractual line: "the system will be 95% accurate." Your colleague suggests refusing to commit to accuracy at all and offering availability and latency only, which is what the standard template covers.

Both of those are wrong. Say why each fails, and what replaces them.

Answer first, then open
One promises data you do not control. The other pushes all the risk onto the client.

The flat 95% is a promise about the customer's future input distribution. It closes the negotiation, which is why it is tempting, and you cannot keep it once their case mix moves.

Refusing entirely is technically safe and looks like discipline. It leaves the client unable to plan, and it moves the entire performance risk to the party least able to measure it.

What replaces both: commit to a number, on a named population, with a stated measurement method and cadence, and a stated exclusion list. "At least 90% on standard motor and household claims, scored quarterly on a 400-claim stratified sample by two senior handlers, excluding the complex tail which routes to human triage."

That is signable by both sides, which neither of the other two is.

4. Documentation and implementation guidance

By the end of this section you will know which part of a decision record expires, and why that is the part that gives the document a lifespan.

Documentation of an architecture serves a reader you have not met, at a moment you cannot predict, usually when something has changed.

That reader's question is almost never "what does this system do." They can read the code for that. It is "why is it like this, and can I change it?"

A document recording only the chosen design cannot answer that. The reader sees a component and has no way to distinguish a load-bearing constraint from an arbitrary preference, so they either change nothing out of fear or change something that mattered. Both cost more than writing the document properly would have.

Implementation guidance is the adjacent artifact, answering a different question. Not "why" but "how do I build this without rediscovering your reasoning."

It carries the interface contracts, the configuration that matters and why, the failure modes and what to do about each, and the tests that must pass. Prose describing the system is not implementation guidance.

Worked example

An ADR for the model choice on the claims-routing system, in the compact form that actually gets written.

ADR-014 · Model selection for claim classification

Status. Accepted, 2026-09-03. Supersedes nothing. Revisit if daily volume falls below 15,000 or the latency ceiling is relaxed.

Decision. Use the mid-tier model for claim classification, with low-confidence cases routed to human triage.

Alternatives considered. The frontier model for all claims: 88.0% on the evaluation set, but adds 2.1s median latency and roughly £18,000/month at projected volume. A fine-tuned smaller model: potentially cheaper, but requires a labelled corpus the client does not have and adds a retraining obligation. Two-stage routing with escalation: rejected for now, adds a failure surface for a 1.3-point gain, and recorded as the first thing to reconsider if the complex tail grows.

Trade-offs. Spends 1.3 points of accuracy on the evaluation set. Buys a 2.1s latency margin against the 6s p95 commitment and £18,000/month. The accuracy given up falls almost entirely in the complex tail, which routes to human triage regardless, so end-to-end impact is smaller than the model-level gap suggests.

Constraints that drove it. p95 response under 6s, contractually committed. Projected volume 40,000/day. No labelled training corpus available. Complex claims (22% of volume) already have a human triage path for regulatory reasons.

Two hundred words, and a successor in eighteen months can tell instantly whether the decision still holds.

How the exam probes it

Shape one: a team is documenting an architecture, or a stakeholder asks what the documentation should contain. The key includes alternatives, trade-offs and driving constraints alongside the decision. Distractors offer a thorough description of the chosen design, often with appealing extras like detailed diagrams and a full component inventory. More effort, missing the load-bearing content.

Shape two: a decision made a year ago is questioned because a constraint has changed. The key traces back to the recorded constraint and re-evaluates. Distractors re-derive from scratch, or defend on authority.

Shape three: a receiving team must implement or extend the system. The key supplies interface contracts, configuration rationale, failure modes and the tests that must pass. Distractors offer a walkthrough session or a narrative overview, neither of which survives the first personnel change.

Checkpoint 4 · before you read on

Eighteen months on, the latency commitment is renegotiated from 6 seconds to 12. A new architect asks whether the model choice should change.

What does a well-written ADR let them do in ten minutes, and what would a design document alone have forced?

Answer first, then open
Check the recorded constraint, find it has expired, and re-run the comparison.

ADR-014 names the p95 6-second ceiling as a driving constraint and names the frontier model's cost as 2.1 seconds and £18,000/month for 1.3 points. With the ceiling at 12 seconds, the latency objection is gone and the decision reduces to whether 1.3 points is worth £18,000/month, which is now a straightforward business question.

What a design document alone forces: a full re-derivation. Nobody knows the frontier model was ever considered, what it scored, or why it was rejected, so the evaluation gets run again from scratch, at cost, possibly reaching a different conclusion for reasons nobody can compare.

The general point: trade-offs are stable and constraints expire, so the constraint is the part that makes the record re-openable. Writing an explicit revisit trigger, as this one does, is the cheap version of the same idea.

5. Lifecycle phases

By the end of this section you will be able to say what a handover must contain for the receiving team to answer "is it still working?" without you.

Five phases are named in the objective, which means the exam expects you to know which phase a scenario is in and what the architect owes at that point.

Two of them carry most of the marks, and they are the two where something crosses a boundary.

Pilot to scale. A pilot succeeds under conditions the pilot itself created: a narrow user group, a curated or naturally clean input set, close support from the people who built it, and a volume low enough that edge cases have not been sampled.

Scaling changes all four at once. The architect's contribution at this moment is not a capacity plan. It is an explicit list of which assumptions held at small scale and may not hold at large scale. Cost and model choice follow from that list; they are not the starting point.

Do the arithmetic on the third assumption class, because it is the one people find counterintuitive. A 1-in-500 failure appears zero or one times in a 200-case pilot, which reads as an anomaly. At scale it is eighty events a day, and rare events are only rare relative to volume.

Delivery to operations. A handover that survives its first year is defined by what the receiving team can do without you, and specifically by whether they can answer "is it still working?" without your judgement.

The analogy

Handing over code and a demo is shipping a model with no validation set. Silent degradation here is distribution shift, and you already know that shift is invisible from the outputs alone: they stay fluent, well-formed and plausible. The only thing that ever caught it was holding out data and re-scoring, which is exactly what an evaluation suite plus a re-run routine is.

Where it breaks With a deployed model you usually get ground truth eventually, from conversions, refunds, downstream labels. Here the ground truth is a human's judgment about the right handler queue, and it may never be recorded anywhere unless the handover deliberately builds the override log that captures it. So the detection apparatus is not something you connect to existing data. It has to be constructed, and constructing it is part of the handover rather than something operations can add later.

Monitoring and iteration follow from that package. Monitoring here means two layers: operational telemetry, meaning latency, error rates, cost and volume, and quality telemetry, meaning evaluation scores, human-override rates and confidence distribution.

The second layer is what catches drift, and it is the layer teams skip, because the first is what their existing tooling already provides.

A rising human-override rate on stable input volume is the earliest honest signal that a system is degrading, and it costs nothing to collect, because the overrides are already happening.

Worked example

Handover package · transferred to Claims Operations, 2026-11-14

Evaluation suite. 400 stratified claims with senior-handler labels, plus 60 cases added from the pilot's override log. Runnable by one command; produces per-segment accuracy.

Criteria. Standard segment at or above 90% and complex segment at or above 55% are acceptable. Below either, or a human-override rate above 18% for two consecutive weeks, raises a defect and pauses any pending configuration change.

Routine. Suite re-run quarterly, and additionally within 10 working days of any model version change or claim-form change. Results tabled at the monthly operations review.

Owner. The Claims Operations Systems Lead, with a named deputy. The override log review is fortnightly and belongs to the same owner.

Guidance. Interface contracts, the confidence-threshold rationale (ADR-017), the four known failure modes with the response to each, and the regression tests that must pass before any prompt or model change ships.

The demonstration and the repository access are assumed. They are not what makes this a handover.

Checkpoint 5 · before you read on

Six months after go-live, user complaints are up noticeably. The error rate on the dashboard is flat, latency is flat, and no deployment has happened.

What do you check, and why is the flat error rate consistent with a real problem?

Answer first, then open
Input distribution shift. The error rate is operational telemetry and cannot see this.

A flat error rate means nothing is crashing or timing out, which is exactly what silent degradation looks like. The outputs are still well-formed and still returned successfully. They are just increasingly wrong, and nothing in the operational layer is scored against correctness.

What to check, in order: the human-override rate, which is the cheapest early signal and is already being generated; then re-run the evaluation suite; then look for what changed in the inputs, such as a new form version, a new region, or a new product line.

The distractors reach for a model upgrade or a prompt rewrite before establishing what changed. Both are real activities, and both skip the step that would tell you what to fix.

The wrong turns

Scaling as a capacity question. Rate limits and infrastructure are the visible constraint and the easiest to plan, so they absorb the attention. The input distribution is the constraint that actually bites, and it is invisible until it arrives.

Handing over code and a demonstration. This is what handover means in most software contexts and it feels complete. For a probabilistic system it omits the only mechanism by which the receiving team detects that anything has gone wrong.

Naming a team rather than a person as owner. A distributed owner is no owner, and this is the distractor that most often looks acceptable.

Treating go-live as the end of the engagement. Iteration is a named phase. A system whose evaluation set never grows after launch has one that is steadily diverging from what it actually processes.

6. One engagement, five phases

By the end of this section you will have watched the same operation performed on five different objects, which is what makes this domain learnable rather than a list.

Discovery. The claims director asks for routing "as accurately as possible." You do not design.

You establish that a misrouted claim costs a mean 1.4 days of cycle time, that the current human baseline is roughly 11% misrouting and has never been formally measured, and that 78% of volume is two clean claim types while 22% spans a long tail.

You write the requirement with a number, a measurement method, a held-out stratified evaluation set and a stated error cost. The sponsor signs it. Bounded.

Design. You evaluate two models against the 400-claim set. The frontier model wins by 1.3 points and loses on latency and cost at 40,000 requests a day.

You write ADR-014 with the decision, the three alternatives, the trade-off, the driving constraints and the condition under which the decision should be reopened. Recorded with its expiry conditions.

Communication. In the steering meeting the sponsor asks for one number. You give three: 94% on the standard segment, 61% on the complex tail, 91% end-to-end with human triage against 89% today, all measured on unseen claims scored by senior handlers.

When the sponsor asks why the tail cannot be automated too, you name the three queues carrying a regulatory-reporting obligation and explain that the system can identify the claim type but cannot attest that an accountable person made the reporting decision. Segmented, measured, specific.

Commitment. The SLA commits 99.5% availability, a 6-second p95 response, and at least 90% routing accuracy on the standard segment only, scored quarterly on the stratified sample by two senior handlers, with the complex tail explicitly excluded and routed to human triage.

The handler-override log feeds the evaluation set fortnightly. Qualified and re-checkable.

Pilot to scale. The pilot ran with 40 handlers on one region's claims. Before national rollout you table the assumptions at risk.

The input distribution widens across regions with different claim-form versions. The human triage queue at 12% of pilot volume becomes 4,800 claims a day at national volume, which is a staffing line item rather than a rounding error. The 1-in-500 malformed-attachment failure that appeared once in the pilot becomes dozens of events per day.

The cost model and the model-tier decision are then rebuilt on those numbers rather than extrapolated from the pilot. Assumptions before arithmetic.

Handoff. Claims Operations receives the evaluation suite, the per-segment thresholds, the override-rate trigger, a named systems lead with a named deputy, the quarterly-plus-on-change re-run routine, and the implementation guidance with four known failure modes. Detectable.

Iteration. Six months later, complaints rise while the error rate is flat. The override log shows the increase concentrated in one region that changed its claim form.

The suite is re-run, the new form's cases are added to the evaluation set, and ADR-014's revisit trigger is checked. Volume is still above 15,000/day, so the model decision stands. The prompt is adjusted for the new form and the regression tests pass. The loop closed because it was built to close.

The property worth naming

Every phase did the same thing to a different object: made a claim about a probabilistic system bounded, measured, and checkable again later. That is why the five objectives are learnable as one discipline rather than as five lists, and it is why an option that leaves something open-ended is nearly always the distractor.

Where these reflexes show up again

The evaluation suite meets Domain 4. Section 5's handover artifact is Domain 4's eval set, handed to somebody else with a routine attached. Same object, different moment.

The segmented report meets Domain 4's masking trap and Domain 5's fairness objective. An aggregate concealing a weak segment is the same defect in all three, and in all three the fix is to break the number out by the segments you are accountable for.

The SLA's population clause meets Domain 1's value pillars. A latency commitment eliminates architectural options before comparison starts, which is Domain 1 section 6, and it is the same commitment being written here.

Quick reference

The through-line. Every commitment about a probabilistic system must be bounded, measured and re-checkable. When an option makes something open-ended into something checkable, it is usually the key.

Discovery, in four parts. Target number, measurement method, evaluation set, cost of an error. Plus: characterise the input distribution including the tail.

Communicating performance, in three components. Break out by case type, state how it was measured, give the end-to-end outcome including human review. Both failure directions are tested: the flattering aggregate, and the refusal to quantify.

Metric choice. The metric tied to the decision the stakeholder actually faces, stated with what it does not cover.

Why not full automation. Name which specific decisions carry accountability or are unverifiable, and what automating them would risk. Never "AI is imperfect."

ADR, in four parts. Decision, alternatives considered, trade-offs, constraints that drove it. Add the evaluation numbers behind it and the condition that reopens it.

Implementation guidance. Interface contracts, configuration rationale, failure modes and responses, tests that must pass.
