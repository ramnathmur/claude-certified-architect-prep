Here is a meeting you have probably sat in. A team ships an assistant. It demos well, everyone is pleased, and about three months later somebody asks the obvious question: is it actually working?

The room goes quiet. One person says it feels better since the last prompt change. Another says support tickets are up, though that might be seasonal. Nobody has a number, so the loudest opinion wins.

That silence is the whole subject of Domain 4. The exam guide lists six objectives and they look like six separate skills. They are one loop, and the loop runs in a direction.

Walk it once. You decide what "working" means, as a number with a threshold attached. You build a fixed set of cases to measure that number against. Every change gets gated by that set before it reaches real traffic. When something breaks you diagnose it in a fixed order rather than by hunch.

Then the arrow that matters. Production shows you a failure nobody on the team had imagined, and that failure becomes a new case in the set.

An evaluation set built at design time only contains the failures its authors could think of. Production contains the ones they couldn't.

Break that return arrow and you still get numbers, still gate changes, still ship. You are just measuring your own imagination, with increasing precision, forever.

What Professional tier adds

Foundations asks which evaluation design is technically correct. Professional asks whether you can defend it. Which design is affordable at 200,000 requests a month. Which one survives a regulator asking how you know. How you explain the tradeoff to a stakeholder who thinks "97% accurate" is a complete sentence.

One structural note. Pass/fail runs on total score with no per-domain floor, so a weak Domain 4 is survivable in the arithmetic. It is not survivable in practice. These same reflexes come back in Domain 3's observability objective, Domain 5's human-in-the-loop validation, and Domain 6's SLA conversations.

1. What actually makes a metric a metric

By the end of this section you will be able to look at any number a team is reporting and say, in one sentence, whether it is a metric or a decoration.

Try this test on anything a team calls a metric.

If the number crossing its threshold would not change what you do, it is decoration. Most of what teams collect fails immediately. Dashboards are full of it.

So what does a real one need? Four things. A specific failure being counted, so "quality" is out and "the fraction where the answer contradicts the gold label" is in. A denominator. A named segment of the population it describes. And a threshold declared before anyone saw a result.

That last one does the most work, and it is the one people skip. Set the bar after the scores come in and you have not set a bar. You have described what you got.

Five families, and why they are separate

The guide names five. They are separate for a practical reason: they fail in different ways and they get owned by different people.

Accuracy is task fidelity against a ground truth. It is the only family that forces you to decide in advance what "correct" means, because you cannot compare against a label you never wrote.

Latency is not a number. It is a distribution, and the mean is close to useless for an SLA, because the users who give up and leave are sitting in the tail. Report p95 or p99.

There is a second distinction inside latency worth getting right. Time-to-first-token is what a person watching a cursor experiences as responsiveness. Total completion time is what matters for a batch job nobody is staring at. Confuse them and you will optimize the wrong half of the pipeline.

Cost gets measured per unit of business work, not per token. Tokens are the unit you are billed in and the one unit nobody outside engineering can reason about. "$0.08 per resolved ticket" is a sentence a finance stakeholder can hold you to. "1.4 million tokens a day" is telemetry.

Safety is a rate of policy-violating output over a stated denominator, and the thing to get right is achievability. On an open-ended generative task, a target of zero is not ambitious. It is unreachable.

Watch what happens the first time you miss it. Nobody re-engineers the system. Everyone quietly agrees the number was aspirational, and from then on it is enforced by nobody. Set a small non-zero rate instead, say under 0.1% of ten thousand outputs flagged for toxicity. Save the hard zero for properties a deterministic check can actually guarantee, like zero outputs containing another person's identifier.

Security is a different axis from safety, and the exam likes this distinction. Safety is about what the model says. Security is about what the system permits: whether an injected instruction inside a retrieved document can reach a tool, whether an agent's credentials are scoped, whether data can leave through a tool call.

You measure security against an adversarial test suite, not a quality rubric. A system can be perfectly safe and completely insecure at the same time. Two failure modes, two owners.

How the exam tends to phrase this

A scenario hands you a requirement written in adjectives, usually something like "the system needs to be fast, accurate, and safe," then asks which measurement plan you would adopt.

The wrong answers cluster predictably. Metrics that are easy to collect rather than decision-relevant, like average response length or tokens per day. A single composite quality score, which sounds tidy and tells you nothing on the day it drops three points. A threshold to be "calibrated once we see baseline performance," which is the after-the-fact bar wearing a lab coat. And a safety metric quietly applied to what is really a security requirement.

2. Building an evaluation set that isn't lying to you

By the end of this section you will be able to look at an evaluation set and predict which failures it is structurally incapable of reporting.

This is the longest part of the domain and where the exam's best traps live, so we will go slowly.

Start from one principle. An eval set is a measuring stick, and a measuring stick only works if it stays the same length.

Change the cases between runs and a score change tells you nothing, because you can no longer tell "the system got better" from "the test got easier." That has an uncomfortable consequence, and most teams meet it the hard way: your set will go stale, and refreshing it destroys comparability with everything you measured before.

You handle that by versioning the set and re-baselining deliberately. Not by quietly editing a case in place because it looked wrong to somebody last Tuesday.

The analogy

Refreshing an eval set is changing your test split. You already know you cannot compare a model's accuracy across two different test sets and call the difference progress. Same rule, same reason, and a prompt change is just as capable of overfitting to a fixed set as gradient descent is.

Where it breaks A test split is usually a random draw, so resampling gets you a comparable one. An eval set is deliberately stratified and hand-verified, so you cannot regenerate an equivalent one on demand. That makes the comparability loss permanent rather than inconvenient, which is exactly why the versioning discipline is stricter here than it is in training.

Where the cases come from

Real production traces first. Synthetic cases second. Hand-written cases only for failure shapes you have good reason to expect but have not yet seen.

The reasoning is blunt. The failures you imagine from a spec are not the failures that occur. A set built entirely from a design document encodes its authors' mental model, and the point of evaluation is to test that model against reality rather than confirm it. Treat every imagined case as a hypothesis until a real trace proves the failure exists.

Here is a number that surprises people. Twenty to fifty hand-verified cases beat several hundred unverified synthetic ones.

That sounds backwards until you ask what an unverified case contributes. It contributes a score with no information behind it, because you do not know whether its label is correct.

Put arithmetic on it. Take 500 cases with a 6% label-error rate. Measurable accuracy is now capped at 94%, and nothing you do to the system can move it past that. Teams burn weeks chasing the last six points of a number that was never real.

The bias that ruins most eval sets quietly

Here is how it happens, and it is nobody's fault in particular. A team collects examples while building the system. They keep the ones that were interesting to look at. They drop the ones that were too messy to bother labelling.

What is left is a set assembled almost entirely from cases the system already handles. It will report excellent performance while concealing every failure that matters.

Now the important part. Adding five hundred more cases collected the same way does not fix this. It multiplies the bias tenfold and hands you a tighter confidence interval around a number that was wrong to begin with. The fix is compositional, not volumetric. You rebuild around the segments and failure modes you have to report on.

Why natural frequency is the wrong sampling rule

Most people bring the same instinct to sampling, and it is exactly backwards: match the eval set to production frequency so the number "represents reality."

Watch what that does. A document-extraction system runs 78% invoices, 15% purchase orders, 5% credit notes, and 2% handwritten remittance advices. Sample fifty cases at natural frequency.

The handwritten segment gets exactly one case. You cannot measure a failure rate on one case. You cannot even detect a 40% failure rate on one case, because a single pass or a single fail is noise either way.

If that framing feels familiar, it should. This is the class-imbalance problem wearing different clothes. Nobody trains a classifier on natural frequency and then expects usable minority-class recall. The rare class needs deliberate over-representation, for the same reason and by the same arithmetic. An eval set is no different.

So build the set to answer the question you have to answer. If you must report accuracy by document type, every type needs enough cases to support a rate, with thirty as a working floor. The rare and expensive segments get over-sampled relative to their traffic share. If you need an aggregate figure afterward, reconstruct it by weighting.

The masking trap

An aggregate accuracy of 97% can be hiding a 40% failure rate on a rare document type, because an overall metric averages segment failures away.

And the segments it hides are disproportionately the rare, awkward, expensive ones nobody wants to think about. Before anyone reduces human review on the strength of an aggregate number, every segment has to clear the bar independently.

One more balancing move worth knowing: for every failure your eval set is meant to catch, include a near-miss case where the system behaves correctly and a brittle grader would wrongly flag it. A set built only from failures hides false alarms; a set built only from clean cases hides misses. Aim for roughly equal counts, and treat that as a requirement rather than a nicety as stakes rise.

Three instruments, three different jobs

When the guide says "mixed methodologies," it is asking whether you know these are three distinct tools rather than one grading pipeline with three settings.

The deterministic check comes first: exact match, schema validation, a regex, a numeric comparison, unit tests against the output, a PII pattern scan. Exact, reproducible, free at any volume, immune to grader error.

The range of what code can decide is wider than people assume. Schema validity. Whether a cited span exists in the document. Forbidden-field presence. Numeric tolerance. Tool-call ordering. Refusal when refusal was required. All code-checkable.

Never route something code-checkable to a model grader. Citation validation shows why in one number: asking a model "is this citation accurate?" scores around 38% agreement with ground truth in published evaluation, which is worse than a coin flip. Checking whether the quoted string literally appears in the cited document is exact and free.

The model grader works from a rubric, and it exists for the properties code cannot reach: tone, coherence, whether an answer resolves the question that was asked. Four things separate a trustworthy grader from a decorative one.

Decompose the rubric into binary sub-criteria rather than a 1-to-5 scale. Nobody can say consistently what separates a 3 from a 4, so that boundary drifts between runs and takes your score with it.

Use a grader from a different model family than the generator. A same-family grader shows self-preference bias and shares the generator's blind spots, which means the failures it cannot see are precisely the failures the system is most likely to produce.

Validate it against real human labels before believing anything it reports. Hand-label a sample, run the grader on the same sample, measure agreement. Treat roughly 75% as a starting bar to calibrate from rather than a figure carved anywhere official.

And know its biases going in. It favours whichever option it saw first. It rewards length whether or not length helped. If only a same-family grader is available, its output gets reported as degraded, never as ground truth.

The human is there for calibration, not grading at scale. Humans establish the labels the deterministic checks compare against, produce the sample the model grader is validated on, and periodically re-audit that grader to catch it drifting.

One hard limit is worth memorising. If two domain experts would not independently reach the same verdict on a case, no automated grader can be trusted on that case at any threshold. That is not a rubric problem. The case needs re-scoping.

Put the three together and the order never changes: deterministic checks filter, the model grader scales, humans calibrate.

Those fifty weekly cases get stratified across segments and across pass and fail verdicts, rather than only the worst-looking ones. Track grader-versus-human agreement over time, so nobody finds out about drift the slow way.

Shapes that need more than one score

Some architectures cannot be graded with a single row, and the exam knows it.

A RAG system needs two: one for retrieval, one for generation. A faithfulness score computed over the wrong passages is a meaningless number, because an answer can be perfectly faithful to material that never addressed the question.

An agent needs two: trajectory (was the right tool called, in a sensible order) and outcome (did the final state satisfy the goal). A correct answer reached by a wrong or irreversible path is still a failure. Picture an agent that deletes a record, then reports the correct value from before the deletion. Grade the outcome alone and that scores as a pass.

A multi-turn system needs you to attribute the first turn that broke rather than the final state, because a late self-correction masks an early defect that will not always self-correct next time.

One more distinction to have ready, and it is the one people get backwards under time pressure. pass@k counts a success if at least one of k attempts worked. pass^k counts a success only if all k attempts worked.

The choice is not about difficulty. It is about who catches a bad attempt. If a validator downstream can catch and retry, use pass@k. If every attempt is user-visible or does something in the world, use pass^k.

An agent that books account changes might use pass@3 at 0.90 or better for task completion, because a validator catches a bad plan before it executes. The row checking "did it confirm before any irreversible action" needs pass^5 at 1.0, because one silent irreversible action out of five tries is one too many.

The tempting answer is to enlarge the set, because "get more data" is the correct reflex nearly everywhere else in engineering. Here it multiplies whatever bias the set already carried and tightens the confidence interval around a number that was never true. The right answer rebuilds from real production failures and reports by segment.

3. Change control, or treating a prompt like production code

By the end of this section you will be able to say why a prompt change that improved every number you looked at can still be unsafe to ship.

Let me ask you something that sounds trivial. You change one sentence in a prompt. What could that break?

The honest answer is that you do not know, and you cannot know by reading it. That is not a statement about your carefulness. It is a statement about what a prompt is.

Think about what you rely on when you change ordinary code. A type system rejects a whole class of mistakes before anything runs. A compiler catches another class. And the change itself is bounded: edit a function body and the blast radius is the callers of that function.

A prompt has none of the three. No type system. No compiler. And no unit of change smaller than the entire behaviour, because the model conditions on all of it at once.

So the sentence you added to fix date formatting can move accuracy on a document type you were not thinking about, in a direction nobody predicted, and nothing in your toolchain will mention it.

The analogy

A prompt is a weight matrix you happen to edit by hand. You would never hand-adjust a few weights, eyeball a couple of outputs, and push to production without running the validation set. Editing a prompt is the same operation. It has far better ergonomics, which is exactly what makes it feel like a smaller act than it is.

Where it breaks A gradient update is continuous and local, so a small step usually produces a small, smooth change in behaviour. A prompt edit is discrete and global: one clause can shift how the model reads the whole instruction and move a segment you never touched. The analogy understates the risk rather than overstating it, which is the useful direction for an analogy to fail in.

Measurement is the only safety net, and it runs in a fixed order: regression evaluation, then a controlled A/B, then a staged rollout.

That order is not a ritual. Each gate exists because the one before it is blind to something.

One detail in that first gate is easy to miss. The regression set is also the only place your rare segments get proper coverage, because that is where you deliberately over-sampled them. Live traffic will not do it for you.

Making the A/B mean something

Five disciplines, and each one has a specific way of being violated by a reasonable person having a busy week.

Change one variable at a time. Swap the prompt and the model and the retrieval depth in a single variant and a win tells you only that the bundle helped. You now have three suspects and no way to isolate which earned it.

Randomize at a stable unit like user or session, never at the request level. Randomize per request and a single conversation flips between prompt versions mid-thread, which corrupts the conversation and the metric together.

Declare the primary metric and the guardrails before the test starts. Guardrails such as latency, cost, or safety rate can only block a change. They can never justify shipping one on their own.

Fix duration and sample size in advance. Then do not peek and stop the moment the numbers look good. Peeking inflates your false-positive rate, and it does so while feeling like ordinary diligence, which is what makes it dangerous.

Report by segment as well as in aggregate. The masking trap from section 2 has not gone anywhere. An A/B reports a mean, and a mean can hide a segment getting worse while the average improves.

What this looks like on a real system

A support assistant handles 40,000 requests a week. The team wants to add three targeted few-shot examples, to fix two tools the agent keeps confusing.

Gate 1. The regression run on the 400-case set shows tool-selection accuracy on the ambiguous subset climbing from 0.71 to 0.93, with overall accuracy holding steady. Nothing regressed, so the change earns a live test.

Gate 2. The A/B is declared up front. Primary metric is first-contact resolution. Guardrails are p95 latency under 4.0 seconds and cost under $0.11 per resolved ticket. Split 50/50 by user, two weeks, no peeking.

Resolution moves from 0.82 to 0.86. Latency ticks from 3.4 to 3.6 seconds, inside the guardrail. Cost ticks from $0.094 to $0.099, inside the guardrail. The gain concentrates exactly where predicted and nothing else regresses.

Gate 3. It ships at 10%, then 50%, then 100%.

Now notice the part that is easy to read past. Those extra tokens were not free. They cost half a cent and two-tenths of a second, and the change shipped anyway. Not because nobody noticed, but because both costs were declared as guardrails in advance and both cleared.

Checkpoint 3 · before you read on

A colleague runs one variant that changes the prompt, upgrades the model, and raises retrieval depth from 5 to 10 passages. First-contact resolution improves by four points and the result is statistically clean.

What have you actually learned, and what will this cost you in three months?

Answer first, then open
You learned that the bundle helped. Nothing more.

Three suspects, one result, no way to attribute the gain. That is not a bookkeeping complaint. In three months the model gets deprecated, or the retrieval bill needs cutting, and somebody asks which of the three was carrying the improvement. Nobody can answer, so nothing can safely be removed.

Worth noticing: one of the three could be actively hurting while the other two carry the gain, and a clean aggregate win conceals that perfectly.

4. Diagnosing what actually broke

By the end of this section you will reach for retrieval before you reach for the model, and be able to explain to a sceptical manager why that is the cheaper move as well as the correct one.

Something breaks in production. Where do you look first?

Most engineers reach for the model, and it is worth being honest about why. A model swap is a one-line change, it feels decisive, and it is the part of the stack that feels most like the source of intelligence.

Here is the empirical fact that should reorganize the instinct. When an LLM system degrades, the fault sits in what feeds the model far more often than in the model itself.

The reason is structural. Every layer upstream poisons everything downstream. Swap the model while retrieval is still broken and you have changed the phrasing of a wrong answer.

So the order is data and retrieval first, then prompt and context, then model mismatch. And that order is not only empirically right. It is economically right, which is the argument that actually wins the meeting.

Hallucination is three different failures

Treat hallucination as one phenomenon and you get one reflexive fix, usually an instruction not to make things up, which addresses none of the actual causes.

It is three failures wearing one symptom.

Grounding. The context did contain the answer and the model departed from it. Fix: require a citation for every claim, plus a deterministic check that the cited span exists.

Retrieval. Retrieval returned nothing relevant and the model filled the vacuum. Fix: repair retrieval, and add the refusal path that was missing.

Context management. Something was dropped by compaction and the model reconstructed it from nothing. Fix: extract precision-critical facts verbatim rather than summarizing everything uniformly.

Same symptom, three root causes, three different fixes. And the evidence that tells you which one you have is already in your logs: what was retrieved, what actually reached the context window, and whether the claim has a resolvable source.

Worked case

A policy assistant's grounded-answer rate falls from 0.94 to 0.71 over a week. The team's first instinct is a bigger model.

Following the order instead: retrieval Recall@5 has collapsed from 0.92 to 0.55. A corpus refresh restructured the headings, the chunker now splits mid-table, and the entitlement tables that answer the most common question class are split across two chunks that each score below the relevance floor.

The investigation stops at layer 1. The model was never touched. A bigger model would have produced a more articulate answer built on exactly the same missing passages.

There is a general lesson under that case. When quality drops sharply and something changed right before it, the thing that changed is your hypothesis.

Sharp drops have causes. Gradual drift has drivers, and that is a different kind of investigation with a different toolkit.

The exam's version of this always pairs a symptom with a timeline detail: a corpus refresh, a re-index, a prompt release. The timeline detail is the answer key. The official guide's own sample question follows exactly this shape, where a RAG system turning confidently wrong right after a document refresh points at retrieval and indexing rather than the model.

Checkpoint 4 · before you read on

A RAG assistant is fine on Tuesday. A nightly corpus reload runs. On Wednesday morning it is citing clause numbers that do not match the text it quotes, and it sounds completely certain doing it.

Name your first three checks, in order, and say what you are deliberately not checking yet.

All three live at layer 1. The model is not a suspect yet.

One. Did the re-index actually complete? Compare chunk count against the expected count from source.

Two. Did the chunking configuration change alongside the refresh?

Three. Did the embedding model version move between what is recorded in the index and what is running at query time? Nothing errors when this happens, which is what makes it the classic silent killer.

Not yet: the model, the prompt, and the temperature. Confidence is not evidence of a sampling problem. The model reports whatever it retrieved, faithfully and fluently, and the timing tells you what changed.

5. Making it cheaper and faster without breaking it

By the end of this section you will know which single lever to reach for when a scenario names latency and cost in the same sentence, and why the obvious alternative is a trap.

Start with the fact that makes this domain tractable. A token is a line of cost and a line of latency at the same time.

That coupling is convenient. Cut tokens and you usually help both at once. It also cuts the other way: add context to chase accuracy and you usually hurt both at once.

Optimization here means moving along that curve deliberately, inside a quality bar you already declared. One rule governs everything else.

Measure which stage dominates before optimizing anything. Halve a stage that is 8% of your latency budget and you have bought 4%. People do this constantly, because the stage they understand best is rarely the stage that is slow.

The highest-value lever, by a wide margin

Prompt caching. When a large block of content is byte-identical on every request, a system prompt or a policy document or a tool schema, put it first, put whatever varies last, and turn caching on.

The stable prefix gets reused instead of reprocessed. Time-to-first-token drops, cost drops, and quality is untouched, because nothing about the content changed.

Per the guide's own sample question, this is the answer whenever a scenario names latency and cost in the same sentence.

Two things break it silently. The prefix has to be genuinely identical, so a timestamp or session ID rendered into it kills the cache instantly and invisibly. And the static content has to actually come first.

The rest of the ladder

Route by difficulty. Send the routine majority to a smaller, faster model and escalate only the hard minority. Validate the escalation trigger on a labelled set rather than assuming it works.

Retrieval discipline matters more than people expect. Fetching the top 20 passages instead of the top 5 costs tokens on every request and often hurts accuracy, because the extra passages are noise sitting in the middle of the context window where attention is already weakest.

Output shaping. Output tokens are usually the expensive side of the ledger, and they drive latency one generated token at a time.

For long conversations, extract the facts that must stay exact into a structured block and summarize the rest. Uniform summarization is how a specific dollar amount quietly becomes "approximately."

And know what streaming actually buys. It moves time-to-first-token. It does not reduce total completion time and it does not reduce cost. A real win for an interactive chat interface, worth precisely nothing for an overnight batch job.

The caching lever, with real numbers

A claims assistant sends an 8,000-token static policy preamble on every request. Volume is 200,000 requests a month.

Without caching, that preamble is billed and reprocessed 200,000 times. Work out what that means: 1.6 billion input tokens a month that are byte-for-byte identical every single time.

And it is not only a cost line. That preamble sits in front of every request's first token, because it has to be processed before generation can begin.

Reorder the static content to the front, enable caching, and the prefix stops being reprocessed. Cost drops, time-to-first-token drops, and the policy content is exactly what it was.

The trap version of this answer

Someone proposes cutting the preamble from 8,000 tokens to 3,000 by trimming "the parts we rarely need." It produces a bigger number on the slide.

It also removes required policy content from a regulated determination process. That is a compliance regression wearing an optimization's clothes, and it is exactly the trade a Professional-tier item is built to test.

The stakeholder sentence for the real fix: we reordered the prompt so the unchanging policy text gets cached instead of resent, which cut cost and response time without removing any policy content. Nobody in that conversation needs to hear the word "token."

Checkpoint 5 · before you read on

A team enables streaming on an overnight batch job that summarizes 40,000 documents. They report it as a latency optimization.

What actually improved, and what should they have measured first?

Answer first, then open
Nothing improved.

Streaming moves time-to-first-token. It does not reduce total completion time and it does not reduce cost. For a batch job nobody is watching, time-to-first-token is not a quantity anyone experiences.

What they should have done first is the rule that governs this whole section: profile which stage dominates. If retrieval is 70% of the wall clock, no amount of work on generation will show up in the result.

6. Watching it in production

By the end of this section you will be able to name the two log fields that turn a week-long investigation into a four-minute one.

An evaluation set tells you what happened on a fixed set of cases. Monitoring tells you what is happening on traffic nobody anticipated.

One can never substitute for the other, and the reason is worth stating precisely: a frozen input distribution is exactly what makes the eval set a good measuring stick, and exactly what makes it blind to drift.

Production instrumentation has one organizing requirement. Attribution. A metric moving is useful only if you can say what moved it.

What to log

The field that matters most is the one people skip: model identifier and prompt version, on every request.

Without it, a quality change can never be tied back to a release, and every investigation begins as archaeology through a deploy log. With it, the first question in any investigation has an instant answer.

Alongside it: retrieved document IDs and relevance scores, token counts split by input, output and cache-read, and latency broken out by stage rather than reported as one aggregate number nobody can act on.

Cheap proxies that move first

Production hands you signals that move before an offline evaluation would notice anything.

Escalation rate rising usually means the system is meeting more cases it cannot handle. Retry rate rising means outputs are failing some downstream check. Thumbs-down rate rising is a quality regression the deterministic checks do not cover.

None of these is a quality measurement, and that is exactly why they are worth alerting on. They move first, before anyone has opened the eval harness.

Run the model grader continuously as well, on a stratified live sample. Not the whole stream, and not only the flagged cases. Novel error patterns show up first in the high-confidence stream, which is precisely where nobody is looking.

Alert on rates and segments rather than individual events, and favour leading indicators like cache-hit rate over lagging ones like user complaints.

A story worth keeping

Cost per request triples overnight. Latency climbs almost two seconds. Every quality metric stays completely flat.

Sit with that combination for a second before reading on, because it is the whole clue. Cost and latency moved together while quality did not move at all.

That pattern points at the token or caching layer specifically. A genuine change in model behaviour would have disturbed quality too, and it did not.

The cache-hit-rate panel shows a drop from 0.93 to 0.11 at 2:14am. The prompt-version field shows a release landed at 2:10. Someone had inserted a rendered timestamp into the system preamble for debugging, which made the prefix different on every request and silently killed cache reuse.

Time to diagnosis: about four minutes, because two fields existed. Without them it is a week of bisecting deploys while finance asks pointed questions.

Checkpoint 6 · before you read on

Different system, same shape. Cost per request falls 40% overnight, latency improves, and grounded-answer rate falls from 0.94 to 0.78.

Nobody deployed a prompt change. What is your first hypothesis?

Answer first, then open
Something is putting less into the context window.

Cost and latency improving together points at the token layer, exactly as in the story above. The difference is that quality moved this time, so the token reduction is not free: material that was being used has stopped arriving.

Check retrieval first, per section 4's order. A retrieval change that returns fewer or shorter passages produces precisely this signature: cheaper, faster, and less grounded. A silently truncated context or an over-aggressive compaction step produces it too.

The general reflex worth keeping: cost and quality moving in opposite directions at the same moment is almost always one change, not two.

One more thing that is easy to treat as an afterthought. Production logs of a real system contain real user data.

Redact at write time rather than read time, set an actual retention period, and restrict who can read it. In a regulated sector this is not hygiene. It is a control with an auditor's name attached, and any eval sample pulled from those logs inherits the same obligation.

7. How it all runs as one system

By the end of this section you will have seen all six objectives hand their output to the next one, on a single system, over a year. This is the shape the exam's hardest items take.

Take that claims assistant through a full year.

Week one. Six metrics, with thresholds agreed with the business sponsor before anyone knows what the system will score. That is the only time the conversation is honest, which is the entire reason for the sequencing.

Weeks two and three. 400 cases pulled from real submitted claims, hand-verified by an adjuster. They are stratified so that the 2% that are handwritten remittance advices get real coverage. That 2% carries 30% of the actual risk, which is the whole argument against sampling at natural frequency.

From then on. Every prompt edit runs the regression set, then a declared A/B, then a staged rollout. No exceptions, including for changes that look obviously safe.

Month four. Grounded-answer rate drops from 0.94 to 0.71.

The diagnosis order runs as designed. Retrieval first, and retrieval has in fact collapsed following a corpus refresh that broke table chunking. The model is never touched. The fix is a chunking change, verified against the regression set before anything ships.

Then the step that matters most, and the one that is easiest to skip when everyone is relieved the incident is over. The newly discovered failure mode becomes twelve new cases in the eval set.

That's the loop actually closing: production just taught the evaluation set something its authors didn't know a month ago.

Month five. Profiling shows the policy preamble dominates both cost and time-to-first-token, so it is reordered and cached.

A separate proposal to also trim it by 5,000 tokens is rejected outright. The removed sections are required by regulation, and the quality bar from week one is not something you renegotiate for a cost saving.

Continuously, underneath all of it. Dashboards tracking accuracy by claim type, escalation rate, cost per claim, cache-hit rate, and grader-versus-human agreement over time. Every surfaced failure is a candidate for the next version of the eval set.

The property worth naming

Nothing in that whole year depends on anyone's gut feeling about whether the system "feels" better. Every decision is gated by a number defined before the result was known, and every number can be traced back to a specific layer that produced it.

Where these reflexes show up again

Domain 4 is not a self-contained skill, and the exam does not treat it as one. Three of its habits reappear under other names.

The golden-query gate in Domain 3. After every re-index, a fixed set of queries with known-correct chunk IDs has to clear a hit-rate bar before promotion. That is a regression set, applied to retrieval instead of to the whole system, and it exists for the same reason: a measuring stick only works if it stays the same length.

The human-review budget in Domain 5. Deciding which cases a person must see is the same stratification problem as section 2. Spend the budget on the rare, high-consequence segment rather than in proportion to volume, or the review is decorative for exactly the cases it was bought to catch.

The SLA conversation in Domain 6. Section 1's insistence on p95 over the mean, and on cost per unit of business work rather than per token, is what makes an SLA defensible in front of someone who is going to sign it.

Common misconceptions

The mythA bigger evaluation set is a better evaluation set.
Why it's temptingMore data is the correct reflex in almost every other part of engineering.
The realityCoverage of real failure modes beats count. Enlarging a biased set multiplies the bias and tightens the interval around a wrong number.
The one testDid the new cases come from real production traces, or from collecting more of what the team already thought to write down?
The myth97% accuracy means the system is ready to reduce human review.
Why it's tempting97% sounds finished. It is a single, confident, reportable number.
The realityAn aggregate can hide a 40% failure rate on a rare segment. Every segment has to clear the bar independently before oversight is reduced.
The one testBreak the number out by segment. If any segment hasn't been checked separately, the 97% hasn't earned its confidence yet.
The mythThe model started hallucinating, so we need a better model.
Why it's temptingA model swap is a one-line change and feels like the decisive engineering move.
The realityDiagnose data and retrieval first, then prompt and context, then model mismatch. Hallucination has three distinct causes with three distinct fixes.
The one testHas Recall@5 against gold passages been checked since the last thing that changed? If not, the model isn't cleared as a suspect yet.
