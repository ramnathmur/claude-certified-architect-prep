An architect who has already chosen the pattern, drawn the boundaries and picked the integration protocol still has to decide, for every call the system makes: which model receives it, what instructions frame it, what examples accompany it, what history rides along, and which parts of all that can be reused rather than rebuilt.

Those five decisions are the five objectives, and they are not independent. Each one moves the same three dials.

There is a second, quieter through-line, and the distractors in this domain are built around it.

Every technique here is a targeted instrument, not a general improvement.

Few-shot examples fix inconsistency, not ignorance. Chain-of-thought fixes broken derivations, not missing facts. A bigger model fixes capability shortfalls, not attention position. Caching fixes repeated prefixes, not long prompts in general.

The most common way to get a Domain 2 question wrong is to select a technique that is genuinely good, applied to a failure it cannot touch.

Next: the first lever, and the one where "to be safe" turns out to be the wrong instinct.

1. Selecting a model

By the end of this section you will treat capability headroom as a purchase that needs a named requirement, and you will apply the latency gate before you look at accuracy.

Model families ship in capability tiers. Higher tiers reason further across ambiguity, hold longer chains of inference together, and handle instructions that are underspecified. Lower tiers are faster and cheaper per call.

This lesson names no specific model, size or price, and neither should your reasoning. Tier names and their numbers change on a schedule you do not control, and an architect who has memorised a price list has memorised the one part of the job that expires.

What does not expire is the selection procedure.

A model choice is a purchase of capability headroom, and headroom no requirement demands is cost you pay on every call, forever, for nothing.

That framing is what makes "use the most capable model, to be safe" an incorrect answer rather than a conservative one. Safety in an architecture is a property you can point at a requirement for. If nobody can name the requirement the headroom serves, it is not safety.

The problem is real in both directions. A classification service at production volume makes the same call millions of times, so a few hundred milliseconds and a fraction of a cent compound into the dominant line item and the dominant term in p95 latency. A legal-analysis workflow running eighty times a month has no volume pressure at all, and its binding constraint is whether the model can hold a multi-document argument together without dropping a clause.

Four inputs, in this order

Task complexity. How many inference steps, how much ambiguity, how much synthesis across sources. This sets the floor on capability.

The accuracy bar, expressed as a number. Not "high accuracy" but the specific threshold below which the downstream cost of an error exceeds the savings. This is what you benchmark against.

Latency budget. A hard gate. A model that cannot answer inside the budget is disqualified regardless of how accurate it is.

Volume. The multiplier that turns per-call differences into architectural ones.

Then one non-negotiable step. Benchmark candidates on representative data before committing. Not a vendor benchmark, not a hand-picked set of clean examples, but a sample of actual traffic including the messy tail.

Worked example

A support platform classifies inbound tickets into one of fourteen routing categories. 40,000 tickets a day. The routing decision is made synchronously while the customer waits on a confirmation screen, and the product team has set a p95 budget of 800ms.

The team benchmarks two candidates on 2,000 real labelled tickets drawn across a full week, weekend traffic and oddly-worded tail included.

Now change one number and watch the answer move. Suppose the accuracy bar is 95.5%, because a misroute means a compliance-sensitive ticket sits in the wrong queue for a day.

The lower tier no longer clears it. The higher tier does, and still fails the latency gate. So neither candidate is simply the answer, and the design has to change: classify asynchronously and show an optimistic routing state, or run the lower tier first and escalate only the low-confidence minority.

That escalation design is worth understanding even though it rarely appears as an option. Run the fast model on everything, take the confident labels, and re-run the rest on the higher tier. If 96% come back confident you pay the expensive model on 1,600 calls a day instead of 40,000, and p95 stays on the fast path, because only the escalated 4% sits above the 95th percentile.

Watch that last clause, because it is a constraint rather than a rounding detail. The escalated fraction has to stay below the percentile the SLA names. Escalate 12% instead and the 95th percentile is itself an escalated call, so p95 becomes fast path plus slow path, around 2.2s here, and the 800ms gate the design existed to clear is missed by a factor of nearly three.

The reason that is worth knowing: model selection is a choice per call path, not per system, and one system can hold several.

A VP asks you to use the most capable available model across a new document-summarisation feature, "so we don't get caught out on quality." The feature runs 300 times a day, internally, with no stated latency requirement.

What is the right response, and how does it differ from the same request on the 40,000-a-day classifier?

Answer first, then open
Ask what specifically requires it. Then notice the volume changes the stakes, not the principle.

The principle holds either way: headroom bought without a named requirement is unpriced cost, so the question is always "which part of this task needs the extra capability?" If summarising these documents involves real synthesis across sources, that is a complexity driver and the answer may genuinely be the higher tier.

What volume changes is the size of the mistake. At 300 calls a day with no latency gate, over-provisioning is a small, recoverable waste. At 40,000 synchronous calls a day it is the dominant line item and it breaks the p95 budget as well.

So this is not "always push back." It is: make them name the driver, and let volume tell you how hard to push.

The wrong turns

"Default to the most capable model, to be safe." It sounds thorough, and nobody was ever criticised in a design review for over-provisioning. It is wrong when the task is bounded, high-volume and latency-gated, because nothing in that shape calls for extra reasoning and the cost compounds on every call.

"Fine-tune a smaller model." A real and sophisticated lever, deployed one step too early. You have not yet tested whether a base model clears the bar, and base-model evaluation might have closed the question for free.

Choosing on a published benchmark instead of your own data. Your accuracy bar is defined over your distribution, including the awkward tail your users actually produce.

Treating the choice as permanent. Selection gets revisited when the task changes, when volume moves by an order of magnitude, or when a new tier ships. An architecture with a model identifier hard-coded in forty places has made that revisit expensive.

2. System prompts and guardrails

By the end of this section you will be able to say, for any rule, whether it belongs in the system prompt, in a template slot, or in code, and why the third one is not optional for some of them.

The system prompt is the only place in a request with durable authority over the whole conversation.

Everything else either loses force as the conversation moves on, or never reaches the model at all.

That second category deserves a beat, because it is the failure architects actually commit. A rule set as an environment variable at deploy time has no path to the model's behaviour.

The model sees the bytes in the request. A rule that is not in those bytes does not exist, however carefully it was written down.

Three artifacts, commonly conflated

The system prompt carries persistent behavioural constraints: persona, tone, response format, scope boundaries, refusal conditions, escalation triggers. It is the constitution.

The template is the structure that assembles a request, separating the stable skeleton from the per-request variable slots. It exists so a thousand requests share one authored artifact rather than a thousand string concatenations, and so the stable portion sits where section 5 can cache it.

Guardrails are the constraints defining what the system refuses, escalates, or routes to a human. They are a design decision made when the system prompt is written, not a monitoring feature bolted on after an incident.

That last distinction carries architectural weight, and it is where Domain 2 hands off to Domain 5.

A guardrail expressed in a system prompt is a strong default, not an enforcement boundary. It shapes behaviour reliably in the overwhelming majority of cases, and it can be argued with.

Anything that must never happen needs enforcement outside the model: a tool that is not exposed at all, a code-level check on the tool's arguments, an approval hook.

Worked example

A claims assistant must speak in a specific institutional register, never quote a settlement figure, ask a clarifying question when the policy number is missing rather than guessing, and escalate whenever claim value exceeds a threshold or the customer asks for a human.

A weak implementation puts the register in the system prompt, the settlement rule in a paragraph of the first user message, and the escalation threshold in a config file the deployment pipeline reads.

Count what reaches the model. Two of those four rules never do. The settlement rule reaches it once, in turn one, and its authority decays as thirty turns of assistant output accumulate above it.

The sound implementation puts register, settlement prohibition, clarifying-question rule and escalation triggers all in the system prompt, stated as concrete testable conditions. "When policy_number is absent from the retrieved case record, ask for it before proceeding" beats "be careful about missing information."

The escalation threshold also exists as a code-level check on the arguments passed to the settlement tool, so a model that talks itself past the instruction still cannot execute the action.

How the exam probes it

The placement question. A behavioural rule is described and you choose where it belongs. The correct answer is the system prompt; the distractors are the first user message, an environment variable, the first assistant message, or a document nobody sends to the model.

The drift scenario. An assistant honours its persona early and stops by turn ten or twelve, in a conversation nowhere near any capacity limit. The root cause is accumulated assistant output diluting the system prompt's relative influence.

The fixes are structural: replace verbose abstract rules with a small number of concrete examples demonstrating the behaviour, and reinforce at conversation breakpoints. Reaching for a bigger context window here is a category error, because the conversation was never close to full.

The guardrail-layering question, where something must never happen and the options offer a prompt instruction, a log, a confirmation step, and removal of the capability. Removing an unneeded capability beats logging or confirming its use.

Checkpoint 2 · before you read on

A team reports that their assistant "keeps forgetting" its required disclaimer by around turn twelve. Conversations average eighteen turns and a few thousand tokens, well inside any window. The disclaimer rule is in the system prompt, written as a paragraph of careful prose.

Diagnose it, and say why a larger context window is the wrong instrument.

Answer first, then open
Dilution by accumulated assistant output, not capacity.

A few thousand tokens is nowhere near a limit, so capacity is ruled out by arithmetic before you think about anything else. What has changed by turn twelve is the ratio: the system prompt is now a small fraction of a request dominated by the model's own prior output.

Why a bigger window does nothing: it changes how much fits, and the problem was never that things did not fit. This is the same category error as offering a larger window against an attention problem, which section 4 covers.

The fixes are structural. Replace the careful paragraph with two or three concrete examples demonstrating the disclaimer in use, and reinforce at conversation breakpoints. Concrete beats verbose specifically because models drift on unfalsifiable instructions faster than on demonstrated ones.

3. Prompt techniques

By the end of this section you will diagnose the failure mode before reaching for an instrument, which is the entire skill this objective tests.

Three techniques, three distinct failure modes.

Zero-shot is instruction only. It is the default and it is often sufficient. It fails when the instruction, however carefully written, underdetermines the output: two readings both satisfy it, and the model alternates between them across calls.

Few-shot supplies demonstrations, and its mechanism is worth getting exactly right. It is not teaching the model the task, because the model already knows the task.

Examples pin down the specific choice the instruction left open: exact output shape, exactly where a boundary between two categories falls, exactly which tool an ambiguous phrasing maps to.

Which is why the number of examples matters far less than their placement. Four examples sitting precisely on the ambiguous boundary resolve the ambiguity. Fifteen examples of obvious cases confirm what the model was already getting right, and consume context doing it.

The analogy

Few-shot examples are labelled data, and the same sampling logic applies. You already know you do not improve a classifier by adding more examples of the class it gets right. You add examples near the decision boundary, because that is where the uncertainty lives and where a label carries information. Few-shot selection is that decision made by hand, one prompt at a time.

Where it breaks With training data, more is generally better once you are sampling sensibly, because gradient descent averages over many examples and the marginal cost of one more is close to nothing. Few-shot has no such forgiveness: every example is re-sent on every call for the life of the system, so the volume-versus-targeting trade is far harsher. The rule is four to six targeted examples, not four hundred, and that ceiling comes from the token bill rather than from anything about learning.

The working rule: 4 to 6 examples targeted at the ambiguous cases, not 10 to 15 easy ones. For a pure format-consistency problem, 3 or 4 examples of the exact required format is usually enough. Where the ambiguity is a judgment call, give each example a stated rationale, because the rationale is what generalises to the ambiguous case you did not think of.

Chain-of-thought asks the model to reason in steps before answering, and its mechanism is that it gives the model tokens in which to perform intermediate computation. A multi-step derivation gets worked rather than guessed.

It does nothing for a single-step task, because there are no steps to work through. And it is not free: a reasoning cue lengthens the output, output tokens are generated serially, so it adds latency to every call as well as cost.

A fourth technique is worth carrying because it shows up as both a distractor and an answer. Prefilling supplies the opening of the assistant's message so the model continues from it rather than starting fresh. Its canonical use is suppressing a stereotyped opening. It is a shape control, not a content control.

Checkpoint 3 · before you read on

An extraction step returns correct values but formats dates three different ways across runs, despite an instruction specifying ISO 8601. A colleague proposes adding a chain-of-thought cue so the model "reasons about the format before writing it."

Name the failure mode, the right instrument, and the dose.

Answer first, then open
Inconsistent form. Few-shot, three or four examples of the exact format.

The values are correct, so the model is neither ignorant nor mis-deriving. What varies is the shape, which is the signature of an instruction that underdetermines the output even though it reads as specific.

Why chain-of-thought is wrong here: formatting a date is single-step. There is no intermediate computation to give room to, so the cue buys latency and output tokens on every call and returns nothing.

The dose matters too. This is a pure format problem, so three or four examples of the exact required format is enough. The 4-to-6-with-rationales guidance is for judgment boundaries, and rationales would be padding here.

The wrong turns

"Add more examples." More examples reads as more effort. If they do not sit on the failure boundary they are wasted context and the failure persists unchanged.

"Refine the instructions further." Nearly free, and it feels like the responsible first move. When instructions have already failed on a consistency problem, more prose is more of the thing that did not work.

"Chain-of-thought never hurts." It hurts on every single-step call, measured in latency and output tokens, at whatever volume the system runs.

Applying a technique uniformly "for consistency." Consistency across features is a real engineering value in code. Applied to prompt techniques it overrides the per-task diagnosis that this entire objective consists of.

4. Context and token management

By the end of this section you will diagnose three different "it forgot something" symptoms apart, and know which layer each one lives in.

Start from one fact. The Claude API is stateless.

Every call is independent, nothing on the server remembers the previous one, there is no session identifier that restores prior turns, and no server-side conversation store the model consults.

So everything a conversation appears to remember is something the application re-sent. Continuity is constructed by the client on every single request: the full prior message history goes into the messages array, every time, or the model does not have it.

Two consequences follow directly, and both are examinable.

Consequence one. Apparent amnesia in a short conversation is an application bug. If the model does not know something the user said two turns ago and the conversation is three turns long, the application did not include the prior messages. It is not a capacity limit, and it is not a missing parameter, because no such parameter exists.

Consequence two. Cost and latency grow quadratically in turn count. This is the part that surprises people, so let us do the arithmetic.

Suppose each turn adds about 300 tokens to the transcript. On turn N, the request carries roughly 300 × (N − 1) tokens of history plus the new message.

The analogy

You have seen this curve before, in attention's cost in sequence length. Both produce the same quadratic shape, and both punish long things far harder than intuition expects, which makes the curve familiar enough to reason about quickly.

Where it breaks They are the same shape for entirely different reasons, and the difference is the whole diagnosis. Attention's cost is quadratic within one forward pass, in sequence length, and it is a property of the model. This curve is quadratic across turns, and it is a property of your application resending the transcript. Conflate them and you will go looking for a model or a window to fix a client-side bug. Use the analogy for the intuition about how fast it grows, never for where the fix belongs.

Lost in the middle

The second structural fact: attention across a long input is not uniform. Content at the beginning and at the end is processed reliably. Content in the middle is attended to less reliably, and the effect grows with input length.

The practical failure shape: a synthesis task over a long document set produces an output reflecting the opening summary and the closing conclusions accurately, while a critical finding sitting in the middle is simply absent.

Nothing errors. The output looks complete and reads fluently, which is what makes this failure expensive. It is invisible without ground truth.

The hybrid context strategy

Given a conversation that will outgrow any budget you set, something has to be compressed. The tested answer is that compression must be non-uniform, because the information in a conversation is not uniform.

Extract precision-critical facts verbatim into a structured block. Amounts, dates, identifiers, agreed terms, allergies, thresholds, account numbers. These go into a compact block included in every prompt, sitting outside the summarised region, and they are never paraphrased.

Summarise low-density discussion. Pleasantries, exploratory back-and-forth, the four turns spent narrowing a question. This is where compression actually pays, because the token-to-meaning ratio is poor.

Keep recent turns verbatim. The current exchange needs full fidelity for the model to respond coherently to what was just said.

Pure summarisation fails with a specific signature. "The 15% discount I agreed to on the second call" becomes "promotional pricing was discussed." The summary is not wrong. It is unusable, and it is confidently unusable, because nothing downstream signals that a number was lost.

A pure recency window fails differently: it drops early content wholesale. A fact stated in turn three of a sixty-turn conversation falls out of a last-twenty-turns window entirely, and in some domains that fact is the one that matters most.

Two properties make the persistent facts block work. It is updated as new facts appear, so it stays current rather than being a snapshot of turn one. And it is positioned outside the summariser's reach, so no future compression pass can degrade it.

Instructing the summariser to "be sure to preserve numbers" is a weaker design, because it makes correctness depend on the summariser performing perfectly every time rather than on the fact never entering the compressible region.

Two adjacent levers

Trim verbose tool results before they enter context. A single lookup can return forty fields when five are relevant, and every one of those then rides along in every subsequent turn. Instructing the model to "ignore the irrelevant fields" does not help, because the tokens are already spent and the attention cost is already paid.

Isolate verbose exploration in a subagent. When a phase produces a large volume of output that only matters in summary, running it in a subagent that returns a condensed result keeps the main conversation's context available for the work that needs it.

Both are the same principle at different points: decide what enters context on purpose, rather than letting everything accumulate by default.

Checkpoint 4 · before you read on

Three reports land on the same morning. (a) A three-turn conversation where the assistant does not know what the user said in turn one. (b) A fifty-turn conversation where latency and cost have climbed steadily. (c) A single call over forty documents where the output misses one finding that sits in document twenty.

Give the cause of each, and name the distractor each one attracts.

Answer first, then open
Three different layers. None of them is the context window.

(a) Missing history in the request. Three turns is nowhere near capacity, so the application is not resending prior messages. The distractor: a session parameter or a vector database, neither of which addresses a client that is not sending what it has.

(b) Full history resent every call. Cumulative tokens grow with the square of turn count, and time-to-first-token scales with prompt length. The distractor: the model writing longer replies, or a storage problem.

(c) Lost in the middle. Document twenty is in the penalised region. The distractor: a larger context window, which changes how much fits and not how attention distributes.

The pattern worth carrying: "it forgot something" is three different bugs, and the arithmetic on conversation length tells you which one you have before you consider any fix.

5. Prompt reuse

By the end of this section you will recognise the caching answer from two words in a scenario, and know the four ways teams ship a cache that never hits.

Prompt caching reuses a stable input prefix across requests. It does not store outputs, and that one word, prefix, explains every rule that follows.

The mechanism: when a request arrives, the system checks whether the leading portion of the input matches a previously processed prefix. If it does, the processing already done for that portion is reused rather than recomputed. The match is on the prefix, in order, from the first byte.

Three properties fall straight out of that.

Order matters absolutely. The cacheable region is a prefix, so it must come first. Static content at the front: system prompt, policy text, tool definitions, stable few-shot examples. Variable content at the back: the user's message, retrieved documents, timestamps.

Byte-identity matters. A prefix that differs by one character is a different prefix. A session identifier, a request UUID or a "current time" line injected at the top invalidates the cache on every single call, while looking in the code like an innocuous logging nicety.

Invalidation cascades forward. Change something at position 100 and everything after position 100 is a new prefix, no matter how much of it is unchanged.

There is an economics wrinkle worth internalising without memorising any number. Writing a prefix into the cache carries a premium over ordinary input processing, and reading from it carries a discount.

So caching pays once the prefix is reused enough times to amortise the write, and cache entries do not live forever. A low-traffic endpoint may pay the write repeatedly and read rarely. Treat the multipliers and lifetimes as a documentation lookup; own the break-even reasoning.

Worked example

This is the shape of the guide's own sample question in this domain, so it is worth walking carefully.

An application sends an identical large preamble on every request: a compliance policy, tool definitions, and a stable block of few-shot examples. Call it 8,000 tokens. The user's message averages 200.

Without caching, every request processes 8,200 input tokens from scratch. The user's 200 tokens are 2.4% of the work, and the other 97.6% is the same computation performed again.

With the static preamble ordered first and caching enabled, roughly 200 tokens of genuinely new input get processed. Time-to-first-token drops because prefill over the cached region is skipped, and input cost drops because the reused portion bills at the read rate.

Latency and cost move together, which is exactly why "both latency and cost are concerns" is the phrase that flags this answer.

Four bugs people actually ship

The template renders Request ID: 7f3a... as the first line of the system prompt. The prefix differs on every call, the hit rate is zero, and the metric that would reveal it is one nobody is watching.

Retrieved documents are placed before the policy text, because the assembly code appends them in retrieval order. The policy is no longer a prefix, so the largest stable block in the request is uncacheable.

Someone "optimises" by truncating the policy to cut tokens. The token count falls and required policy content is now missing, which is a correctness regression sold as an efficiency win.

Someone adds a preliminary call asking the model to summarise the preamble at request time. That adds a round trip, adds its own latency, and risks compressing policy language that needed to be exact, to solve a problem caching solves without any of it.

Modular prompts and Skills

Caching is reuse within a system. Modular prompts and Skills are reuse across systems and teams, and the failure they address is organisational rather than computational.

The pattern is familiar. Four teams each maintain their own copy of a system prompt that started as one prompt. Someone edits one copy to fix a tone problem.

Nine months later the four copies disagree about escalation thresholds, refusal language and output format, nobody can say which is authoritative, and a behaviour change now requires four uncoordinated edits landing at four different times.

Copy-paste reuse is reuse that decays from the moment it is created.

The architectural answer is a versioned, shared, reviewable component: prompt fragments and Skills held in version control, referenced rather than duplicated, changed through review. The property that matters is that a behaviour change becomes one reviewable event with a diff and an author, instead of N silent drifts.

Skills extend that from prompt text to packaged capability: instructions plus supporting resources, versioned as a unit and loaded when relevant rather than pasted into every prompt.

That loading-when-relevant property connects to Domain 3's progressive-discovery objective and back to caching. A monolithic prompt carrying every capability's instructions all the time is both a token cost and, if it changes as capabilities are added, a caching liability.

Modularity has a caching payoff worth making explicit. Splitting a prompt into a stable core and a volatile periphery is the same operation as splitting it into a cacheable prefix and an uncacheable tail. Design the modules along the stability seam and one decision buys both.

Checkpoint 5 · before you read on

A team enables prompt caching on a service with a 6,000-token static preamble. Cost does not move. Investigating, you find the preamble is byte-identical across requests and is assembled by code that appends the retrieved context first, then the preamble, then the user message.

What is wrong, and why did "byte-identical" mislead them?

Answer first, then open
The preamble is identical and it is not a prefix.

Caching matches on the leading portion of the input, in order, from the first byte. Retrieved context sits in front of the preamble and changes on every request, so the match fails at position zero and nothing after it is ever reached.

Why "byte-identical" misled them: it is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. Being unchanged earns nothing unless the unchanged block is also first. Stability and position are two separate requirements and the team verified one of them.

The fix is a reordering, not a rewrite: preamble first, retrieved context and user message last, in ascending order of volatility. No content changes at all, which is what makes this the cheapest fix in the domain.

6. One production design, all five

By the end of this section you will have seen why this domain reads as five objectives and behaves as one.

An insurer builds a policy-servicing assistant. It answers coverage questions, updates contact details, initiates claims, and escalates anything above a value threshold.

Roughly 12,000 conversations a day, averaging fourteen turns. Compliance requires a standing policy text on every model call.

Model selection is decided per call path. The intent-classification step at the front of each turn is bounded, high-volume, and sits in front of a user waiting on a screen, so it takes the lowest tier clearing its benchmarked accuracy bar on real transcripts.

The coverage-reasoning step reads policy language against a described situation and must hold several conditions together, so it takes a higher tier. Its volume is a fraction of the classifier's and its task complexity is the binding constraint. Nobody buys headroom without naming the requirement it serves.

System prompt and guardrails. Register, refusal boundaries, the clarifying-question rule and the escalation triggers live in the system prompt as concrete testable conditions.

The value threshold for escalation also exists as a code-level check on the claim-initiation tool's arguments, because that one must never be argued past. Capabilities the assistant does not need are not exposed at all rather than exposed and monitored.

Prompt techniques are applied per observed failure. Zero-shot handles most turns. The classifier carries five few-shot examples sitting on the one boundary measurement showed it confusing, "change my address" against "change the address on an open claim", each with a one-line rationale.

The coverage-reasoning step carries a reasoning cue, because it genuinely derives across multiple conditions. The classifier does not, because it is single-step and the cue would cost latency on all 12,000 conversations a day for nothing.

Context management runs the hybrid strategy. A case-facts block holds policy number, claim ID, amounts, agreed adjustments and current status, updated as facts appear and positioned outside the summarised region.

Earlier discussion is summarised, the last several turns stay verbatim, and tool results are trimmed to relevant fields before entering context. When a long policy document is read into a call, a key-findings block goes at the top and section headings run throughout, because that document is exactly the shape that loses its middle.

Prompt reuse ties it together. The template puts compliance policy, tool definitions and stable few-shot examples first in a fixed order, forming a cacheable prefix.

Do the arithmetic on that: at fourteen turns per conversation and 12,000 conversations a day, that prefix would otherwise be reprocessed roughly 168,000 times daily.

The case-facts block, summarised history, retrieved documents and current user message follow, in ascending order of volatility. The compliance policy and escalation rules are shared, versioned Skills that the claims and servicing teams both reference rather than copy, so a regulatory wording change is one reviewed commit rather than two divergent edits.

The coupling is the point

Notice how often one decision served two objectives. Ordering the template by stability served both caching and modularity. Trimming tool results served both token cost and attention quality. Putting the guardrail in the system prompt and in code served both behavioural coherence and enforcement.

That coupling is why this domain reads as five objectives and behaves as one.

Where these reflexes show up again

Caching meets Domain 4's optimization objective. "Static content first, then enable caching" is the answer whenever latency and cost appear in the same sentence, and it is the same answer in both domains.

The guardrail layering meets Domain 5. Section 2's rule that a "must never" needs a non-persuadable layer is Domain 5's four guardrail layers, with scoped permissions as the strongest.

The missing-fact row meets Domain 3. Recognising that no prompting technique creates knowledge is what routes a problem to retrieval design instead of another week of prompt iteration.

Common misconceptions

The mythThe safest choice is always the largest model.
Why it's temptingIt sounds risk-averse, and nobody in a design review has ever been criticised for over-provisioning.
The realityModel choice is a cost, latency and quality trade argued from a stated requirement. Oversized capability is unpriced cost, not safety.
The one testName the complexity driver that requires the headroom. If nobody can, it is not safety.
The mythClaude has some memory across calls by default.
Why it's temptingConversations plainly do remember things, and the illusion is convincing because the client usually does resend.
The realityThe API has none. All continuity is the application's responsibility to construct and resend on every request.
The one testHow long is the conversation? If it is short and something was forgotten, the request was missing it.
The mythA bigger context window fixes attention problems.
Why it's temptingBoth the symptom and the remedy involve long inputs, so the two feel like they belong to the same axis.
The realityA larger window changes how much fits, not how attention distributes across what is there.
The one testWas the input near the limit? If not, capacity was never the constraint and a bigger window changes nothing.
The mythCaching means storing the response for reuse.
Why it's temptingThat is exactly what caching means in HTTP and in application caching, so the intuition transfers without friction.
The realityPrompt caching reuses a stable input prefix. It helps on every request sharing a preamble, whatever the user asked.
The one testIs the shared block byte-identical and first? Both conditions, or the hit rate is zero.

Quick reference

Model selection. Task complexity sets the capability floor. Latency budget is a hard gate. The accuracy bar is a number derived from the downstream cost of an error. Volume is the multiplier. Benchmark on representative data before committing. Fine-tuning is not the first move.

System prompts and guardrails. The system prompt is the only durable authority across turns. The first user message decays; environment variables never reach the model. State rules as concrete testable conditions. Anything that must never happen gets enforced outside the model.

Prompt techniques. Diagnose first, then select. Inconsistent form takes 3 to 4 format examples. A confused boundary takes 4 to 6 targeted examples with rationales. A broken multi-step derivation takes a reasoning cue; a single-step task takes none. A missing fact takes retrieval. A stereotyped opening takes a prefill.

Context engineering. Stateless API, full history resent every call. Short-conversation amnesia is missing history, never capacity. Cumulative tokens grow with the square of turn count. Lost in the middle is fixed by position and structure, not by length, rotation or a bigger window. Hybrid strategy: extract precision-critical facts verbatim, summarise low-density discussion, keep recent turns intact, and keep the facts block outside the summarised region.

Prompt reuse. Caching reuses a byte-identical input prefix, never the output. Static first, variable last. Any dynamic value at the top kills the hit rate. Invalidation cascades forward from the first changed byte. It cuts latency and cost together, which is the flag for it. Modular prompts and Skills are versioned, shared and referenced rather than copied.

Every technique here is a targeted instrument. Read the constraint the scenario states, name the failure mode, and pick the instrument built for that failure, not the one that sounds most thorough.
