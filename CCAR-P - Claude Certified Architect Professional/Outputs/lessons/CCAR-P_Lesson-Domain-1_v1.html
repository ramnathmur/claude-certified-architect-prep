Seventeen percent is the second-largest weight on this exam. If items track the published weight, that is roughly eleven of the 63, which is enough for Domain 1 alone to move you across or under 720.

The six objectives look like six separate topics. Read them left to right and they are one argument.

A business states an outcome. You find the decision inside their workflow that a model can own. You wire that decision into a system with an input boundary, a processing core, an output contract, and a feedback path. You pick the simplest pattern that satisfies the stated requirements. You decompose whatever is still too big to evaluate as one unit. And you express the whole thing in the unit the business will measure it in.

Every step in that chain constrains the next, which is also why this domain's distractors work so well.

A wrong answer here is usually a design that is defensible in isolation and unjustified by the requirement the scenario actually stated.

Foundations tested whether a design was correct. Professional tests whether it survives production, and whether you can defend it to someone holding a budget. Four questions run underneath everything below: what does it cost at volume, what breaks first under real traffic, how do you explain it to a non-engineer, and what does a regulated sector add.

The ladder that organises the whole domain

One idea sits at the centre of Domain 1, and most of its questions are, underneath the scenario prose, asking you to place a set of requirements on it.

1. Translating a business problem

By the end of this section you will be able to find the one sentence in a scenario that disqualifies half the options, and you will look for it before reading them.

A business problem arrives as an outcome, never a specification.

"We take too long to issue quotes." "Our support backlog grows faster than we can hire." "Underwriters spend their week reading PDFs."

None of those name a system, and none of them can be built. An outcome has no interface.

Translation is converting an outcome into a decision: a specific, recurring point in a workflow where someone looks at inputs and produces a judgment.

Systems get built around decisions. That is why this is its own objective, and it is worth understanding why the previous generation of automation could skip it.

Rules engines and RPA were pointed at tasks: click here, copy that field, paste it there. That works as long as the input never varies. Language models moved the automatable unit from the task to the judgment, and that made the sourcing question harder. The question stopped being "which clicks repeat" and became "which judgments repeat, and can we see whether they were right."

What makes a decision a candidate

Four properties argue for it.

Scale exceeds practical human capacity. Volume, frequency or concurrency is past what the team can sustain. Without this you are building an expensive assistant.

The decision is a repeatable pattern with observable feedback. The same shape of judgment recurs, and somebody eventually finds out whether it was right. No feedback surface means no evaluation, and no evaluation means no improvement path.

The context is machine-readable, or can be made so. If the inputs live in a filing cabinet or in an employee's head, the first project is data access, not AI.

Throughput justifies the fixed build cost. Amortisation is a design input rather than an afterthought.

Five signals that the stance itself is wrong

These matter more than the four above, because the exam hides them in the setup paragraph.

Decisions are irreversible and rare. No learning surface, high blast radius. The worst combination available.

Regulation explicitly requires a human decision-maker. Statutory human-in-the-loop is a constraint, not a preference to be optimised around.

The output is novel-creative judgment with low repeatability. Each output is bespoke, so there is no pattern to match.

The human relationship is the product. Coaching, therapy, relationship-managed sales. Automating the relationship removes the thing being bought.

A human must gate every single iteration. If the user refines, selects or approves inside every loop pass, you are designing an assistive tool. That is a legitimate product. It is not usually the architecture the scenario is asking for, and it changes the value case completely.

One thing to notice about all five. They rarely apply to a whole workflow. They apply to individual steps inside it, which is why translation almost always produces a split: some steps automated, some drafted then approved, some left alone.

Worked example

A commercial insurance broker issues quotes. The measured current state: 400 quote requests a week, 22 minutes average handling time, three analysts at roughly 35 productive hours each.

That is 105 hours of capacity against about 147 hours of work, which is why the backlog is permanent rather than seasonal. Turnaround averages 3.2 business days, and the broker loses business above two days.

Now decompose the 22 minutes.

The risk-tier step deserves a second look. Its feedback loop is twelve months long, which does not disqualify it. It forces a proxy signal instead, and analyst override rate is the obvious one.

The backlog disappears, and notice what the value story became: turnaround, not headcount.

How the exam probes it

The scenario gives you an organisation, a business goal, a workload figure, and one or two constraints buried in the prose. All four options automate something. They differ in which step they target.

The correct option targets the step that either consumes the stated cost or carries the stated requirement. The distractors target a step that is visible but cheap, or a step the scenario has already told you is constrained.

Watch for the constraint sentence. "Quotes must be signed by a licensed underwriter" is one clause in a paragraph of context, and it invalidates every option that has the model issuing the quote. Professional-tier items hide the disqualifying constraint in the setup rather than in the options.

Checkpoint 1 · before you read on

A wealth manager asks whether Claude can handle their client review meetings, which take advisers about four hours a week each. The scenario notes in passing that clients choose this firm for the relationship with their named adviser.

What do you automate, and what has that clause just ruled out?

Answer first, then open
Automate the preparation, never the interaction.

"Clients choose this firm for the relationship" is the wrong-stance signal the human relationship is the product. Automating the meeting removes the thing being bought, so every option that has a model conducting or replacing the review is disqualified by one clause in the setup.

What is left is still substantial: portfolio summarisation, drafting talking points, assembling performance data, flagging items needing discussion. The adviser walks in prepared instead of spending the morning preparing.

Notice the shape, because it recurs: the signal did not apply to the whole workflow, only to one step in it. Translation almost always produces a split rather than a yes or no.

The wrong turns

Building what was asked for instead of what was wanted. "Can Claude write our quotes?" is a request. "We lose business above two days' turnaround" is the requirement. An option producing beautifully written quotes that does not touch turnaround has solved the request.

Skipping the baseline. If you cannot state the current cost in a unit, you have nothing to compare against, and section 6 becomes unanswerable later. On the exam this appears as an option proposing to "measure improvement after launch," which is measurement with no before.

Treating a wrong-stance signal as a challenge to engineer around. When a scenario states a regulatory human-decision requirement, the correct answer accommodates it. An audit log, a confidence threshold, or post-hoc review offered as a substitute for the required human decision is the trap.

Automating the relationship when the human contact is the product being sold. The right answer automates the preparation.

2. End-to-end architecture

By the end of this section you will be able to look at a working deployed system and say why it will never get any better.

Four stages, and the fourth is the one people ship without.

Input is the boundary, and three jobs happen there. Acquisition, whether webhook, queue, batch or upload. Validation and normalisation: does this document parse, is this field present, is this file the type it claims. And the trust decision.

Everything crossing the input boundary from outside your control is untrusted content. That is a prompt-injection surface, and where it enters determines where it must be constrained.

Input design also fixes what the rest of the system can never recover. Information not captured at the boundary does not exist downstream, at any price.

Processing is the model call plus the deterministic code wrapped around it. The most common design error at this stage is putting work in the model that code does better.

Arithmetic, lookups, deduplication, sorting, threshold comparison, schema validation. All cheaper, faster and exactly correct in code. The model's job is judgment on unstructured input.

Output is a contract, not a text blob. It has a schema, a destination, a consumer, and an error tolerance, and the consumer determines the shape.

A downstream service needs parseable structure and a confidence field it can route on. A human reviewer needs the reasoning and the citations. An auditor needs the inputs, the prompt version, and the timestamp. Design for the consumer the scenario names.

Feedback is what makes it a system rather than a script, and it is the stage the exam cares about most, because it is the one most often missing.

Feedback, properly

Three sources, with very different latencies and costs.

Implicit signals are the reviewer editing a draft, overriding a category, deleting a suggestion. Immediate, and near zero cost, because you already have both versions.

Explicit signals are thumbs up and down, a labelling queue, a correction form. Immediate to days, and they require human effort, so volume is low.

Outcome signals are whether the quote converted, whether the ticket reopened within seven days, what the twelve-month loss ratio was. Free to collect, and they arrive late and confounded.

Implicit signals are the workhorse, and the reason is worth stating plainly.

The analogy

A reviewer's edit is a labelled training pair you are already paying to produce. The model's output and the human's shipped version are input and corrected output, generated by a person you employ anyway. A system that puts a reviewer in the path and then overwrites the field in place is discarding a labelled dataset it is funding, which is a thing no team would tolerate if it were phrased that way in a budget meeting.

Where it breaks This data has selection bias baked in, and training data usually does not. You only see corrections on the cases a human actually looked at, so the set over-represents whatever your routing sends to review and under-represents the auto-approved stream entirely. Treat it as a high-value evaluation corpus rather than a representative sample, and keep sampling the auto-approved path separately, or you will tune hard on the segment you were already watching.

A feedback loop is only closed when the signal reaches something that changes behaviour. So name the destination: the evaluation set, the prompt, the retrieval layer, the routing thresholds, or model selection.

Worked example

Support ticket triage at 12,000 tickets a month.

Input. A webhook on ticket creation. Attachments extracted to text, and anything that fails extraction routes to a human queue rather than proceeding with empty content. PII redaction runs before the model call, because that requirement is deterministic and pre-model.

Customer tier and account history are attached at the boundary. That is the architect's decision rather than the model's, it costs nothing there, and it is unrecoverable later.

Processing. Retrieval over the knowledge base scoped to the product line named in the ticket, classification into one of 14 categories, and a drafted first response with citations.

Output. A JSON contract: category, confidence, draft_response, citations[], suggested_priority, prompt_version. Routing is code, not judgment: at or above threshold auto-assigns, below it goes to a human triager with the draft attached.

Feedback, three signals wired to three destinations.

Category overrides run at roughly 4%, about 480 a month, and each one is a labelled example. They cluster, so a monthly review of the confusion pairs drives prompt and category-definition changes.

Edit distance between the draft and what the agent actually sent. High-edit drafts get pulled into the eval set; low-edit drafts confirm the pattern works.

Reopen rate at seven days, joined back to the original category and confidence. This is the outcome signal, and it catches what the other two cannot: triage that looked confidently correct and resolved the ticket wrongly.

Now do the arithmetic on that first signal. 480 labelled examples a month is a substantial eval corpus generated for free, and it exists only because the architecture routed the override event somewhere instead of overwriting the field in place.

Checkpoint 2 · before you read on

A document classifier has been in production for six months at 89% accuracy. Two people review its output daily and correct what it gets wrong. Accuracy has been 89% every month since launch.

Say why it has not moved, and name the cheapest fix.

Answer first, then open
The corrections are being made and then thrown away.

Two reviewers correcting output daily are generating labelled pairs continuously. If accuracy has not moved in six months, those corrections are ending at the record they fixed. The loop has three stages and no fourth.

The cheapest fix is capturing the override event rather than only the corrected value, and routing it into the eval set and the prompt. No new model, no new data collection, no new headcount. The data already exists and is being deleted.

What the distractors will offer: a larger model, more few-shot examples chosen by the team, or a monitoring dashboard. The first two guess at what is wrong; the third measures the flatness more precisely without changing it.

The wrong turns

Calling logging a feedback loop. Logs answer what the system did. Feedback answers whether it was right, and what changed as a result.

A human review step whose output is discarded. The single most common real-world gap, and it appears repeatedly as a distractor's blind spot.

Repairing downstream what the input boundary could have prevented. Adding an output filter to catch a document type the input validator should have rejected. This distractor family is persistently attractive because the downstream fix is easier to describe.

Designing the output for yourself instead of the consumer. Rich prose to a service that needs a parseable field, or a bare enum to a human who needs to know why.

Where this goes next: you have stages. Section 3 decides what runs inside the processing box, and it is the decision the whole domain is built around.

3. Choosing a pattern

By the end of this section you will have a single test that places almost any scenario on the ladder, and you will be able to argue it in both directions.

Here is the ladder again, with what each rung buys and what it costs.

The five rungs, priced
RungWhat it isBuys youCosts you
Plain callOne prompt, one responseStructural determinism, lowest latency and cost, trivially evaluableNothing beyond the model's parametric knowledge
Augmented LLMModel + retrieval, tools, or memoryCurrent and private data; the ability to actA retrieval layer to build and evaluate; tool-call latency
WorkflowFixed steps orchestrated in codeEach step separately promptable, testable and model-assignable; predictable cost and latency; gates between stepsCannot handle a path you did not enumerate
AgentModel chooses the sequence; a loop terminates on a stop conditionHandles tasks whose path is not knowable in advanceNondeterministic cost and latency; harder evaluation; wider blast radius per action
Multi-agentCoordinator plus specialistsContext isolation; parallelism; per-role tool privilegesToken multiplication; coordination overhead; information loss at every handoff

The discriminator is enumerability. Can you write down, in advance, the set of paths a request might take?

If yes, code them. That is a workflow, and it will be cheaper, faster, more testable and easier to debug than an agent doing the same thing.

If the path depends on what the model discovers partway through, and those discoveries cannot be enumerated, you need an agent.

The analogy

Choosing a rung is choosing a model size, one level up. You already know not to reach for the largest model because it is the newest; you reach for the smallest one that clears the bar, and you make the case with a measurement. The ladder is the same discipline applied to architecture, and it fails the same way: by defaulting to the top of the range because the top sounds like the serious answer.

Where it breaks A model swap is one parameter you can A/B in an afternoon and reverse just as fast. A rung change is a rewrite: different code, different evaluation strategy, different cost distribution, different debugging surface. So the cost of being wrong is far higher here, which means the decision deserves more scrutiny up front and far less "we can always change it later" than a model choice does.

Two clarifications people get wrong in both directions

A workflow can branch. Routing a request to one of five sub-prompts based on a classification step is still a workflow, because the five branches are enumerated.

Branching is not agency. The model choosing among enumerated options is a routing workflow. The model choosing what to do next without a fixed menu is an agent.

An agent is not a smarter workflow. It is a workflow whose control flow has been handed to a nondeterministic component. That is the right trade in some cases and a bad trade in most.

The five named workflow patterns worth having cold: prompt chaining (fixed sequence, each step feeding the next, with optional programmatic gates between); routing (classify, then dispatch to a specialised handler); parallelisation (independent subtasks fanned out and aggregated, either sectioning by aspect or voting for reliability); orchestrator-worker (a controlling step dispatching to workers, with dispatch logic in code); and evaluator-optimiser (a generator and an independent critic, looping until the critic passes).

That last one earns a warning. An evaluator-optimiser only works if the evaluator is genuinely independent, meaning a separate call that does not see the generator's reasoning. A generator asked to self-review inside the same context has already rationalised its choices, and it will confirm them.

The hybrid answer

Real systems are rarely on one rung, and the answer the exam rewards is often a workflow handling the enumerated majority with an escape hatch to an agent for the remainder.

Take invoice processing at 50,000 a month. 96% come from 40 known vendors in stable formats; 4% are one-off, malformed or novel.

A pure workflow fails on the 4%, dropping 2,000 items a month into a human queue. A pure agent pays agent-grade cost and latency on the 96% that never needed it.

Now cost all three. Take $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens. The exact rates change over time; the arithmetic does not.

Against a stated 10-second SLA the workflow passes and the agent fails, and no amount of prompt tuning changes that.

How the exam probes it

Two mirror-image shapes, and you will see both.

Shape A: the scenario sounds sophisticated and the requirements are enumerable. Prose full of "intelligent," "adaptive" and "end-to-end," over a workload that is five known steps. The correct answer is a workflow, and the agentic option is the distractor that sounds more capable while nothing in the requirements calls for it.

Shape B: the scenario states a requirement the workflow cannot meet. The number of steps is not knowable in advance, the relevant tools are discovered at runtime, the next action depends on what the last one found. Here the agent is correct, and the workflow option is the half-move that handles the enumerated part while silently dropping the requirement that made the task non-enumerable.

Both directions get tested. Drilling only "do not over-engineer" will lose you Shape B items, which is exactly how a one-directional habit becomes a repeat miss.

A third shape asks you to justify a choice rather than make it. The correct statement cites a requirement from the scenario; the distractors cite a general virtue such as "agents are more flexible," true in the abstract and unconnected to this scenario.

Checkpoint 3 · before you read on

A compliance team wants a system to investigate suspicious transaction alerts. The scenario says an investigator "follows the evidence wherever it leads," pulling different records depending on what each lookup reveals, and that no two investigations take the same path.

Which rung, and what specifically in that sentence decided it?

Answer first, then open
Agent. "No two investigations take the same path" is the enumerability test failing explicitly.

The path depends on what each lookup reveals, and those discoveries cannot be listed in advance. A workflow cannot handle a path nobody enumerated, so the requirement rules out the cheaper rung rather than merely making it less elegant.

Why this is worth practising: most people drill the over-engineering direction until "workflow" becomes reflex. This is Shape B, where reflex loses the mark. The workflow option here will look responsible and will quietly drop the requirement that made the task non-enumerable.

What still applies: the agent gets the narrowest tool set that does the job, and there is almost certainly a routine subset of alerts that should never reach it.

The wrong turns

Agentic by default. The most expensive habit in the field. Agency is a cost centre justified by non-enumerability and by nothing else.

Workflow by reflex when the scenario has disqualified it. Over-correction is a real failure mode. Read for the sentence saying the path cannot be known in advance.

Confusing branching with agency. Enumerated branches are still a workflow.

Reaching for multi-agent when one agent with the right tools suffices. Rung five is not the top of a mountain. It is the rung with the worst cost multiplier and the most information loss.

Self-review inside the generator's context. An evaluator that sees the generator's reasoning is not an evaluator.

4. Multi-agent systems

By the end of this section you will diagnose a multi-agent failure by looking at the coordinator first, which is where the exam puts nearly all of them.

Multi-agent systems are hub-and-spoke. One coordinator, several specialist subagents, and no direct communication between subagents. Everything flows through the coordinator.

That single constraint is the whole design, and it buys four things.

Context isolation is simultaneously the main benefit and the main hazard.

It is why parallel subagents do not pollute each other's reasoning. It is also why a synthesis subagent produces a report with missing citations: it never received the source metadata, because the coordinator passed merged free text instead of a structure separating content from provenance.

The instinct to fix that by telling the synthesis agent to "remember to cite sources" fails for a mechanical reason. It cannot cite metadata that never reached it.

The four things the coordinator owns

Decomposition. How the problem is partitioned before delegation. Partition explicitly, assigning distinct subtopics or source types, or subagents will overlap and leave gaps.

Context passing. What each subagent receives, in what structure. Content and metadata stay separate so attribution survives aggregation.

Error handling. Subagents return structured failure context: failure type, what was attempted, partial results, viable alternatives. A bare error string gives the coordinator nothing to decide with.

Termination. When the loop stops, which requires stated quality criteria. Without them a refinement loop either stops too early or does not stop.

Orchestration strategies

Four shapes, and when each earns its place
StrategyShapeUse when
Parallel fan-outCoordinator dispatches N independent subagents at once, aggregates resultsSubtasks are genuinely independent and wall-clock latency matters
Sequential chainingSubagent B's input depends on A's outputA real dependency exists. Do not serialise independent work
Iterative refinementDelegate, synthesise, evaluate for gaps, re-delegate targeted queries, re-synthesiseCoverage cannot be guaranteed in one pass
Evaluator-optimiserGenerator plus an independent critic, looping until criteria passQuality is checkable and the first attempt is often insufficient

Two mechanical points decide items on their own.

Parallelism requires dispatch in a single coordinator turn. Issuing the same delegations across separate turns runs them sequentially, the design intent is lost, and no error is raised.

Coordinator prompts state goals and quality criteria, never step-by-step procedure. A procedural coordinator prompt produces checklist-shaped, shallow results, because it has converted an adaptive system into a brittle script. The fix for a shallow research coordinator is a goal-oriented rewrite, not more detailed steps and not more subagents.

When multi-agent is actually justified

Three conditions, and you want at least two present.

Independent parallelisable subtasks where wall-clock time is a stated constraint. Context that would not fit or would interfere in one window: four research threads at 30k tokens each accumulate to 120k in a single agent, with every thread's noise contaminating every other's reasoning. And genuinely different tool privileges per role: a drafting agent that must not have write access, a verification agent that must not have search access.

If none of those hold, one agent with the right tool set does the job at a fraction of the cost.

Take a research report over four independent domains. A single agent runs sequentially, with context accumulating to roughly 160k tokens by the final synthesis, and everything it read about domain one still occupying the window while it reasons about domain four.

The multi-agent version pays for itself on wall clock. If branches take 40, 55, 35 and 50 seconds, you pay about 55 seconds rather than about 180.

Token cost goes the other way: typically 4 to 5 times the single-agent spend, because context that was shared is now duplicated across isolated agents.

Multi-agent trades tokens for latency, isolation and privilege separation. If the scenario names none of those three, the trade is unfunded.

How the exam probes it

Root-cause questions dominate, and they share one shape: the symptom appears in the output and the answer names something the coordinator did.

The report misses whole subtopics while every subagent returned correct results, so decomposition was too narrow. Two subagents investigated the same ground, so the coordinator did not partition before delegating, and the fix is partitioning up front rather than deduplication after.

Citations are wrong or missing, so content and metadata were passed as merged text. Synthesis has coverage gaps, so the coordinator evaluates and re-delegates targeted queries, and it does not hand the synthesis agent a search tool, because that dissolves the least-privilege boundary that justified the architecture.

A subagent failure killed the whole run, so you need structured error propagation plus a coordinator recovery policy. And human reviewers keep re-asking for information the system already gathered, so the handoff payload was a flag or free text rather than a structured summary that stands alone.

A trap worth memorising

An empty result is not a failure. "Zero results found" is a valid and informative finding. A timeout is an access failure requiring a retry decision. Conflating the two produces pointless retries in one direction and lost findings in the other, and the two failure modes look identical in a bare error string.

Checkpoint 4 · before you read on

A research system runs a coordinator plus five subagents. The final report is thorough on three topics, silent on two others that were in scope, and every subagent's individual output was checked and found correct.

Where is the fault, and what will the tempting fix fail to do?

Answer first, then open
Decomposition. The coordinator never delegated the two missing topics.

Every subagent returned correct results, which rules the subagents out entirely. Correct answers to the wrong set of questions is a partitioning failure, and partitioning happens before any subagent is invoked.

The tempting fix is more subagents, and it fails for a mechanical reason: more agents executing the same bad partition produce more of the same gap. You get a longer report with the same two holes.

The real fix is explicit partitioning up front, and for coverage that cannot be guaranteed in one pass, iterative refinement: synthesise, have the coordinator evaluate for gaps, re-delegate targeted queries, re-synthesise.

The wrong turns

Fixing the subagent when the coordinator caused the problem. The most common root-cause miss in this domain.

Instructing rather than configuring. Telling a subagent not to use a tool it possesses is probabilistic. Removing the tool is deterministic. Least privilege means removing the capability, not logging or confirming its use.

Adding subagents to fix a decomposition problem, or adding more detailed coordinator steps to fix shallow output. The second deepens the rigidity that caused it.

Unbounded refinement. A loop without stated sufficiency criteria does not terminate on judgment. It terminates on a limit, which is a different and worse thing.

5. Decomposition

By the end of this section you will have one rule for where to split and, just as important, one rule for when to stop.

Decomposition is the technique the other five objectives keep calling.

Translation decomposes a workflow into steps. Architecture decomposes a system into stages. Pattern selection decomposes a population into the enumerable majority and the awkward remainder. Orchestration decomposes a problem space before delegating.

Four things go wrong when a task stays too large for one unit of work.

Attention dilutes. A single pass over fourteen files goes deep on some and shallow on others, and you cannot predict which.

Evaluation criteria mix. If one unit is judged on extraction accuracy and tone and completeness at once, a failing score does not tell you what failed.

Partial failure becomes unrecoverable. One monolithic call either succeeds or you redo everything.

There is nowhere to put a gate. Validation, human approval and cost controls need a seam, and a monolith has none.

The second one gives the rule worth carrying into the exam.

Split until each unit has one evaluation criterion and one failure mode. Stop when the coordination overhead exceeds the benefit.

Both halves matter, and the second half is the one people skip. Over-decomposition is a real failure: every split adds a handoff, and every handoff loses information that was implicit in the shared context. A ten-step chain where three steps could have been one is three extra opportunities for something to fall out.

The five techniques

Decomposition techniques and what each fits
TechniqueSplit byFits
FunctionalStage of the processA predictable pipeline: extract, validate, enrich, format
AspectualDimension of analysisSame input, different lenses: a security pass, a performance pass, a style pass
Data-partitionChunks of the corpusMap-reduce over 200 documents; per-file then cross-file
Confidence-tierHow hard the case isCheap path for the routine 90%, expensive path for the rest
Risk-tierBlast radiusReversible actions autonomous; irreversible actions gated

The last two are the Professional-tier ones, and it is worth naming why.

Confidence-tier decomposition is where cost control actually lives. Routing by difficulty rather than running every item through the most capable path is the main architectural lever you have on spend.

Risk-tier decomposition is where governance meets architecture. It is the honest answer whenever a scenario mixes routine actions with one action nobody can undo.

Cutting across all five is the choice between a fixed pipeline and dynamic adaptive decomposition. Fixed when structure is predictable and reproducibility matters. Dynamic when scope is unknown up front and each step depends on what the last one discovered.

That is the enumerability discriminator from section 3 again, applied to the shape of the plan rather than the shape of the runtime.

Worked example

A pull request touching 14 files, roughly 800 changed lines.

Run it as a single pass and three things happen, all predictable. Attention dilutes, so files seven through fourteen get a lighter read than files one through three. Verdicts become inconsistent, with a pattern flagged in one file and approved in another. And cross-file issues get missed, because the model is spending its capacity on local detail.

How the exam probes it

"Which decomposition strategy?" The stem describes the task's scope and you match fixed against adaptive. Open-ended investigation with unknown scope takes adaptive; a templated repeating review takes the fixed pipeline, and there the adaptive option is unnecessary complexity.

"Output is shallow, inconsistent, or misses cross-cutting issues." Diagnose the decomposition. The answer usually adds a separate pass with a different evaluation criterion. A larger model and more detailed instructions are the two distractors.

"Where should the split be?" The right seam is where two different evaluation criteria meet, or where a gate must be inserted. The tempting seam is wherever the process has a natural-sounding name.

Over-decomposition items. Less frequent, and they exist: a design split so finely that context is lost between handoffs, where the correct answer merges steps. If you have only ever drilled "split it up," this reads as counterintuitive and you will miss it.

Checkpoint 5 · before you read on

A contract-review pipeline has eleven sequential steps. Reviewers report that the final risk summary often contradicts findings from step three, and that the system "seems to forget" details it clearly extracted earlier.

Which way does this one go, and what is the tell?

Answer first, then open
Over-decomposition. Merge steps.

The tell is "forgets details it clearly extracted." The information existed and was lost in transit, which is a handoff problem, not a capability problem. Every split adds a handoff, and every handoff drops whatever was implicit in the shared context.

Eleven steps for a contract review is very likely several units that share one evaluation criterion. Apply the rule in both directions: split until each unit has one criterion and one failure mode, and stop when the coordination overhead exceeds the benefit.

Why this item is worth practising: almost all decomposition drilling runs one way. If your reflex is "add a pass," this scenario takes the mark off you.

6. Business value pillars

By the end of this section you will treat a stated SLA as a filter you apply before comparing designs, not as a tiebreaker you apply after.

The exam guide names five pillars: efficiency, transformation, productivity, cost, and performance SLAs.

They are not five framings of the same thing. They are five different units, and the one the scenario names determines which architecture is correct.

Five pillars, five units
PillarThe unitThe question it answers
EfficiencyInput per unit of output: minutes per quote, FTE-hours per thousand ticketsAre we doing the same work with less?
TransformationExistence: something not previously done at allAre we doing something we could not do before?
ProductivityOutput per person, or share of time on higher-value workAre the same people producing more, or better?
CostUnit economics: cost per transaction, total run rateWhat does the solution itself cost to operate?
Performance SLALatency, availability, accuracy, expressed as a commitmentDoes it meet the promise we made?

Efficiency and productivity sit close enough together to be worth separating carefully.

Efficiency reduces the input to a fixed output. Productivity changes what the freed capacity does. The same project reads two ways: "we cut handling time 40%" is efficiency, and "underwriters now spend Friday on broker relationships instead of PDFs" is productivity.

Which one you lead with depends on who is listening, and the exam does sometimes ask exactly that.

Cost is not inference cost. Total cost includes inference, retrieval infrastructure, human review time, the cost of errors, and maintenance. A design that halves inference cost while doubling human review time has made things worse, and the arithmetic showing it is short.

Pillars as architectural constraints

This is what makes section 6 an architecture objective rather than a communications one.

A latency SLA constrains the pattern. Say the commitment is p95 under 3.5 seconds and a single model call runs about 1.5s at p95.

A five-step serial chain is about 7.5s, so it fails. The same five steps with three of them parallel is roughly 1.5 plus 1.5 plus 1.5, about 4.5s, and it still fails. Move the two enrichment steps off the request path by precomputing at ingest, and the request path is two calls at about 3s, which passes with about 500ms of margin. An agent averaging nine calls is not in the conversation, and its tail is worse than its mean.

Notice what the SLA did there. It did not rank the designs. It eliminated a rung of the ladder before ranking started.

A cost pillar at volume constrains model selection and decomposition. That is where confidence-tier decomposition earns its place, and where prompt caching and the ordering of static content become architectural rather than cosmetic decisions.

A transformation pillar loosens constraints the others impose. If the business case is that a capability did not previously exist, per-unit efficiency comparison has no denominator. A design that would fail an efficiency test can be correct under a transformation pillar, and the reverse. Read for which pillar the scenario named.

Worked example

The invoice pipeline again, argued three ways for three audiences. Same system, same numbers.

To the CFO, on cost. Processing cost falls from $4.10 per invoice in fully manual handling to $0.11 in compute plus $0.38 in residual human review, on 50,000 invoices a month. Annual run rate on the platform is roughly $65,000 against about $2.4M in current handling cost.

To the operations director, on efficiency and productivity. Average handling time drops from 11 minutes to under 2. The team of sixty stops keying invoices and moves to exception handling and vendor disputes, which is the work that was permanently deferred.

To the CIO, on SLA. p95 processing time is 7.5 seconds against a 10-second commitment, with a documented escape route for the 4% taking the agentic path and a human queue behind that.

Three sentences, three units, one architecture. Producing them is the skill this objective tests, and it is what Domain 6's communication objective builds on.

Checkpoint 6 · before you read on

Two designs both work. Design A is more accurate and uses a seven-call agentic loop. Design B is slightly less accurate and uses a three-step workflow. The scenario's second paragraph mentions a contractual commitment to respond within four seconds at p95, and a single call runs about 1.5s.

Which one, and at what point in your reading should you have known?

Answer first, then open
Design B, and you should have known before reading either option.

Seven calls at 1.5s is about 10.5s at p95, against a 4-second commitment. Design A is not a worse choice, it is a disqualified one, and its accuracy advantage never gets weighed because there is nothing to weigh it against.

The timing point is the real lesson. A stated SLA is a filter you apply to the option set before you start comparing, not a tiebreaker you reach for once two designs look close. Read the setup paragraph for any latency, availability or turnaround number and apply it first.

The trap is choosing the technically superior design that fails the named pillar. It will be described attractively, and its accuracy figure will be real.

The wrong turns

Optimising an unnamed pillar. A cost-optimised answer to a latency question is wrong, however elegant.

Speaking in model metrics to a business audience. Accuracy is an input to the business case, not the business case.

Counting only inference cost. Human review, error remediation and retrieval infrastructure are part of the number.

Leading with efficiency when the value is transformational. A capability that did not exist has no baseline to be efficient against, and forcing one understates the case.

7. One design, all six objectives

By the end of this section you will have seen a single constraint found in objective 1 propagate all the way to the sentence that satisfies a Chief Risk Officer in objective 6.

A regional insurer wants to cut claims cycle time. 8,000 first-notice-of-loss claims a month, an average of 6.4 days to first decision, 40 handlers.

The regulatory position: claim denials require a licensed adjuster's decision. Approvals under $2,500 do not. There is a stated 24-hour acknowledgement commitment, currently missed 30% of the time.

Objective 1, translate. The outcome is cycle time. Decomposing the handler's work locates the decisions: intake extraction from a mixed bundle of forms, photos and emails; coverage verification against the policy; severity and complexity triage; and approve or deny.

Extraction and triage are automated. Coverage verification is retrieval plus deterministic checks. Denial stays human, and the wrong-stance signal that produced that was regulation, not difficulty.

Objective 2, end-to-end. Input: the claim bundle arrives by email or portal, attachments extracted, anything unparseable routed to a human queue rather than proceeding empty, and the policy number validated against the policy system before any model call.

Output is a JSON contract carrying claim_summary, coverage_status, severity_tier, confidence, recommended_action, citations and prompt_version, designed for two consumers: the routing service and the adjuster.

Feedback runs on three signals: adjuster overrides of severity tier, edit distance on the claim summary, and reopen-within-30-days as the outcome measure.

Objective 3, pattern. A standard auto claim is enumerable: five steps, every time. That is a workflow. Complex liability claims with multiple parties are not enumerable, because the number of parties, documents and lookups is discovered during the work. Those route to an agent, behind a validation gate deciding which population a claim belongs to.

Objective 4, orchestration. The complex path uses a coordinator with three specialists: document analysis, policy interpretation, precedent lookup. They run in parallel from one coordinator turn, because they are independent and the 24-hour commitment makes wall clock a stated requirement.

The coordinator partitions explicitly, passes findings as structured content plus metadata so the final summary can cite the clause it relied on, and evaluates the summary for gaps before accepting it. When a claim escalates, the payload is a structured handoff, because the adjuster does not see the transcript.

Objective 5, decomposition. Three splits on three different criteria. A functional split along the pipeline. A confidence-tier split routing simple auto claims down the cheap path. And a risk-tier split putting every denial and every approval above $2,500 behind a human gate regardless of confidence.

Note where that third seam came from. Regulation set it, not engineering judgment.

Objective 6, value. To the COO: 6.4 days to a projected 1.8, with 24-hour acknowledgement moving from 70% to a designed 98%. To the CFO: about $0.31 per standard claim and $1.20 per complex one, against $47 in current handling cost. To the Chief Risk Officer: no denial is issued without a licensed adjuster's decision, every coverage determination cites the clause it relied on, and the audit trail carries the prompt version used.

The dependency chain is the point

Read those six back in order and follow one thread. The regulatory constraint found in objective 1 set the risk-tier seam in objective 5, which set the human gate in objective 2's output routing, which is the sentence that satisfies the Chief Risk Officer in objective 6.

That chain is what "end-to-end architecture" means, and it is what the exam is testing when it hides a constraint in the setup paragraph.

Where these reflexes show up again

Three of this domain's habits reappear elsewhere under other names.

The feedback destination meets Domain 4's evaluation loop. Section 2's rule that a signal needs a destination that changes behaviour is the same rule as Domain 4's return arrow from production into the eval set. Same mechanism, same failure when it is missing.

Least privilege meets Domain 3 and Domain 5. "Remove the tool rather than instruct against it" is section 4's coordinator rule, Domain 3's authorization objective, and Domain 5's strongest guardrail layer. Learn it once.

Confidence-tier decomposition meets Domain 4's optimization objective. Routing the routine majority to a cheap path and escalating the hard minority is section 5's cost lever and Domain 4's cascade, described from two different directions.

Common misconceptions

The mythAgentic is the modern architecture; workflows are legacy.
Why it's temptingEvery conference talk and vendor deck is about agents, so the ladder reads as a timeline of what serious teams have moved on to.
The realityThe ladder is a cost ladder, not a maturity ladder. Move right only when a stated requirement makes the left rung impossible.
The one testPoint at the sentence in the scenario that the cheaper rung cannot satisfy. If you cannot find one, the cheaper rung is the answer.
The mythThe subagent produced the bad output, so fix the subagent.
Why it's temptingThe defect is visible in that subagent's output, and that is where anyone naturally looks first.
The realityIn hub-and-spoke, most output defects trace to coordinator decomposition, context passing, or termination criteria.
The one testWere the subagents' individual outputs correct? If yes, the fault is upstream of all of them.
The mythHuman review in the loop means the system will improve.
Why it's temptingA reviewer correcting output looks exactly like a learning loop, and the corrections are real.
The realityOnly if the correction is captured and routed somewhere. Reviewers whose edits are discarded generate no learning at all.
The one testName the destination the correction reaches. If the answer is "the record it fixed," the loop is open.
The mythCost means inference cost.
Why it's temptingInference is the line item that is new, itemised, and easy to attribute to the project.
The realityTotal cost includes human review, error remediation, retrieval infrastructure and maintenance. Halving inference while doubling review is a regression.
The one testAdd the human minutes to the arithmetic. If the option never mentions them, it has not costed the design.

Quick reference

The ladder. Plain call, augmented LLM, workflow, agent, multi-agent. Move right only on a stated requirement. The discriminator is whether you can enumerate the paths.

Workflow patterns. Prompt chaining, routing, parallelisation (sectioning or voting), orchestrator-worker, evaluator-optimiser. The evaluator must be independent.

Four stages. Input (acquire, validate, normalise, trust boundary), processing (model plus deterministic code), output (schema, destination, consumer, error tolerance), feedback (signal plus a destination that changes behaviour).

Feedback signals. Implicit (edits and overrides, cheapest and best), explicit (labels and ratings), outcome (conversion, reopen, loss ratio, late but decisive).

The coordinator owns decomposition, context passing, error handling and termination criteria. Root causes live there.

Multi-agent is justified by parallelism under a latency requirement, context isolation, or per-role tool privilege. Expect 4 to 5 times the tokens.

Decomposition techniques. Functional, aspectual, data-partition, confidence-tier (the cost lever), risk-tier (the governance lever). Fixed pipeline for known scope; dynamic adaptive for discovered scope.

Granularity rule. One evaluation criterion and one failure mode per unit. Stop when handoff overhead exceeds benefit.

Five value pillars. Efficiency (input per output), transformation (a capability that did not exist), productivity (output per person), cost (total, including human time), performance SLA (a commitment that filters designs before comparison).
