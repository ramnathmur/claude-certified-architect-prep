All five objectives here are versions of one question. Where does a property of this system actually come from?

Start with what a model is. It is a conditional probability distribution over text. Give it a well-phrased instruction and it will usually comply.

Usually. Not always.

That is enough to build extraordinary products on. It is not enough to found a guarantee on, and governance is the discipline of guarantees.

Anything that must tend to happen can live in the prompt. Anything that must always happen has to live somewhere deterministic.

That split is the discriminator behind most of what this domain tests, and each objective is one face of it.

Guardrails convert a tendency into a boundary. Failure modes are the catalogue of what breaks when you mistake one for the other. Human-in-the-loop is the mechanism for decisions no automated control can settle alone.

Compliance is the case where an external authority already decided a property is a guarantee and you do not get a vote. Ethics is the case where nobody has written the property down yet, and the architect has to name it.

One more thing about the tier. A Foundations answer names the right control. A Professional answer states what it costs, what it does not cover, and how you would explain it to the person signing the contract.

Next: we start with the controls themselves, and with the reason four modest ones beat one excellent one.

1. Guardrails and the four layers

By the end of this section you will be able to say why four modest controls beat one excellent one, and which of the four is strongest regardless of how well the others are built.

A guardrail is a constraint that holds independently of what the model generates.

Put the constraint in the prompt and you have not built a guardrail. You have built a request, and it is subject to the same probabilistic process it was meant to restrain. It can be argued with, out-competed by a longer instruction elsewhere in context, or simply skipped on the tail of the distribution.

Independence from the model's output is the whole point. It gives four layers rather than four alternatives, because each one fails differently and each acts at a different moment.

Now the question that decides most exam items here. Why layer at all, rather than perfecting one control?

Because the failure modes are uncorrelated. Read the four along the bottom of that figure: novel phrasing, well-formed harm, legitimate capability used illegitimately, inattention. No two of those are the same weakness.

An attack, or an ordinary accident, has to clear all four independently. The probability of that is the product of the four failure rates, not the sum.

The analogy

Four guardrail layers are an ensemble. You already know why an ensemble beats its best member, and it is not that the members are individually strong. It is that their errors are uncorrelated, so the cases one gets wrong are not the cases the others get wrong. Defence in depth is the same arithmetic: four modest layers whose failures are independent beat one excellent layer whose failures are all its own.

Where it breaks With an ensemble you can measure the correlation between members, and people usually do. Guardrail independence is normally assumed rather than tested, and it is easy to break without noticing: if input validation and output filtering call the same policy classifier, or if permission scoping and human approval both resolve identity through the same service, the failures are correlated and you are multiplying numbers that are not independent. The multiplication is only as good as the independence, and the independence is a design claim you have to check.
The reflex worth having cold

The exam guide's own sample material states this flatly: least privilege means removing an unneeded capability, not logging its use or asking for confirmation before it runs. That's one of the very few pieces of verified answer logic this project holds directly from the guide, and it generalizes well past tool configuration. A log tells you the boundary was already crossed.

Worked example

An insurance carrier's claims assistant reads a claim, drafts an adjuster note, and originally could issue payment directly.

Look at where the credential sits before and after review.

The rest of the surviving design follows the same discipline. The claim narrative is wrapped as untrusted data rather than instructions, with bank-account and national-ID patterns tokenized before assembly.

Output is schema-validated, and every cited policy clause is checked against the actual document. Recommendations above $5,000, and every recommendation to deny, route to a named adjuster.

But notice what the strongest control actually did. It did not teach the model to use issue_payment responsibly. It deleted the capability.

Why the weaker answers still tempt

A strengthened system prompt is fast, demos well, and produces a measurable improvement in testing. Sit with what that improvement is: a tendency going from 97% to 99.4% is still a tendency.

Logging and alerting presented as a control is genuinely valuable observability that acts after the fact. On any "how do you prevent this" item, an option that detects rather than prevents is wrong-axis.

Requiring confirmation before a dangerous call is a half-move. Better than nothing, worse than removal, and it fails the moment the confirming party is itself an automated pipeline.

And one sophisticated layer will always read as more impressive than four modest ones. Boring checks that fail independently beat one elegant check that fails alone.

Checkpoint 1 · before you read on

A team hardens their system prompt against a known misuse and measures compliance rising from 97% to 99.4% across 40,000 daily requests. They propose closing the risk item.

Compute what they have actually bought, and say which of the four layers they should have reached for.

Answer first, then open
They bought a reduction from 1,200 failures a day to 240.

0.6% of 40,000 is 240. That is a real improvement and it is not a control, because the risk item almost certainly says the behaviour must never happen, and 240 times a day is not never.

Which layer: scoped permissions, if the misuse routes through a capability. Removing it takes the number to zero by construction rather than by tendency. If no capability is involved, the honest answer is that a deterministic input or output check has to carry it, and the prompt change is a quality improvement layered on top rather than the control itself.

The reflex: convert every percentage into a count at the stated volume before accepting it as a control. 99.4% sounds finished. 240 a day does not.

2. Diagnosing what actually broke

By the end of this section you will be able to derive this domain's whole failure catalogue from two facts, and read a symptom's timing to find its cause.

There is a shortcut through this objective, and it is worth having, because it means you do not have to memorise a list.

Two facts about how a model works generate the entire catalogue.

Take root fact 1 slowly, because the consequences are not obvious.

Plausibility correlates with truth. It is not identical to it. When the two diverge, the output is fluent, specific and wrong, and the fluency carries zero information about correctness, because fluency is what the process optimises for.

A stated confidence fails for the same reason. "I'm highly confident" is generated by the exact process that generated the claim it is attached to.

The analogy

A model's stated confidence is another sample from the same distribution. It is not a measurement taken of the answer. It is more text, conditioned on the same context, produced by the same process. Asking a model how sure it is has the same epistemic standing as asking it the question a second time and seeing if it says the same thing, which is to say: some, but not the kind you can route on.

Where it breaks This is a statement about self-reported confidence in generated text, and it does not generalise to probability estimates as such. A calibrated classifier's output, a retrieval relevance score, or a verifier's agreement rate are genuine signals and section 3 routes on exactly those. Over-extend this into "confidence scores are useless" and you will reject the correct answer in a routing question, which is the more expensive mistake of the two.

Retrieval does not fix any of this. It changes what the model conditions on. Feed it a stale chunk and it reports the contents faithfully and confidently.

Which gives you the exam's favourite tell: a RAG system turning confidently wrong right after a document refresh points at retrieval and indexing, not the model. The timing is the evidence.

Root fact 2 produces prompt injection, and the indirect case is the serious one. Hostile text sitting inside a retrieved page, a PDF, a ticket body. Never typed by the user at all.

This is architecture rather than a bug with a patch, which is why the fix is structural: treat retrieved content as untrusted and keep privilege out of the model's reach, rather than filtering phrasings.

Long context adds its own mechanical failure. Material buried mid-document is used less reliably than material at the start or end. The fix is positional and structural, not simply shortening the input, because shortening risks cutting exactly the content that was being missed.

The one that surprises architects most

Per-step accuracy compounds multiplicatively in an agentic chain, and the arithmetic is brutal in a way most people's intuition is not prepared for.

A ten-step agent at 96% per step lands at 0.96¹⁰, which is about 66% end to end. Every single step passes its own unit test.

Take a hospital's clinical documentation pipeline. Retrieve, extract, reconcile, draft, check against formulary, produce a patient-facing version. Six model-touching steps, each measured at 96% after tuning.

End to end that is 0.96⁶, or about 78%. Roughly one document in five is wrong somewhere, and nothing in per-step testing looked broken.

Notice why the obvious fix disappoints. A better model moves each step a point or two, and the product moves less than you would hope, because you are multiplying the improvement rather than adding it.

The fix is to interrupt the compounding, not to raise the steps.

Validate every extracted lab value against the structured source deterministically, right after extraction. Validate against the actual formulary database rather than the model's recollection of it, right after that check.

Now look back at section 1, because this is the same arithmetic pointed the other way.

In a pipeline, each step's success rate multiplies, and the product falls away from you. In a guardrail stack, each layer's failure rate multiplies, and the product falls in your favour.

One mediocre layer barely helps. Four independent layers compound into a very small breach probability, exactly as four independent pipeline steps compound into a large failure probability.

Defence in depth is this widget's maths working for you instead of against you.

Two habits cost points here.

Attributing every failure to the model and reaching for a bigger one requires no diagnosis and is easy to justify to a sponsor. It converts a fixable engineering problem into a permanent cost increase, and does nothing whatsoever for a retrieval fault.

Treating confabulation or injection as something a better prompt fixes reduces the rate without creating the guarantee a genuinely required control needs. Back to the two columns from the intro.

Checkpoint 2 · before you read on

A support agent reads customer-submitted ticket attachments. One day it emails a summary of another customer's account to an address it found inside a PDF. No code was deployed and the system prompt already says never to email anyone not in the ticket record.

Name the mechanism, and say why the existing instruction did not stop it.

Answer first, then open
Indirect prompt injection, from root fact 2.

Hostile text sat inside a retrieved document. The user never typed it, so nothing in the user's input looked suspicious, and no filter on user input would ever have seen it.

Why the instruction failed: it is text in the same channel as the attack. Both are just tokens in the context, competing, and there is no mechanism by which one is privileged over the other. There is no parameterized query for a transformer, which is precisely why the fix has to be structural.

The fix: wrap retrieved content as untrusted data, and remove the capability. If the agent holds no tool that can email an arbitrary address, this entire failure class stops existing rather than becoming less frequent.

3. Spending the review budget

By the end of this section you will route work to humans on the two variables that matter, and be able to name the headcount your routing implies.

Human review is scarce, expensive, and imperfect. Treat it as a budget to allocate, never as a switch to flip.

Two variables decide the allocation.

Consequence is what it costs if the output is wrong, and reversibility matters more than raw magnitude. A draft a person reads before sending is recoverable. A payment issued or a record deleted is not.

Confidence is how likely this specific item is to be wrong, and it has to come from somewhere genuinely independent of the model: an independent verifier, a retrieval score, schema validation, a calibrated classifier, a plain business rule.

The model's own stated confidence never qualifies, because it is text produced by the same process being assessed. That is root fact 1 from the last section, and it is exactly why that analogy warned you not to over-generalise it. These other signals are real. That one is not.

Cross the two variables and you get the routing logic the exam actually wants:

Routing by confidence and consequence
Low consequenceHigh consequence
High confidenceAuto-approve, with continuous samplingHuman approves, always
Low confidenceAuto-approve or queue, by costHuman approves, always

Look at the high-consequence row. Both cells say the same thing, and that is deliberate.

Past a consequence threshold, confidence stops being a routing variable at all, because the human's job there is accountability rather than accuracy. Somebody has to own the decision.

What separates a real review layer from a decorative one

The reviewer sees the source evidence beside the conclusion. A reviewer shown only a fluent output approves it, every time. That is not carelessness, it is the absence of anything to check against.

Every approve, reject and edit is captured as labelled data. Those edits are the most valuable dataset the system will ever produce, and most teams throw them away.

The auto-approved stream is sampled continuously on a fixed schedule. That sample is the calibration signal telling you whether the threshold still holds.

Review capacity is a stated number, not an assumption.

Worked example

A claims carrier runs 12,000 claims a day. Here is the whole policy in one picture.

The Professional-tier move is naming that 25-reviewer cost out loud, and then naming the two levers that shrink it.

Raise the auto-settle threshold, which raises the unsupervised error rate. Or improve the independent verifier, which is engineering work with an uncertain payoff.

Stating the trade with a number attached is the architecture. A routing diagram without it is half a design.

The traps in this objective

"Review everything at launch, then relax as confidence grows" sounds prudent and staged. It is the classic architected trap: no exit criterion, no capacity plan, no defined measurement that would ever justify relaxing it.

Here is a useful reading habit. If a scenario gives you a volume figure at all, an option requiring review of all of it is almost always wrong, and the figure is in the scenario precisely so you can rule it out.

Random sampling as the routing mechanism is statistically respectable and the wrong tool. Uniform sampling meets rare, risky cases exactly in proportion to their rarity. Sampling belongs on the auto-approved stream as monitoring, never on the whole stream as routing.

Routing on the model's self-reported confidence is the purest wrong-axis distractor in the domain, and it is nasty because the wrong option will literally use the word "confidence" and so will the right one. The difference is where the number came from.

4. Compliance as an architecture problem

By the end of this section you will reduce any compliance scenario to three questions, and recognise the single most attractive wrong answer in the domain on sight.

A regulation is an obligation that has to hold regardless of how the system behaves on any given day.

Sit on the word regardless. It can only ever be enforced architecturally, which puts every compliance requirement in the right-hand column from the intro before you have read a word of the specific regulation.

Every compliance scenario reduces to three questions. Where is the boundary between systems you control and systems you do not? What exactly crosses it, field by field? And what proves that a year later, to an auditor?

One pattern answers most of them, and it runs in a fixed order.

Now compare that to an instruction like "do not repeat any patient identifiers."

It is evaluated by the model after the identifiers are already in the request. The transmission the regulation actually cares about has already happened, and whether it happens again depends on the model complying.

The sentence worth having ready

A required control cannot rest on probabilistic behavior. High accuracy, a documented residual risk, and a more capable model are three different-sounding wrong answers that all fail the same way: none of them converts a tendency into a guarantee.

What each regime forces into the design

GDPR forces a lawful purpose stated before collection, with processing confined to it. A prompt built for support triage cannot quietly become a marketing dataset.

It also forces data minimization, and erasure that has to reach every derived store. That means logs, eval datasets and vector indexes, because an embedding is still derived from the person's record. Plus constraints on where processing physically happens.

HIPAA forces a choice: de-identify PHI before it leaves the covered boundary, or bring the processor inside the boundary under the right agreement. Plus "minimum necessary" and audit trails over PHI access.

Keep de-identified and pseudonymized straight. The second retains a re-identification key, and that key has to stay inside the boundary either way.

FedRAMP forces the processing environment itself to be authorized at the right impact level. This one is a procurement fact rather than a configuration setting: the deployment topology you would otherwise pick may simply be unavailable, and no configuration makes an unauthorized environment compliant.

Worked example

A hospital's discharge-summary drafter runs the pattern end to end. The EHR and hospital VPC are in scope. The inference endpoint is outside it.

Name, medical record number and provider identifiers get tokenized. Dates get shifted by a per-patient offset. All of this happens before the clinical narrative is sent, and the token map lives in a store with no network path out.

The draft returns, gets re-associated locally, and a clinician signs before anything enters the chart.

De-identified request and response pairs retain for thirty days under a stated incident-investigation purpose. The token map follows the EHR's own retention policy and never leaves the network. Every crossing writes an immutable record.

Two proposals were rejected. A system-prompt instruction not to repeat identifiers, which acts after transmission. And nightly log redaction, which fixes the record rather than the transmission that already happened.

Four traps, in order of how attractive they are

The prompt-level instruction is the single most attractive wrong answer in this whole domain, precisely because it is usually phrased in the regulation's own words. That is what makes it read as exactly responsive.

A signed processing agreement is a close second. It is genuinely necessary, it allocates liability, and it does nothing to stop data crossing a boundary.

Post-hoc log redaction fixes the record after the transmission already happened.

An enterprise tier or a no-training-on-your-data commitment is the subtlest of the four, because everything said about it is true. It is simply not the boundary control the question asked for.

Checkpoint 4 · before you read on

A customer exercises their right to erasure under GDPR. Your team deletes their records from the primary database and confirms completion.

Name at least three places the data almost certainly still exists, and say which one people forget most reliably.

Answer first, then open
Erasure has to reach every derived store, not the source of truth alone.

Logs and traces. Prompts and retrieved context were written there at request time, under retention policies set for debugging.

Evaluation datasets. Real production traces are the best source of eval cases, which is exactly why the person's data is likely to be in one.

The vector index. This is the one people forget, and the reasoning is worth having explicitly: an embedding is derived from the person's record, so deleting the row and leaving the vector has not completed the erasure.

Also worth checking: backups, and any fine-tuning set. The general shape is that erasure is a property of the whole system, and section 4's third question, what proves this to an auditor a year later, is asking whether you can show all of it.

5. Fairness as a system property

By the end of this section you will know why the two most popular fairness fixes are the two that destroy your ability to measure fairness at all.

Models train on data that encodes the distribution of past decisions. Where those decisions were skewed, the skew reappears.

And it reappears in fluent, confident prose, which makes it considerably harder to notice than a skewed coefficient would have been.

Fairness belongs to the whole system rather than the model alone. The model, the eval data, the threshold, and the population it is applied to all contribute. A model can sit completely unchanged while a system's fairness properties shift, because the applicant pool moved.

An aggregate accuracy figure is a weighted average, and a weighted average hides exactly the disparity you are looking for.

So the eval set needs adequate sample size per group rather than population-proportional sampling. If that reasoning feels familiar, it is Domain 4's stratification argument arriving in a different domain with different stakes.

Why removing the protected attribute does not work

It is the first thing everyone proposes. It feels principled, and it is often legally encouraged as a floor.

It also does not remove the signal. Postal code carries it. School name carries it. Employment gaps carry it. Phrasing register carries it. A capable model recovers the association without being told, because recovering associations from correlated features is precisely what capable models do.

Blindness is not fairness, and it destroys your ability to detect the disparity you were trying to avoid. You have deleted the column you would have measured with.

The definitions genuinely conflict

This is not a gap in the field waiting to be closed by better research.

When base rates differ, equal selection rates, equal false-negative rates and equal calibration cannot all hold at once. That is arithmetic, not a shortage of effort.

So the design has to declare which one it holds itself to, stated in terms of which harm it is controlling, as a written stakeholder decision rather than a default setting.

Two different things called "transparency"

The word covers two obligations that are satisfied by completely different artifacts, and the exam separates them.

Disclosure is the affected person knowing AI was involved, and how to reach a human.

Explainability is being able to account for a specific decision after the fact.

Explainability is served by a reconstructable trace: inputs, retrieved evidence, model and prompt versions, score, threshold, and the rule that fired.

It is not served by asking the model to explain itself. A generated rationale is plausible text about the decision, not a record of what produced it. That is root fact 1 again, wearing yet another costume.

Worked example

A recruiting screener shows the whole shape.

The held-out set is stratified with equal N per demographic category rather than proportional sampling, and deliberately includes near-pairs: candidates identical in substance but differing in a proxy signal such as school name.

The declared metric is false-negative-rate parity, chosen because the harm being controlled is a qualified candidate never reaching a human. It is written down, because somebody will ask why this metric in twelve months.

The threshold is set against the worst-performing group's error rate rather than the mean. That costs throughput, on purpose.

Every decision persists the fields used, the rubric version, the model version, the score, and the human decision that followed. That trace is the explainability artifact, and it is distinct from disclosure, which is telling candidates AI is used and giving them a route to request human review.

Subgroup metrics get recomputed monthly against live traffic, because a fairness result is a measurement rather than a fixed property.

Four wrong answers

Removing the protected attribute fails for the reason above, and it is the most attractive of the four because it feels principled.

A model-generated explanation attached to each decision reads beautifully in a demo and is generated text, not evidence.

A single aggregate fairness score as a release gate is better than nothing, and it obscures which definition is being enforced and which group absorbs the error.

A fairness statement in the system prompt is the same category mistake as every other prompt-level control here: a tendency, standing in for what the question required, which was a measurement.

Checkpoint 5 · before you read on

A team removes every protected attribute from their screener's inputs and adds a line to the system prompt instructing the model to evaluate all candidates equally. They report the system is now fair.

Say what each change actually did, and what they can no longer do.

Answer first, then open
One removed the measurement. The other added a tendency.

Removing the attributes did not remove the signal, which still arrives through postal code, school name, employment gaps and phrasing. It did remove the label they would have needed to compute subgroup metrics.

The prompt line is a tendency where the requirement is a measurement, which is the intro's left column standing in for the right one.

What they can no longer do: detect the disparity at all. They are in the worst available position, which is having a real disparity and no instrument, while reporting an aggregate that will look fine.

Notice both changes are things a careful person proposes in good faith. That is what makes this objective hard rather than obvious.

6. One regulated system, all five objectives

By the end of this section you will have seen all five objectives decided on one system, and you will have the single question that catches nearly every wrong answer in this domain.

A European bank deploys an assistant that drafts hardship-plan offers for customers in arrears. It reads account history and correspondence, proposes a restructuring offer, and drafts the letter.

All five objectives have a claim on it at once, and it is worth naming why before looking at the design.

EU personal data is in scope. The offers are consequential decisions reaching individuals. The assistant touches a system that can amend an account. Volume rules out reviewing everything. And the correspondence it reads is third-party text, which makes it untrusted input.

Compliance first. The payload is arrears history, account terms and stated circumstances, not the full customer record. Name, account number and national ID are tokenized before the call and re-associated locally. Processing runs against an EU-region endpoint.

Logs from this purpose are walled off from the marketing pipeline as an access control, not as a policy statement. That distinction is the whole of section 4 in one clause.

Then privilege. The assistant holds no tool that can amend an account. It emits a structured offer object, and a separate service validates it against term limits and eligibility, holding the only credential that matters.

Correspondence is wrapped as untrusted data, so the eligibility decision never depends on instructions hidden inside it.

Then the failure modes, each by its own mechanism. Confabulated policy terms are caught by validating every cited term against the actual policy database. Injection is neutralized by the plain absence of privilege. Version drift is handled by pinning the model and running a 200-case regression set before any change. And a deterministic validation step in the middle interrupts compounding across the model-touching steps.

Then the review budget. Every declined offer, and every offer above a defined concession value, goes to a case handler regardless of confidence, because those are the outcomes a customer feels and a regulator asks about.

Offers inside standard bands where the model and validator agree auto-issue, with 2% audited weekly. The reviewer's screen shows arrears history, the specific policy clause, and the proposed terms side by side.

Then fairness. Decline-rate parity is measured monthly across the bank's accountable subgroups on a stratified set, chosen because wrongful decline is the harm being controlled. Every decision carries a full trace.

The trades are where the Professional tier lives

None of these has a context-free correct answer, and that is the point of naming them.

Tightening the human-review band buys accountability and costs case-handler headcount. That number belongs in the business case.

Aggressive minimization improves the compliance posture and can strip context that mattered to offer quality.

Longer log retention helps fairness auditing and enlarges the erasure surface.

Pinning the model version protects against drift and defers capability gains.

The architect's job is surfacing each trade with a number attached, not resolving it silently.

The five sentences a sponsor needs

The person signing this off is not an engineer, and being able to say the design without jargon is itself a tested competence.

The assistant cannot change an account; it proposes, and the bank's existing rules engine decides. Customer identifiers never leave the bank's systems. Every decline is seen by a person. Outcomes are checked across customer groups every month. The reasoning behind any individual offer can be shown.

Five sentences, no jargon, every one of them tracing back to a mechanism in the sections above. This domain shares that competence with its neighbour on stakeholder communication.

Checkpoint 6 · before you read on

Across all five objectives, here are five options from five different questions: strengthen the system prompt, document the residual risk, upgrade to a more capable model, route on the model's confidence score, report aggregate accuracy.

They are wrong in five different-looking ways. State the one thing they have in common.

Answer first, then open
Every one is a tendency standing in for a guarantee.

A prompt instruction is a tendency. A documented risk is an acknowledgement, which changes nothing about behaviour. A better model shifts a rate. A self-reported confidence is a sample from the same distribution as the claim. An aggregate is a weighted average that can conceal the failing case entirely.

Not one of them holds regardless of what the model happens to generate today, which is the definition of a guarantee from the intro.

The question to ask before picking any answer in this domain: does this control hold regardless of what the model generates? If the honest answer is "usually", it belongs in the left column and the question was asking about the right one.

Common misconceptions

The mythLeast privilege means logging or confirming when a risky tool is used.
Why it's temptingLogging and confirmation are real, checkable practices, and they show up in every security checklist. Keeping the capability and watching it feels responsible.
The realityLeast privilege means removing the capability the system doesn't need. A log tells you the boundary was already crossed. It does not prevent the crossing.
The one testDoes the scenario say the capability isn't needed? If yes, the answer removes it. Logging or confirming it is the designated wrong answer. (The same reflex Domain 3's authorization objective tests.)
The mythWe're at 99.2% accuracy, so we meet the compliance requirement.
Why it's tempting99.2% sounds like a finished, defensible number, and it's the kind of figure a sponsor wants to hear.
The realityA percentage is a tendency. A required control needs a mechanism that cannot fail probabilistically: code, permissions, or a person, not a score.
The one testDoes the requirement use a word like "always," "never," or "must"? If so, no accuracy figure, however high, satisfies it on its own.
The myth91% accuracy overall means the system works for everyone.
Why it's temptingAn aggregate figure sounds complete and is easy to report in one line to a stakeholder.
The realityAn aggregate is a weighted average that hides subgroup failure. 94% for the majority group can sit beside 78% for a minority one. Measure by subgroup, with adequate sample size per group.
The one testHas the number been broken out by the groups the organization is accountable for? If not, the 91% hasn't earned its confidence yet. (The same masking pattern Domain 4's evaluation objective tests.)
